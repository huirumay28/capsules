Before

Before 1982: numbered generations and a lost decade

Chinese critics like to divide the country's directors into numbered generations. The first generation made silent films in Shanghai in the 1910s and 1920s. The second generation made the great Shanghai films of the 1930s and 1940s, such as (1937) and 's (1948). The third generation worked after 1949 under the new People's Republic, when filmmakers were sent to Moscow to study and the was founded, in 1950. The trained in the early 1960s, just before everything stopped.

Everything stopped in 1966. During the almost all film production was halted except for a small number of approved model works, and the took no new students. The young people who would later become the spent those years as in villages, as soldiers or as factory workers. was sent to a farm in Xishuangbanna in the far south-west and then joined the army. worked in a textile mill. Their own families were often targets: 's father had been condemned in the 1950s as part of the counterrevolutionary clique and spent years in prison and labour camps.

When the ended in 1976, older directors began to make what were called , films about suffering in the years just past, such as 's (1980). These films were emotional and conventional in style. The generation that came next would take a very different route.

The idea

What made the films different

Chinese films after 1949 had been built around dialogue and a clear political message: good characters and bad characters, and a happy ending in which the Party solved problems. The broke with that in several ways.

  • Images instead of words. The critic wrote that was the first Chinese film since 1949 to tell its story through images rather than dialogue. Long, still shots of the land, the sky and faces carry the meaning.
  • Rural China and the distant past. Rather than the present-day city, most early films are set in villages, deserts, Tibet or Inner Mongolia, often in the 1920s to 1940s. Many draw on the of the 1980s, such as , who looked for the deep culture of the countryside.
  • Ambiguity about politics. shows a soldier of the Communist who collects folk songs but cannot save the village girl who trusts him. Critics noted that the film was unsure whether the Party could really help the peasants, which was very different from earlier propaganda films.
  • Colour as storytelling. Especially in 's films, red and other strong colours carry emotion: the fields, the dyed cloth in , the red lanterns of the Chen household.
  • Women at the centre. Many of the best-known films are about a woman trapped by family, marriage or custom, often played by .

Because these films looked so new, they attracted controversy at home, and many audiences found them slow. , for example, was well received by Western arthouse audiences but did poorly at the Chinese box office.

Deep dive

Yellow Earth: a song that does not save anyone

was made in 1984 by the with a young crew: directed and photographed it. It is set in 1939 on the of northern Shaanxi, near the Yellow River. A soldier from the , Gu Qing, comes to a poor village to collect folk songs, which the Communists plan to rewrite with revolutionary words. He stays with a widowed farmer, his silent young son and his teenage daughter, Cuiqiao, who has been promised in an arranged marriage.

Gu Qing tells Cuiqiao that in the Communist areas women can choose whom they marry. She asks him to take her with him. He says he must follow the rules and will return for her. He does not come back in time. After the wedding Cuiqiao tries to cross the Yellow River at night, singing, and her song stops in the middle of the river. When Gu Qing finally returns, the villagers are praying for rain.

The film is famous for how it looks. often filled most of the frame with earth and placed people at the very top or bottom, so that the land seems to press down on them. There is little dialogue, and much of the story is carried by folk songs. In 2005 the Hong Kong Film Awards placed fourth on a list of the 100 best Chinese-language films.

For audiences in 1984, the shock was its message as much as its style. A Party soldier is kind but useless, the peasants' own customs are what destroy the girl, and the film does not say that the revolution will fix this. That doubt, told through pictures instead of speeches, became the mark of the whole generation.

Foreign money and foreign festivals mattered because the domestic situation had become harder. After the crackdown on the in June 1989, censorship tightened, and several films that won abroad were banned or cut at home. was banned until July 1992. , a between mainland China and Hong Kong, premiered in Hong Kong on 1 January 1993; when it was shown in China, officials objected to its portrayal of homosexuality, a suicide and the 1960s turmoil, and it was re-released in September 1993 with 14 minutes removed. was denied a theatrical release, though it was still shown in some cinemas.

Critics, inside and outside China, argued about this success. Some said that films about foot massages, concubines and dyed cloth showed the West an exotic, backward China that foreign festival audiences wanted to see, and that the directors were making films for juries abroad. Others answered that the films used the past to criticise the present, in a way that would have been impossible in a story set in 1990.

Think about it

A question to take with you

Many films won prizes abroad by showing China's past as harsh and exotic, and critics asked whether they were made for foreign eyes. Think about how Taiwan appears in films or stories made for audiences overseas. Is there a difference between showing a society's hard history honestly and packaging it for outsiders? How would you tell which one a film is doing?
