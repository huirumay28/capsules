The concept

What a moral panic is

A is a period in which a society becomes intensely and suddenly alarmed about a person, a group, a behaviour or a new invention that it sees as a threat to its values. The alarm is usually spread by the news media, taken up by politicians, police, church leaders and campaigners, and often ends in new laws or harsher punishments. When the panic passes, the threat often turns out to have been much smaller than people believed, or never to have existed in the form described.

The British sociologist gave the idea its classic definition in his 1972 book . He wrote that societies appear to be subject, every now and then, to periods of , in which "a condition, episode, person or group of persons emerges to become defined as a threat to societal values and interests." The important word is defined. was not mainly interested in what young people at the seaside actually did. He was interested in how other people came to describe, explain and react to what they did.

called the people blamed in a panic . A is a visible, easily recognised type of person who comes to stand for everything a society fears is going wrong: the delinquent teenager, the drug user, the immigrant criminal, the corrupter of children. Once a group is cast in that role, almost anything its members do can be read as more evidence of the danger.

A does not require that the problem be invented from nothing. There were real fights at the English seaside in 1964, real street robberies in the 1970s and real cases of child abuse in the 1980s. The panic lies in the gap between the event and the reaction: the problem is exaggerated, the rare case is presented as typical, and the response grows far beyond what the evidence supports. summed this up by saying that in a the untypical is made typical.

Case study

Mods and rockers at Clacton, 1964

In early 1960s Britain, two youth styles had become easy to tell apart. wore sharp Italian-style suits and long green parka coats, rode Vespa and Lambretta scooters, and listened to modern jazz, soul, Motown, ska and British rhythm and blues bands such as and . wore black leather jackets and boots, rode heavy motorcycles, and preferred 1950s rock and roll by , and British rock and roll singers such as Billy Fury. Their look owed a great deal to in the 1953 film .

Over the Easter weekend of 1964, groups of young people went to the small Essex resort of . The weather was cold and wet, there was little to do, and some teenagers scuffled, broke windows and annoyed residents and shopkeepers. , who was then a young social worker and graduate student, later concluded from police records, court reports and interviews that the trouble was real but limited, and that the division between and was far less clear on the ground than it became in print.

The next morning's national newspapers presented the weekend as an invasion. They used words such as terror, riot and battle, described the young people as vermin and louts, and treated the two styles as rival armies. Editorials asked what was wrong with the nation's youth. Over the bank holiday in May 1964, more clashes followed at Brighton and Margate, and in August 1964 at Hastings, watched by reporters and photographers who had arrived expecting trouble. Magistrates handed out heavy fines and short prison sentences, and one described the defendants as "sawdust Caesars". Members of Parliament visited the resorts and called for tougher measures against hooliganism.

's achievement was to separate the events from what he called the , the first wave of reporting that fixed the story in the public mind. He found three habits in that reporting. The first was exaggeration and distortion: numbers, violence and damage were inflated, and ordinary incidents were described in the language of war. The second was prediction: papers warned that worse was coming at the next holiday, which helped bring crowds and police to the next resort. The third was : the scooter, the parka and the haircut became symbols that stood for delinquency, so that any young person dressed that way looked like a threat.

The reaction then fed on itself. Police forces sent extra officers to the coast and made more arrests, which produced more stories. Shops sold more parkas and scooter accessories, and some teenagers went to the seaside precisely because the newspapers said that was where the action was. In this sense the panic helped to create the very and it described. By the late 1960s the fashion had moved on and the story faded, as predicted panics usually do.

The mechanism

How a panic unfolds

described the life of a panic as a sequence. Something happens, or is said to have happened, and is reported. The media coverage turns it into a symbol of a broader decline. , such as editors, bishops, politicians and campaigners, take up the cause and offer explanations and remedies. Experts are called upon to explain why it is happening. The authorities respond, often with new policing, new laws or harsher sentences. Then the panic either fades away or leaves a lasting mark in law and in public memory.

He identified four groups that keep a panic going: the mass media, , the control culture of police, courts and politicians, and the public. None of them needs to be lying. Journalists may simply be covering what seems like a big story, police may simply be preparing for the trouble they have been told to expect, and the public may simply believe what it reads. The panic is produced by the way their actions reinforce one another.

In 1994 the American sociologist and the Israeli sociologist tried to make the idea more precise. They proposed five features that together mark a . There must be heightened concern about the behaviour of a group; hostility towards that group as an enemy of decent society; a degree of consensus that the threat is real and serious; , meaning the reaction is far larger than the actual harm; and volatility, meaning the panic flares up quickly and dies down quickly.

  • Concern: people are worried and talk about the issue a great deal.
  • Hostility: a clearly identified group is treated as the enemy.
  • Consensus: a broad section of society agrees the threat is real.
  • : the reaction is much larger than the harm.
  • Volatility: the alarm erupts suddenly and fades fairly fast.

Mugging, comics and satanic day-care

In (1978), and his colleagues at the applied 's idea to a British panic about in 1972 and 1973. The word had arrived from American news reports and was soon attached to young Black men in British cities. A long prison sentence passed in 1973 on a sixteen-year-old from Handsworth in Birmingham for a street robbery became a symbol of the fight against the new crime. 's group argued that the panic did more than reflect fear of crime. It helped a government facing economic and political trouble to build support for tougher policing, and it tied crime to race in the public imagination.

The theory also helped scholars look back at earlier episodes. In the United States in the early 1950s, the psychiatrist 's (1954) blamed horror and crime comics for juvenile delinquency. A Senate subcommittee held hearings, and the industry responded by creating the , which for decades restricted what American comic books could show. The pattern of a new medium, an expert warning, political hearings and self-censorship would later repeat itself with rock lyrics, video games and social media.

The most damaging panic of the late twentieth century was the American and British fear of in the 1980s and early 1990s. In the in California, members of the family that ran the school were charged with hundreds of acts of abuse, including bizarre rituals, after interviewers used leading questions with very young children. The prosecution ran from 1984 to 1990, became one of the longest and most expensive criminal trials in American history, and ended without a single conviction. Later investigations found no evidence that organised satanic abuse rings of the kind described had existed.

In 1995 and argued that the classic model needed updating. In the 1960s a few national newspapers and broadcasters could speak with one voice. By the 1990s there were many competing outlets, and the groups being accused could answer back through their own magazines, music and spokespeople. Some youth cultures and record labels even learned to court outrage, because a ban or a tabloid attack could sell records. himself accepted much of this in later editions of his book, while maintaining that the basic pattern of exaggeration and disproportionate reaction had not gone away.

Elsewhere

A comparison with Japan's otaku panic

A clear example outside the English-speaking world came in Japan in 1989, when was arrested for the murders of four young girls. Reports that his room was filled with thousands of videotapes, including horror films and anime, led much of the press to present him as a typical , a word for obsessive fans of anime, manga and similar media. For several years the word carried a strong suggestion of social isolation and danger, and fans of these hobbies faced suspicion from parents, teachers and employers.

The case fits 's pattern closely. A single, horrifying crime was treated as a sign of what a whole subculture was becoming, a new style of leisure became the symbol of the threat, and calls for regulation of manga and video followed. As with the , the label later lost much of its , and by the 2000s culture was being promoted by the Japanese government as part of the country's international appeal.

Why it matters

Using the idea carefully

The idea of is a powerful tool, but it can be misused. Calling a concern a suggests that it is irrational, so people sometimes use the phrase to dismiss worries that are in fact well founded. warned against this. In the introduction to the 2002 edition of his book he pointed out that some reactions that look like panics are in fact proportionate, and that the real question is always an empirical one: how large is the harm, and how large is the reaction?

For anyone running a public campaign, including a charity that wants to raise awareness of violence against women, the theory offers two lessons. First, vivid single cases move audiences, but presenting the rare case as typical can produce fear and bad policy rather than real help. Second, campaigns are most trustworthy when they give accurate numbers, name their sources and avoid turning a whole group of people into . spent his later career writing about human rights, and his 2001 book examined the opposite problem: how societies and individuals manage not to notice suffering that is real.
