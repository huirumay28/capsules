The , 1346–1353: the and the world it left behind

In about seven years a single disease killed somewhere between a third and a half of the people of Europe, the Middle East and North Africa. This lesson follows the plague from Central Asia to the Atlantic and asks how the survivors rebuilt work, faith and power.

Nobody counted the dead of the . Medieval chroniclers often gave round or exaggerated numbers, and modern estimates are built from tax records, manorial rolls, clergy lists and archaeology, so death rates in this lesson are given as ranges. Most of the detailed evidence comes from Europe and the Islamic Mediterranean; whether the plague also struck China and India in the 1340s is still uncertain, and the lesson says so rather than guessing. Some famous stories, such as the Mongols catapulting corpses into , come from writers who were not present and are flagged as such.

Before

A crowded, hungry world

Around the year 1300 Europe was fuller than it had ever been. The population had perhaps doubled or more since 1000, forests had been cleared for farms, and new towns had been founded across the continent. Cities such as Florence, Venice, Paris and Ghent held tens of thousands of people. Most Europeans, however, were peasants who worked small plots of land and owed rent, labour or both to a lord. Many families lived close to the edge of survival.

That margin was tested early in the century. From 1315 to about 1322 cold, wet weather ruined harvests across northern Europe in what historians call the , and a cattle disease followed. Millions died. At the same time the long war between England and France, later called the , began in 1337, and the English victory at Crécy in 1346 came in the same year the plague first appeared on the edge of Europe.

The thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries were also an age of connection. The Mongol conquests had created a chain of empires across Asia, and merchants from Genoa and Venice traded at ports on the Black Sea that linked the Mediterranean to the overland routes to Central Asia, Persia and China. Goods, soldiers, pilgrims and grain moved along these routes, and so did the rodents and fleas that carry plague.

The disease

What the plague was and how it spread

The was plague, a disease caused by the bacterium . This was argued for a century but is now settled: in 2011 a team led by and published a reconstructed genome of the bacterium taken from skeletons in the East Smithfield plague cemetery in London, which was dug in 1348–1349. Plague normally lives among wild rodents such as marmots and gerbils, and passes between them through fleas. When it spills into rats living near people, or into people directly, it can cause an epidemic.

Plague takes several forms. In the bacteria travel to the lymph nodes, which swell into painful lumps called in the groin, armpit or neck; without modern antibiotics a large share of victims die within about a week. In the lungs are infected, and the disease can pass from person to person through coughing; it is almost always fatal. , an infection of the blood, kills very quickly. The papal physician , who caught the disease in Avignon and survived, described two forms in 1348: one with continuous fever and spitting of blood that killed in about three days, and one with swellings that killed in about five.

Where the plague began is still being studied. In 2022 a team led by and published DNA from people buried in 1338 and 1339 in two cemeteries near in today's Kyrgyzstan, whose gravestones mention 'pestilence'. Their bacteria sit at the root of the strains that later spread across Europe, which suggests the 's ancestor came from this region of the Tian Shan mountains.

How it moved so fast is also debated. The traditional explanation is black rats and their fleas travelling on ships and in grain stores. Some researchers have argued that human fleas and body lice also passed the disease directly between people, which would help explain how it spread through cities so quickly. Most historians now think several routes worked at once, and that the plague moved along the same roads, rivers and sea lanes that carried trade.

1346–1353

From Caffa to the Atlantic

In 1346 the army of the , the Mongol state that ruled the steppe north of the Black Sea, was besieging the Genoese port of (today in Crimea) when plague broke out among the soldiers. The Italian notary later wrote that the Mongols catapulted dead bodies over the walls to infect the town. De' Mussi was in Piacenza, not , and historians treat his story with caution. Whatever happened at the walls, Italian ships leaving the Black Sea carried plague with them.

By the spring of 1347 the plague was in Constantinople. In October Genoese galleys brought it to Messina in Sicily, and over the winter it reached Alexandria, Genoa, Pisa, Venice and Marseille. In 1348 it swept through Italy, southern France, Spain and the Islamic Mediterranean, and moved north to Paris, the Low Countries and England, where it landed at the Dorset port of Melcombe in June. In 1349 it crossed to Ireland, Scotland and Norway, where a ship from England is said to have brought it to Bergen. By 1351 it had reached the Russian principalities, and it was still killing in Moscow in 1353.

The death toll was enormous but uneven. Most historians now put European mortality between about 30 and 50 percent, and the Norwegian historian has argued for around 60 percent. Florence, Siena, Venice and Paris probably lost about half their people. Some places, such as parts of Poland and Milan, were long thought to have escaped lightly, though newer research questions how lightly. Villages disappeared, and some were never resettled. In England, records of the clergy show that many parishes needed new priests two or three times in a single year.

Rich and poor both died. Joan, the young daughter of of England, died of plague near Bordeaux in 1348 on her way to marry the heir to the throne of Castile. Her intended father-in-law, , died of plague in 1350 while besieging Gibraltar, the only reigning European king to die of it in the first outbreak. The painter of Siena is believed to have died in 1348, and so did , the woman to whom the poet addressed his love poems.

Living through it

Doctors, flagellants and the search for blame

People in 1348 had no idea of bacteria. In October 1348 the medical faculty of the University of Paris, asked by King Philip VI for an explanation, reported that a conjunction of Saturn, Jupiter and Mars in 1345 had corrupted the air, and that the , or , then caused disease in bodies already out of balance. Doctors advised fleeing, burning aromatic wood, avoiding baths and strong emotions, and eating a moderate diet. Clement VI's doctors kept him between two great fires in his palace in Avignon. Many cities closed their gates, burned the belongings of the dead, and appointed health officials, and several Italian cities began to create permanent health boards.

Many Christians understood the plague as God's punishment for sin. In 1348 and 1349 groups of marched from town to town in the German lands and the Low Countries, whipping themselves in public to win God's mercy. Their processions often stirred hostility against the clergy and against Jews, and Clement VI condemned the movement in October 1349.

The worst violence fell on Jewish communities. Rumours spread that Jews were poisoning wells. In 1348 Jews in Savoy were tortured into confessions, which were circulated to other cities. Clement VI issued bulls in July and September 1348 forbidding attacks and pointing out that Jews were dying of plague like everyone else and that the plague was raging where there were no Jews at all. In many places this made no difference. In Basel in January 1349 Jews were burned on an island in the Rhine. In Strasbourg the city council tried to protect them and was overthrown; on 14 February 1349 the Jewish community was burned in its cemetery, and chroniclers gave the number of dead as around two thousand. Communities in Mainz, Cologne, Erfurt and scores of other towns were destroyed in 1349, often before the plague had even arrived. Many survivors moved east, especially into the kingdom of Poland.

In the Islamic world the response was different in tone. Muslim scholars generally taught that plague was a mercy and a martyrdom for believers sent by God, and that the faithful should neither flee nor fear it. There were no pogroms against minorities on the European scale. Communal prayers and processions were held, and in Damascus in 1348 Muslims, Christians and Jews are reported to have prayed together. The Granadan scholar argued from observation that the disease was contagious, a view that was unusual among theologians of the time.

After

Fewer people, higher wages, angrier lords

The plague did not leave after 1353. It returned in 1361, in an outbreak that chroniclers said fell especially on children and young people born since the first wave, and again in 1369, 1375 and roughly every decade afterwards. Because of these repeated outbreaks the population of Europe kept falling or stayed low for more than a century; England did not regain its pre-plague population until the sixteenth century. Plague remained a recurring threat in Europe until the eighteenth century, with the last major Western European outbreak in Marseille in 1720.

With so many people dead, land was plentiful and workers were scarce. Labourers and tenants could demand higher wages or lower rents, or simply move to a lord who offered better terms. Lords and governments tried to stop this. In England the of 1349 and the of 1351 ordered workers to accept the wages paid before the plague and punished those who left their employers. Similar laws were passed in France, Castile, Aragon and Italian cities. They slowed the change but could not stop it, and in England real wages for ordinary workers rose substantially over the following century.

The tension between workers and lords helped produce a wave of revolts. The of 1358 in northern France, the wool workers in Florence in 1378 and the English of 1381 all had different immediate causes, such as war taxes or exclusion from city government. But all of them took place in societies where ordinary people expected more than the old order wanted to give. The English rising was crushed, yet over the next century faded away in England. In much of Eastern Europe the opposite happened in later centuries, as lords tied peasants more tightly to the land.

The plague also changed culture and institutions. The Church lost many of its most dedicated priests, who died caring for the sick, and had to ordain younger and less trained replacements. Bishop of Norwich founded at Cambridge in 1350 partly to train new clergy, and Corpus Christi College followed in 1352. Images of death became common in art: the , in which skeletons lead people of every rank away, and tombs that show the dead person as a rotting corpse. Italian cities developed permanent health boards, and Ragusa's thirty-day isolation rule of 1377, later extended to forty days, gave us the word , from the Italian quaranta giorni.

Women and the plague

A golden age for women's work?

Because workers were scarce, some historians have argued that the generations after the plague were a relatively good time for women in parts of northwestern Europe. In England, records from towns such as York show women working as servants, brewers, spinners and in other trades, and the historian argued that young women had more chance to earn their own money and to marry later and by choice.

Other historians, notably in her study of English brewing, (1996), have pushed back. Bennett showed that women dominated small-scale brewing in the fourteenth century but were pushed out as the trade became larger, more profitable and more regulated in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, and she argued that women's work remained low-status and low-paid throughout. Economists have since linked the plague to the 'European marriage pattern', in which women married in their mid-twenties rather than their teens, though how strong that link is remains debated. The question is a useful example of how a disaster can open opportunities without changing the underlying rules about who holds power.

Women's voices also survive from these years. was a small child when plague reached England and lived through several later outbreaks. Her book of visions, written in English in the late fourteenth century, returns again and again to suffering and to God's love. A generation later, the writer , born in Venice in 1364 and raised at the French royal court, became one of the first women in Europe to earn her living by writing, and her Book of the City of Ladies (1405) argued against the low opinion of women common in her time.

A story

The shoemaker of Siena

In 1348 Siena was one of the great cities of Tuscany. It had begun to build an enormous new cathedral, planned to make the existing one merely the transept of a far larger church. Then the plague arrived in the spring.

The shoemaker and tax official kept a chronicle of the city. He wrote that the dead were thrown into great ditches and covered with a little earth, and that some were so lightly buried that dogs dragged them out. He wrote that fathers abandoned children and husbands abandoned wives, because the illness seemed to pass through breath and sight. And he wrote: 'I, , called the Fat, buried my five children with my own hands.' He added that so many died that everyone believed it was the end of the world.

Siena never recovered its earlier size or power. Work on the giant new cathedral stopped, and it was never finished. The unfinished walls and the huge empty arch of its facade still stand beside the cathedral today, and visitors can climb them. They are one of the most visible marks the left on any European city.

Elsewhere

The 1340s around the world

  • The Middle East and North Africa: The plague struck Egypt and Syria in 1347–1349 as hard as it struck Europe. Cairo, then the largest city west of China, lost a very large share of its people, and the irrigation system of the Nile valley suffered because there were too few villagers to maintain it. The survived, but historians see the plague as a turning point in its long decline.
  • China: The suffered floods, famine and epidemics in the 1330s–1350s, and the Yellow River broke its banks in 1344. Whether any of these epidemics was plague is uncertain, because Chinese records do not describe the symptoms clearly. The began in 1351, and in 1368 founded the .
  • Japan: The country was split between rival after 1336 (the ). There is no clear evidence that the reached Japan, which may have been protected by its distance from the main overland and maritime routes.
  • Taiwan: In the fourteenth century Taiwan was home to Austronesian Indigenous peoples and was not part of any state. The Yuan government did set up an office in the Penghu islands, and the traveller described a land called Liuqiu, which may have been Taiwan, in his Daoyi zhilüe of 1349.
  • West Africa: The was at its height after the famous pilgrimage of to Mecca in 1324–1325. Whether plague crossed the Sahara in the 1340s is debated; some archaeologists and historians, including , have argued that it did.
  • Western Europe at war: The continued through the plague. The English won at Crécy in 1346 and Poitiers in 1356, where they captured the French king John II, and the French ransom and war taxes fed the anger behind the .

Analysis

Did the plague make the modern world?

Some historians see the as the event that ended the Middle Ages. In this view, the labour shortage broke in Western Europe, raised wages, encouraged labour-saving technology, weakened the authority of the Church and set the stage for the Renaissance. The historian , in (2017), lists mass-mortality pandemics as one of four forces, along with war, revolution and state collapse, that have reduced economic inequality in history, and uses the as a central example. 's work on Italian tax records also shows wealth becoming less concentrated after 1348, then becoming more concentrated again from the fifteenth century.

Other historians warn against making the plague explain everything. Many of the changes it is credited with, such as the decline of in England, were already under way before 1348, and in Eastern Europe the same shock was followed by stronger , not weaker. What the plague did depended on what each society already looked like: who controlled land, how strong the state was and whether workers could organise. Recently and others have also argued that the should be studied as a global event across Eurasia and Africa, not mainly as a European one, and that its genetic history reaches back to an earlier spread of plague lineages in the thirteenth century.

The plague also shows how people respond to disasters they cannot explain. Medieval authorities did some things that worked, such as isolation and health boards, and some that did enormous harm, such as blaming Jews. Both responses came from the same fear, and both left long-lasting institutions.

One question

Who wins after a disaster?

After the , workers in England gained higher wages despite laws designed to stop them, while peasants in much of Eastern Europe eventually lost freedom. Thinking about land, laws and who could organise, what do you think decided whether a great disaster helped or hurt ordinary people? Can you think of a modern crisis where the same question applies?

Fun fact

Nobody at the time called it the Black Death

People who lived through it called it 'the ' or simply 'the pestilence'. The name became common in English only centuries later, probably from a Latin phrase, , in which atra can mean both 'black' and 'terrible'. It has nothing to do with the colour of the victims' skin, despite a popular belief.
