In 1970 , the founder of the , published a two-page essay arguing that a company should be managed as a portfolio of businesses with different cash needs. Some units throw off cash, some swallow it, and the job of the head office is to move money from the first group to the most promising members of the second. The four-box picture that came out of this idea, with its , , and (later 'dogs'), became one of the most widely used tools in corporate strategy. This lesson explains the framework first, then follows how , and used portfolio thinking, and ends with the evidence that the matrix can also lead managers astray.

Before

Conglomerates, cash and the experience curve

In the 1950s and 1960s, many large American companies became . A single corporation might own businesses in appliances, jet engines, chemicals, insurance and food. Each division usually kept the cash it earned and asked the head office for more when it wanted to expand. Division managers naturally argued that their own business deserved investment, and the head office, which knew far less about each market than the people running it, had no common yardstick for saying no. The result, as later writers at put it, was that mature divisions often kept reinvesting their own cash at steadily falling returns, while promising new businesses were starved.

, a former Westinghouse vice president who had worked at Arthur D. Little, founded what became the in 1963. He decided early that the firm would specialise in 'business strategy', a phrase that at the time had no settled meaning. 's first big idea was the . Building on the that had measured in aircraft factories in 1936, studied cost data across many industries and found that each time a product's cumulative volume doubled, its total unit cost tended to fall by a roughly constant percentage, often somewhere between 10 and 25 per cent. From 1968 stressed the strategic meaning of this finding: the company that has produced the most will usually have the lowest costs, and the easiest way to produce the most is to hold the largest market share.

If market share drives cost, and cost drives margins and cash, then a company with many businesses can ask a simple question about each one: does it have a leading share, and is its market still growing fast enough that share can be won cheaply? 's 1970 essay turned that question into a picture.

The framework

Four rules and four boxes

opened 'The Product Portfolio' with the claim that a successful company should own products with different growth rates and different market shares, because the mix of products determines the balance of cash flowing in and out. He then gave four rules that determine a product's cash flow.

  • Margins and cash generated are a function of market share. High share and high margins go together, which explained with the .
  • Growth requires cash to finance added assets. The faster a market grows, the more cash a business must spend just to hold its share.
  • High market share must be earned or bought, and buying share requires extra investment.
  • No market can grow forever. The payoff from growth must come when growth slows, in the form of cash that can no longer be usefully reinvested in that product.

From these rules came the matrix. The vertical axis shows the growth rate of the market. The horizontal axis shows , usually measured as the business's share divided by the share of its largest competitor, and drawn on a logarithmic scale. A ratio of 2.0 means the business is twice as large as its biggest rival; a ratio of 0.33 means the rival is three times larger. Each business is drawn as a circle whose size reflects its sales.

  • : high share in a high-growth market. They usually report profits but may need all of their own cash to keep up with growth. If they keep their lead, they become when the market matures.
  • : high share in a slow-growth market. They generate far more cash than they need to hold their position. argued that this surplus should not be reinvested in the same product but moved elsewhere.
  • (sometimes called problem children): low share in a high-growth market. They need large amounts of cash just to keep up. Unless they can be turned into leaders, they become when growth stops.
  • , later usually called : low share in a slow-growth market. was blunt: they may show an accounting profit, but they produce no spare cash and are 'essentially worthless, except in liquidation.'

The prescription follows directly. Milk the , use their cash to back the and a carefully chosen few , and sell or close the . summed up the ideal: 'The balanced portfolio has whose high share and high growth assure the future; that supply funds for that future growth; and to be converted into with the added funds.' , he wrote, 'are not necessary. They are evidence of failure either to obtain a leadership position during the growth phase, or to get out and cut the losses.'

The matrix also implies a life cycle. A successful product usually starts as a , becomes a if the company invests to win the lead, and becomes a as its market matures. A product that never wins the lead slides from to . The most important decisions, in 's view, are therefore made early, while the market is still growing and share can be bought.

Cases

How GE, Procter & Gamble and Google managed portfolios

was the classic home of portfolio planning. In the early 1970s, according to 's account, and developed the as a response to the same problem described. Instead of using only market growth and relative share, the grid scores each business on the attractiveness of its industry (size, growth, profitability, competition and other factors) and on the unit's competitive strength (share, costs, brand and capabilities). Units above the diagonal are candidates for investment and growth, units on the diagonal for selective investment, and units below it for harvesting, sale or closure. notes that the framework, or a descendant of it, still underlies most large companies' formal approaches to managing .

In 1981, the new chief executive turned portfolio logic into a short public rule. In a speech to Wall Street analysts at the Pierre Hotel in New York, titled 'Growing Fast in a Slow-Growth Economy', he said would keep only businesses that were, or could become, in their markets. The rest would be fixed, sold or closed. Fortune later described the speech as marking the dawn of the shareholder-value movement. 's rule is not the , but it rests on the same belief that leading share is what makes a business worth owning, and it gave managers a simple test that anyone in the company could understand.

Limits

What the matrix gets wrong

The matrix spread quickly. 's 2014 article says that at its height, in the late 1970s and early 1980s, the matrix or approaches based on it were used by about half of all Fortune 500 companies, an estimate drawn from 's January 1982 Harvard Business Review article. himself was careful about its value. He found that the grids helped head offices see what each business contributed and make clearer resource trade-offs, but the benefit came from building portfolio thinking into planning, budgeting and the way different units were managed, not from drawing the chart once.

Several criticisms followed. Market share and market growth are only two of the many things that decide whether a business is attractive, and both depend on how the market is defined: a small brand may look like a in 'soft drinks' and a in 'sparkling tea'. Labelling a unit a can also become self-fulfilling, because managers stop investing in it and good people leave. The matrix assumes that cash can move freely inside the company and ignores links between businesses, such as a weak unit that supplies parts to a strong one. And it says little about how to turn a into a , which is where most of the real strategic work lies.

The most striking evidence came from an experiment. and asked 1,015 people, working individually as managers in 27 sessions in six countries over five years, to choose between two investments, one of which would clearly double the money and one of which would lose half of it. Information about the made people more likely to choose the unprofitable one. Of the subjects shown the matrix, 64 per cent picked the losing investment, and of those who actually used the matrix in their analysis, 87 per cent did. The matrix had taught them to prefer a 'star' over a plain calculation of returns.

's own 2014 review accepted part of this criticism. Studying US public companies, it found that in 75 per cent of industries businesses moved between quadrants faster in 2008 to 2012 than in 1988 to 1992, with the average time in a quadrant falling from about four years to under two. It also found that captured a 25 per cent smaller share of total profits in 2012 than in 1982. Its conclusion was that market share is no longer a reliable predictor of performance, so the matrix should be used more often and more experimentally, with more tested cheaply and kept 'on a short leash'.

After

How the idea is used now

Very few companies now run their business strictly by the four boxes, but the underlying idea, that a multi-business company must deliberately move cash from mature businesses to growing ones, is still central to corporate strategy. 's 2012 annual report, quoted by , described 'rigorously testing our portfolio to identify which businesses to grow, run for cash, fix or sell', which is almost exactly 's language. The same logic is used for product ranges, brand portfolios, R&D projects and even venture capital funds, where a few winners must pay for many failures.

The matrix works best as a starting conversation rather than a verdict. It forces a company to ask which of its activities pay the bills, which ones need money now, and which ones it is keeping only out of habit. It works worst when the labels replace judgement, as the and experiment showed.

Fun fact

It was pets before it was dogs, and BCG started with 500 dollars

In 's original 1970 essay the low-share, low-growth box is called 'pets', not 'dogs'; 'dogs' became the common name later. The firm that made the matrix famous began small: in its first month in 1963, the consulting division set up inside a Boston bank billed only 500 US dollars.
