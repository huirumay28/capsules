Taiwan : 'Sing our own songs' (1975–1985)

In the mid-1970s Taiwanese university students mostly sang American and British pop. Within a few years they were writing and recording their own Mandarin songs, set to modern poems, campfire tunes and Indigenous melodies. Before

Before campus folk: Western pop, camp songs and field recordings

In the early 1970s the music young Taiwanese people loved most was Western pop. University students sang American and British hits in coffee houses and at parties, while the Mandarin pop industry mostly produced film songs and ballads. had been in force since 1949, every record had to be approved by the , and songs could be banned for their lyrics.

Two quieter traditions were already feeding the movement that followed. In the mid-1960s the composers and led a folk-song collecting project, travelling around Taiwan from 1965 to 1967 to record traditional Han and Indigenous music. They gathered about 1,700 to 2,000 songs and found singers such as , an elderly Hengchun folk singer who accompanied himself on the moon lute. At the same time, student clubs and the summer camps spread simple singalong songs, some adapted from Indigenous melodies and given Mandarin words, through pocket songbooks.

Politics then gave students a reason to look inward. In 1971 the Republic of China lost its seat at the United Nations, and in 1972 Japan broke diplomatic relations and Richard Nixon visited Beijing. Many young people began asking why they sang other people's songs, and what their own songs would sound like.

Origins

Yang Hsien and the modern poem as a song

In summer 1974, at one of Hu's concerts, Yang performed his own musical setting of (), a poem by , with Yu in the audience. Yu was pleased and encouraged him to set more poems from his collection ().

On 6 June 1975 Yang held a 'modern creation concert' at Taipei . The first half was English songs; the second half was his settings of Yu's poems, combining guitar, piano and violin with a feeling for classical Chinese imagery. The album that came out of it, (), sold out its first pressing of 30,000 copies within a month. It is widely treated as the start of the modern folk movement, and Yang was later called its father.

The word , , immediately caused an argument. Academic musicians said a is created collectively, passed on orally and tested by time, so a graduate student's settings of a living poet could not be 'folk' at all. Deep dive

Li Shuang-tze and the Coca-Cola bottle

, born in 1949 to an overseas Chinese family from the Philippines, came to Taiwan as a child, studied mathematics at and spent most of his time painting. Working around Taipei in 1972, he met at the and asked him a famous question: 'You're Puyuma, right? Don't your people have your own songs? Sing one of your own.' Hu, who was performing to pay for his father's surgery, could not think of one at first, then remembered (), a song his father used to sing.

On 3 December 1976 Tamkang held a Western folk concert hosted by the radio presenter . Li walked on stage carrying a guitar and a bottle of Coca-Cola. He sang four Taiwanese songs, 補破網, 恆春之歌, 雨夜花 and 望春風, stopped partway through the patriotic 國父紀念歌 to ask why nobody was singing along, then sang 's and asked the audience why they had paid twenty dollars to hear Chinese people sing foreign songs. Students from a campus current-affairs club had been planted in the crowd to boo and to shout down the booing. Politics

Two anthems pulled in opposite directions

Two campus songs show how politics took over the music. When the United States announced on 15 December 1978 that it would recognise the People's Republic of China, the student songwriter , who had grown up in a military dependents' village in Kaohsiung, wrote (). Its lyrics describe the Yangtze and the Yellow River and call on the 'great dragon' to open its eyes. 's recording, released in 1980, topped the Minsheng Daily chart for fifteen weeks, and the government welcomed it as a patriotic song.

Then Hou left for the mainland in 1983 against a travel ban, and the song was banned in Taiwan for a few years while Beijing embraced it as a call for Chinese unity. Hou objected that people had misread him. In 1989 he joined the Tiananmen protests, changed 'appeasers' in the lyrics to 'dictators', and on 2 June began a hunger strike in the square with , Zhou Duo and Gao Xin. On the morning of 4 June he helped negotiate the students' withdrawal from the square, and he was later expelled from China.

The turn

Lo Ta-yu ends the innocence

, born in Taipei in 1954, finished medical school in Taichung to satisfy his family and then gave up medicine for music. He wrote film music and songs for others in the late 1970s, including 童年 (Childhood) for , and in 1982 , a label founded only in 1980, released his first album, (). The title is made only of grammatical particles from classical Chinese, a joke at the expense of empty learning.

mixed rock, folk and reggae with lyrics about urban emptiness, Confucian stiffness and a small town losing itself in (). Lo appeared in dark sunglasses, far from the clean-cut image of the contest singers. The album is often called the start of a new era of Mandarin pop, and his 1983 album 未來的主人翁 (Master of the Future) went further with (), named after Wu Zhuoliu's novel about Japanese colonial Taiwan.

In 1985 Lo wrote (), modelled on Band Aid and We Are the World, which had raised money for Ethiopian famine relief. More than 60 singers from Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore and Malaysia recorded it, and it was released on 25 October 1985 with proceeds donated to Taiwan's Consumers' Foundation (消基會). Lo's first lyrics were so critical of society that singers walked out, and under he was persuaded to let other songwriters rewrite them. He moved to New York and then to Hong Kong in 1986, citing political pressure on his work.

Analysis

What campus folk was really about

was a search for identity carried out by students who were not allowed to discuss identity openly. Its slogan asked what 'our own' songs were, and different people gave different answers: the classical Chinese imagery of and , the Taiwanese and Indigenous songs that and wanted to recover, or simply songs about everyday student life. The vague, beautiful 'China' of many hits was safe under ; songs about Taiwan itself, like , were not.

It was also an early lesson in how sponsors shape culture. Appliance makers and record labels turned a protest slogan into contests, compilations and TV specials within about two years. That commercial push gave Taiwan a professional songwriting industry, a generation of producers such as , and the model of the singer-songwriter that later took for granted. It also smoothed the music into something polite, which is why the Tamkang circle rejected it and why 's harder sound felt like a break.

Finally, it is notable how many women carried the movement. opened her radio show to young writers and organised ; preserved Li's songs and sang them at political events; , , and were among its best-known voices; and and supplied lyrics. At the 's launch party in 1979, the opposition activist Tien Meng-shu told in Hokkien to stop singing that 'pig song' in Mandarin; Yang later called this Hokkien chauvinism. It is a reminder that the movement's arguments were about language and ethnic identity as well as nation.

Elsewhere

What was happening elsewhere at the same time

In Britain and the United States, 1976–1977 was the year of punk, another youth movement built on the idea that ordinary people could make their own music without permission from the industry (see the punk lesson). The sound could not have been more different, but the impulse was related. 's direct model was the American folk revival of the early 1960s, the music of and , which Taiwanese students knew from records (see the folk revival lesson).

In Chile, the movement had already shown how dangerous folk songs could be. The singer was killed on 16 September 1973, days after the military coup. In Taiwan nobody was killed for a campus song, but the banning of shows the same instinct in a softer authoritarian state.

Across the Chinese-speaking world, Taiwanese songs spread fast. 's ballads were smuggled into mainland China on cassette from about 1978, and and other campus songs followed. Singapore developed its own student songwriting style, , in the late 1970s and 1980s, and Hong Kong developed 'city folk'. In Beijing, 's Nothing to My Name started Chinese rock in the mid-1980s, and in 1989 Tiananmen protesters sang it alongside 's .

After

After the movement

By the early 1980s the movement had faded. The had held its last yearly contest in 1980, with one final edition in 1984, closed, and many singers went abroad to study, did military service or moved to Hong Kong and the mainland. Taiwan TV's contest kept student songwriting alive from 1984 to 1993 and launched Sky Chang (張清芳). , and UFO Records, founded by people, built the industry of the late 1980s and 1990s on the foundations had laid, and itself was bought by in 1993.

Some of the movement's people went in political directions. joined the movement and in December 1984 became the founding chair of the , after seeing that many of the victims of that year's Haishan coal mine explosion were Indigenous. He recorded his first solo album only in 2005 and won Golden Melody Awards for songwriting and song of the year in 2006. In 2015 the concerts celebrated forty years since 's first show, and the campus songs remain fixtures of KTV and nostalgia for Taiwanese people born in the 1960s and early 1970s.
