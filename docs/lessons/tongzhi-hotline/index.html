The (): a phone line that became Taiwan's first registered LGBTQ+ organization

In March 1998 a newspaper report about a teenage gay suicide pushed a handful of activists to set up a telephone line where gay, lesbian, bisexual and transgender callers could talk to someone like themselves. The first call came in at seven in the evening on 27 June 1998. Two years later the group registered with the Ministry of the Interior and became the first LGBTQ+ organization in Taiwan with legal status. Today answers calls, trains teachers and nurses, supports parents, runs prevention and elder-care work, organizes Asia's first , and lobbies for laws. This Monday lesson covers its history, its services, how it pays for itself, and whether an LGBTQ+ organization fits 's plan to give to women's causes.

Before

Before 1998: a decade of new freedom with nowhere to call

Martial law ended in Taiwan in July 1987, and the following decade was full of new organizations: women's groups, labour groups, environmental groups and, by the mid-1990s, the first openly gay and lesbian groups. The lesbian group 我們之間 was founded in 1990, university gay and lesbian clubs appeared on campuses in the mid-1990s, and the writer and activist () had been petitioning for same-sex marriage alone since 1986. The Monday lessons on the and Taiwan's women's movement describe the same period from the feminist side.

What did not yet exist was a stable service that an isolated person could reach without to anyone. Ordinary counselling lines such as and () were the main places Taiwanese people phoned when they were in distress, but the founders felt these services did not really understand gay lives, and some young gay people felt pressured to 'correct' themselves. In 1997 and 1998 the newspapers carried a series of stories about young gay and lesbian people who had killed themselves or were in crisis. A volunteer wrote in 1998 that reading them and remembering their own adolescence was what made the founders act.

Founding

1998 to 2000: four groups, one rented room in Gongguan

was set up by four groups working together: the (, described in the 1998 article as a group of gay professionals in psychology and social work), the (), the lesbian gender-and-class group (which later became the , ) and members of the (). The activist () is named as a founder. They rented an empty room near National Taiwan University in and furnished it with donations.

On the evening of 26 June 1998 they held a fundraising show in the NTU student activity centre. The next evening, at seven o'clock, the first call came in. At the start the counselling line was open only on Saturday and Sunday evenings from seven to ten, and the first volunteers on the phones were the trained counsellors from the . Within the first month, the volunteer wrote, they believed they had helped save one or two lives, helped several parents understand their children, and even received a supportive call from mainland China.

The key idea was (): gay, lesbian, bisexual and transgender volunteers, selected and trained, answering calls from people like themselves, so that a caller would not have to 'try their luck' with a counsellor who might not understand. Chinese Wikipedia describes this as a feminist peer-counselling approach, which connects to the women's movement's older idea that people who share an experience can help each other without a hierarchy of expert and patient.

On 9 June 2000 registered with the Ministry of the Interior's Department of Social Affairs as a national , , and hired its first paid staff member. It was the first LGBTQ+ organization in Taiwan to gain legal registration. In October 2002 it moved to its current neighbourhood in , Taipei.

Schools

2000 to 2004: Yeh Yung-chih and the Gender Equity Education Act

On 20 April 2000 (), a third-year student at 高樹國中 in Pingtung County, was found bleeding in a school toilet and died the next day. He had been teased and harassed for years because he was gentle and 'girlish', and he had been afraid to use the toilet during breaks. His death was not a story about sexual orientation, since no one knew how he would have described himself. It was a story about (), the way a boy was punished for not acting like a boy.

Educators, women's groups and LGBTQ+ groups used the case to argue that schools needed a law. The () was promulgated on 23 June 2004. It was the first Taiwanese law to protect people on the basis of sexual orientation and , and it required schools to teach gender equity. took part in the drafting campaign. The law gave it a door into schools: volunteers began to give 'getting to know ' () talks to students and teachers, sharing their own life stories, and began to run training camps for teachers.

That door has never been fully secure. In 2011 conservative groups campaigned against new teaching materials, and and other groups sued one group for spreading false claims about them. In 2018 one of the referendums asked voters to stop LGBTQ+ education in primary and junior high schools, and it passed. Even so, in 2025 gave 152 talks to student audiences reaching 9,812 students, and its 2020 and 2025 surveys of LGBTQ+ secondary school students are among the main sources of evidence about how these students are treated.

Families

Parents, HIV and older people: widening the circle

By 2003 had learned that many calls came from parents who had just learned that a child was gay and had no one to ask. It formed a family group in April 2003 and in December published 《》 with the publisher 心靈工坊, a book of the stories of seven mothers and one father. Parents who met through the group founded their own organization in 2006, . Its name is a pun: when a child comes out of the closet (), the parents go into it (入櫃).

began prevention work among gay men in 2002. In 2005 it published 《》, a safer-sex handbook modelled on the British group , and later it handed out small kits with condoms and lubricant. Its approach has always been to talk about sex plainly and with pleasure rather than fear, because shame keeps people from testing. In 2025 it marked twenty years of this work with an exhibition.

Later added groups for older gay men and lesbians, for transgender people, for lesbians specifically, for people with disabilities, and for people in non-monogamous relationships. The work on older people began with an oral history project with older gay men in northern Taiwan, and today it includes lectures for long-term care workers about how to care for an old person who may never have come out.

Marriage

2016 to 2019: marriage equality, a referendum defeat, and a law

Marriage equality became a mass movement in late 2016. On 16 October 2016 the French-born retired professor , who had lived with his Taiwanese partner 曾敬超 for decades and was shut out of decisions during his partner's final illness, died after falling from his apartment. His death moved many people, and that December a very large marriage equality rally filled Ketagalan Boulevard in Taipei. In 2017 , the Taiwan LGBT Family Rights Advocacy, the , the Taiwan Alliance to Promote Civil Partnership Rights and the streaming platform GagaOOLala formed the ().

On 24 May 2017 the Constitutional Court issued , in a case brought by and the Taipei city government, holding that the Civil Code's failure to let two people of the same sex marry violated the constitution, and giving the legislature two years to fix it. Opponents answered with referendums. On 24 November 2018 voters approved proposals to keep marriage in the Civil Code as a union of a man and a woman and to stop LGBTQ+ education in schools, and rejected the proposals put forward by equality campaigners.

For a counselling line this was a public health event as well as a political one. Out.com reported that calls to rose about 40 percent after the vote, with around four in ten calls about the referendum. A 2019 study that compared an online survey taken twenty-three months before the vote with a second survey taken one week after it found that the share of non-heterosexual respondents with suicidal thoughts rose from 15.4 percent to 24.6 percent, while among heterosexual respondents it did not rise (6.3 percent to 5.2 percent).

The legislature then passed a separate law rather than amending the Civil Code: the (), passed on 17 May 2019 and in force from 24 May 2019. Taiwan became the first place in Asia where two people of the same sex could marry. The Coalition registered in May 2020 as the (), with on its board.

Today

What the Hotline does now: the 2025 annual report

According to its 2025 annual report, the Taipei headquarters had eleven full-time staff and nearly a thousand volunteers, and a small southern office in Kaohsiung serves southern Taiwan. Almost every service is run by a volunteer group that meets every two weeks, with staff coordinating. Some of the 2025 figures:

  • Telephone counselling: 708 hours of service, 1,624 calls from 375 people, each call about 32 minutes on average, staffed by 41 volunteers and 15 supervisors five days a week. 63 percent of calls were about seeking support and companionship, 12 percent about relationships and 11 percent about and sexual health. Nearly 80 percent of callers were between 19 and 40.
  • Education: 236 talks to 14,994 people, including 152 talks to students, 28 to government agencies and 26 to medical and social service workers.
  • Families: support for nearly 350 parents and LGBTQ+ people through parent groups in Taipei and Taichung, a parents' phone line staffed by volunteer parents twice a week, coming-out consultations for 43 people, and a podcast on parents and children.
  • and sexual health: a sex education website for gay men with 85,499 visitors and 40 talks. Its anonymous and syphilis testing served 69 people before it was suspended in April 2025 for lack of staff.
  • Transgender services: support groups, talks, a podcast, a book of transgender life stories recommended by the Ministry of Culture, and the seventh Taiwan , with more than 50 organizations and about 3,500 participants.
  • Advocacy: lobbying for an , editing the LGBTQ+ chapters of the civil society parallel report on the and , and sitting on the gender equality committees of the Executive Yuan and several city governments.

Two things stand out. First, much of the work is aimed at the people around LGBTQ+ people (parents, teachers, nurses, care workers, civil servants) rather than only at the community itself. Second, the 2025 report is frank that the organization is under pressure. The chairperson, the veterinarian , wrote that international funding had been cut sharply and that domestic political conflict had stalled gender policy, and the suspension of the testing service shows what happens when money and staff run short.

Money

How the Hotline is funded, and what it publishes

is an (), not a (). This matters for anyone reading its accounts. A , like the or the Women's Rescue in earlier lessons, is built around an endowment of money and is governed by the (), which requires it to publish its financial statements. An is built around its members, who elect a board, and is governed by Taiwan's law on civic associations. It has no large endowment, so it lives year to year on what it raises. When an raises money from the public, however, it needs a under the (), and it must publish the donations it received and how they were used.

's public reports show where its money comes from, even though they do not give one audited total for recent years:

  • The annual fundraising banquet in Taipei is its most important single event. It raised NT$5 million in 2024 and NT$5.1 million in 2025. The Kaohsiung banquet raised NT$700,000 in each of those years.
  • Monthly and one-off donations from individuals, collected online. publishes donor lists (with names partly hidden) each year, and its website shows its Ministry of Health and Welfare number (第1151361068號).
  • A rainbow debit card with (), which donates 0.2 percent of card spending to .
  • Sales from its online shop of rainbow goods.
  • Donations from the United States through , and an that lets American foundations treat it like a US public charity.
  • Grants for specific programmes, including from international funders. The 2025 report says international funding fell sharply that year.

For scale, the budget table in 's 2020 annual report planned NT$14,725,851 of spending for the Taipei office and NT$2,677,620 for the southern office, about NT$17.4 million in total. In that table the largest single line was administration and overheads (NT$4,174,137), which in a small organization mostly means staff salaries and rent, followed by services (NT$1,134,524) and service promotion (NT$1,101,144). The telephone line itself was planned at NT$824,844. These are planned figures for 2020, not audited results for a recent year.

Compared with the foundations in earlier Monday lessons, is mid-sized. The 's yearly budget is many times larger, and the Women's Rescue spent about NT$33.7 million in 2025, while the 's 2025 income was about NT$5.75 million. Because so much of its income comes from one evening each year and from many small donors, a gift from would be a noticeable share of a programme budget rather than a rounding error. It also means depends heavily on community goodwill, which is a strength and a risk.

If wanted to partner with , a sensible first question would be to ask for its most recent audited income and expenditure statement and its fundraising disclosure (勸募活動收支報告), and to ask whether a donation could be restricted to a named programme, such as the parents' line or the transgender support groups.

For cool dairy

Is an LGBTQ+ organization a 'women's organization'?

's plan is to give its proceeds to Taiwan women's organizations. is not a women's organization in the narrow sense. Its services are for LGBTQ+ people of every gender and their families, and a large part of its work is with gay men. There are still good reasons to consider it:

  • Lesbian, bisexual and transgender women are part of its core community. One of its four founding groups, , was a lesbian group, and its budgets and reports include lesbian services in Taipei and Kaohsiung.
  • Mothers are at the heart of its family work: the book that started its parents' programme told the stories of seven mothers and one father.
  • The 2019 referendum study found that suicidal thoughts rose among non-heterosexual women, younger people, and gay, lesbian and bisexual respondents.
  • Taiwan's feminist and LGBTQ+ movements have worked together for decades. The was a founding member of the , and the protects both girls and LGBTQ+ students.
  • Its transgender work sits at the centre of a live debate in Taiwan and abroad about how women's spaces and transgender rights fit together. In the 2024 annual report the chairperson warned that anti-transgender arguments were dividing cisgender and transgender women, 'two groups who are both oppressed under patriarchy', and called for 'empathy and conversations' to find common ground.

There are also reasons for care. If tells shoppers their money goes to 'women's organizations', it should explain clearly why an LGBTQ+ group is included, or it should restrict the gift to a programme that clearly serves women, such as lesbian and transgender women's groups or the parents' work. Questions worth asking before choosing:

  • Can fund a named programme, and will report back on how that money was spent?
  • Does 's audited income show it is stable, and how much does it depend on government or foreign grants that may disappear?
  • Would rather fund one large organization or split its giving between a women's and ?
  • How would describe the partnership on product tags and the website so shoppers understand it?

Elsewhere

1998 elsewhere: Trevor, Matthew Shepard, and the older London line

's founding year was the same year LGBTQ+ youth suicide became a public cause in the United States. was founded in 1998 by the makers of the Oscar-winning short film , about a thirteen-year-old gay boy who attempts suicide. When HBO broadcast the film on 11 August 1998, its makers launched the , because they found there was no crisis line for LGBTQ+ young people. Two months later, in October 1998, the murder of the student in Wyoming made anti-gay violence national news. Taiwan's activists and American activists were responding to the same problem in the same months without coordinating.

Phone lines were an older tool in Britain. (now ) began in 1974 and was the model for many later lines. In mainland China, homosexuality was removed from the official list of mental disorders only in 2001. Against that background, what was unusual about Taiwan was how quickly a phone line turned into a registered organization with a seat on government committees, and how closely it worked with the women's movement.

Fun fact

Parents go into the closet

The parents' group that grew out of is called . 櫃 means closet, and the name explains itself: when a child comes out of the closet (), the parents go into it (入櫃), because they now have a secret of their own to manage with relatives and neighbours. Among 's 2025 social events for parents was one that took parents to a gay bar.
