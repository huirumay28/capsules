Before

Before care ethics had a name, Kohlberg's stages already ranked justice above relationship

Mid-twentieth-century moral psychology and much of Anglo-American moral philosophy treated impartial justice as the adult form of moral thought. Rights, rules, and universalizable principles counted as mature. Context, attachment, and the maintenance of particular relationships looked like something you were supposed to grow out of.

's made that ranking concrete. In his model, people moved from obedience and self-interest through conventional loyalty toward postconventional reasoning about abstract justice. When girls and women were scored on his dilemmas, they often landed at stages that privileged interpersonal harmony. The research program heard that as incomplete development — stuck short of principled autonomy — rather than as a different moral language.

That was the setup walked into. The problem was not only that women scored lower. It was that the yardstick itself defined moral maturity as distancing oneself from in the name of impartial principle. Relational responsibility was coded as immaturity before anyone had named an .

The concept

What care ethics claims: interdependence, attentiveness, responsibility for concrete others

argues that moral life is organized around relationships of . People need care to survive infancy, illness, disability, and old age; they also need ongoing to remain recognizably themselves in ordinary life. Morality, on this view, is not first a contest among independent rights-bearers. It is the work of noticing need, taking responsibility for particular others, and sustaining the conditions in which those others can live as well as possible.

Several claims travel together. First, dependency is normal, not a temporary failure of autonomy. Second, moral attention should be particular: the question is often what this person needs in this situation, not only what a universal rule would permit. Third, emotion and are informative for judgment, not mere noise to be filtered out. Fourth, justice remains important, but justice theories that start from independent adults miss the unpaid and underpaid care labor that makes independence possible for some by shifting dependency onto others.

is therefore not a soft synonym for kindness. It is a challenge to a picture of the moral agent as ideally unencumbered. It asks who is , who does the , whether the care is competent, and how societies distribute the costs of dependency — questions that a justice-only vocabulary can postpone indefinitely.

Origin

Gilligan, In a Different Voice (1982): care as a moral orientation, not a failed stage

's appeared from in 1982. had worked in the Harvard world around 's research. Listening to how women described moral conflict — including abortion decisions and responses to standard dilemmas — she heard a language of responsibility, relationship, and avoiding hurt that the stage system demoted.

Her argument against was precise. She did not claim that women never reason about justice, or that men never reason about care. She claimed that a justice-centered scoring scheme mistook a for developmental delay. When the asked whether a man should steal a drug to save his wife, one child's rights hierarchy looked like mature principle; another's insistence on talking through the relationships looked like confusion. read the second answer as a coherent moral orientation: the world as a web of relationships to be mended, not a math problem of ranked rights.

The book made "a " famous. It also created a problem and later writers had to keep correcting: readers often heard her as saying that care is women's essence. 's stronger claim was thematic — care versus justice as orientations — even while her early evidence drew heavily on girls' and women's accounts. The essentialist reception became one of the tradition's longest internal debates.

Elsewhere

Same years, another polity: Sevenhuijsen and care as citizenship in the Netherlands

While was rewriting care as American political theory, was developing inside Dutch and European debates about citizenship and the welfare state. Her (Routledge, 1998) did not wait for a U.S. export label. It asked how public institutions should take responsibility for care needs, how citizenship looks if dependency is assumed rather than denied, and how justice talk changes when is treated as political work.

That strand matters because it is contemporaneous and concrete. Dutch social policy already organized substantial public childcare, elder care, and health infrastructure; 's contribution was to give feminist a vocabulary for evaluating those arrangements as questions of citizenship, not only private virtue. After

Nursing ethics, disability and dependency, political theory of care

After the 1980s–1990s founding texts, entered professions and neighboring fields. drew on care as a way to describe relational responsibility in clinical work — to particular patients, the moral weight of bodily vulnerability, and the difference between competent care and mere protocol. The fit was natural and contested: nursing had long been coded as women's work, and care language could either dignify that labor or trap nurses in self-sacrifice scripts. The useful inheritance was the demand to evaluate care practices, power, and institutional support — not a sentimental nurse ideal.

In political theory, 's (2006) and 's later (2013) treated care as a public good and a democratic criterion: how a society organizes childcare, elder care, disability support, and paid care labor is a measure of justice, not a private afterthought. Contemporary debates continue over global care chains, migrant domestic labor, pandemic care failures, and whether remains distinct from virtue ethics or becomes one resource among several. The through-line from remains: do not score relational responsibility as moral immaturity, and do not privatize the labor that keeps dependents alive.

A question

A question to keep asking

When you make a moral or political judgment, do you ask only which rule or right wins — or do you also ask who will do the care work, whose needs are noticed or ignored, whether the care is competent, and which relationships are sustained or broken for whom?
