Before

Before the movement: women's work grows, but law and politics stay closed

In the 1960s and 1970s Taiwan industrialized very quickly. Export factories in electronics, textiles and plastics hired large numbers of young women, and women's share of the workforce rose from about a third in the mid-1960s to over two-fifths by 1973. Women were earning money, but the law still treated the family as something run by the husband.

The , written in the Republic of China in the 1930s, gave the husband and father priority in many family decisions. When parents disagreed about a child, the father's decision prevailed. A wife was generally expected to live where her husband lived. Property acquired during marriage was often treated as the husband's. These rules were not only on paper. They shaped what happened in divorce, in custody disputes and in cases of violence, because a woman who left an abusive husband could lose both her home and her children.

At the same time, Taiwan had been under since 1949. The government restricted new political parties, newspapers and civic associations. Official women's organizations existed, but they were linked to the ruling Kuomintang and focused on supporting the state and the family. An independent feminist movement would have to find space inside these limits.

The idea

What 'New Feminism' meant

, a lawyer who had studied law at National Taiwan University and then in the United States, began writing and speaking about what she called in the early 1970s. Her arguments were deliberately moderate. She did not attack the family or marriage. Instead, she argued that women should be treated first as full persons, that they should be able to develop their talents in education and work, and that the law should treat them as equal citizens.

This moderation was a strategy as well as a belief. Under , openly radical arguments could be treated as political subversion. By framing feminism around fairness, education and the modern economy, could reach educated middle-class women through essays, talks and a small publishing operation. Historians often describe this as the founding of modern feminist thinking in Taiwan, and as the first organized women's-rights effort, starting around 1972.

The idea that grew from this period is that women's equality in Taiwan would be won mainly through law. Later activists kept that focus: they drafted bills, lobbied legislators and brought constitutional challenges. This is one of the clearest differences between Taiwan's movement and, for example, the American second wave, which put more energy into consciousness-raising groups and cultural critique.

For the rest of the 1980s it was the only independent group publicly campaigning for gender equality. Turning points

Two cases that changed public opinion

Laws are usually passed when a problem becomes impossible to ignore. In the 1990s, two cases made violence against women impossible to ignore in Taiwan.

The first was the case of . In late 1993 in Banqiao, in what is now New Taipei, Deng killed her husband after years of severe abuse by him. Under the law at the time, she had very few ways to protect herself: there were no civil , and police and courts tended to treat violence inside the home as a private family matter. Women's groups, human-rights groups and the opposition 's women's committee rallied to support her. She was sentenced to three years in prison, a far lighter sentence than a homicide normally brought. The case raised a hard question in public: if the law offered an abused wife no protection, what was she supposed to do?

The second case was the disappearance of , the director of the 's women's affairs department, on the night of 30 November 1996 in Kaohsiung. She was last seen getting into a taxi after a party meeting. That same day, the party congress approved her proposal that a quarter of its elected seats be reserved for women. She was found murdered days later. The case was never solved; the statute of limitations ran out in 2016. Her death, together with other violent crimes, brought tens of thousands of people into the streets in May 1997 to demand better protection for women and for public safety.

These cases connected the private and the public. One showed that violence at home was a matter for the state. The other showed that a woman could be killed simply by trying to get home safely at night. Together they gave feminist groups the public support they needed to pass laws they had already been drafting.

The laws

Rewriting the law, one statute at a time

Family law came first. In 1994 the Council of Grand Justices, Taiwan's constitutional court, issued . It ruled that the clause giving fathers the final say over children violated the constitution's guarantee of equality between men and women. The legislature then revised the custody rules in 1996, so that courts would decide custody based on the best interests of the child rather than defaulting to the father. Further reforms in the following years changed the rules on marital property and residence.

Laws on violence followed. A law on preventing sexual assault was passed in 1997. On 24 June 1998, the Legislative Yuan passed the (). It allowed victims to apply to courts for , which could order an abuser to stay away, to move out of the home or to attend treatment. It required local governments to set up domestic violence prevention centers, and it made police, doctors, social workers and teachers responsible for reporting. The act is often called the first domestic violence law in Asia. That claim is disputed: Malaysia passed a Domestic Violence Act in 1994, and South Korea passed domestic violence laws in 1997. It is fair to say that Taiwan's act was among the first in Asia and was unusually comprehensive.

Work and school came next. had drafted a gender equality in employment bill in 1989. It finally passed in 2001 and took effect in 2002, banning discrimination in hiring, pay, promotion and dismissal, outlawing rules like the ones the Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall workers had fought, and creating rights to parental leave. In 2004 the required schools to teach gender equity and to protect students regardless of gender, gender identity or sexual orientation. That act was shaped by the death in April 2000 of , a gender-nonconforming junior high student who had been bullied and was found dying in a school bathroom.

Debate

Disagreements inside the movement

Taiwan's feminists did not always agree. The sharpest split came in 1997, when Taipei's mayor at the time, , abolished the city's system of licensed prostitution. Some feminists supported abolition as a way to protect women from exploitation. Others, including activists associated with the sex-workers' movement, argued that ending licences took away the livelihood and legal standing of the licensed women without giving them anything in return. The debate was framed as a conflict between 'sexual liberation' and 'sexual critique', and it divided activists for years.

By the late 1990s the movement had also widened. Groups began working on the division of household labour, the decriminalization of adultery, the rights of immigrant wives from Southeast Asia and China, and LGBTQ rights. The itself marched in Taiwan's early Pride parades. Some critics argued that a legal strategy helped educated, middle-class women most and did less for factory workers, immigrant spouses and Indigenous women. Supporters answered that laws such as the protect everyone, including the women who have the least power.

Elsewhere

What was happening elsewhere in East Asia

South Korea followed a similar path, because its movement also grew alongside democratization. The was founded in 1983 to help victims of domestic violence, and after the country's democratic transition in 1987 women's groups pushed through domestic violence laws in 1997, a year before Taiwan's.

Japan democratized much earlier but moved more slowly on these issues. It passed its in 1985, after ratifying the UN , but the law was widely criticized as weak in its early years. Japan's domestic violence law came in 2001, three years after Taiwan's.

Taiwan's situation was unusual in one respect. Because it was excluded from the United Nations after 1971, it could not formally ratify UN human-rights treaties. Its women's rights laws were therefore driven mainly by domestic activists and court rulings rather than by international treaty obligations. Taiwan later adopted into domestic law by its own legislation in 2011.

After

What followed

International indices now rank Taiwan as one of the most gender-equal places in Asia; one ranking based on the UN's Gender Inequality Index placed it sixth in the world in 2019. Women's share of seats in the Legislative Yuan has grown steadily, helped by quota rules of the kind fought for.

Many of the laws from this period are still being amended. The has been revised several times, including to cover violence in dating relationships. The became part of the background to the long debate that led to same-sex marriage in 2019. For anyone interested in Taiwan's social issues today, much of the legal framework for women's safety, children's education and LGBTQ students goes back to the decisions activists made between 1987 and 2004.

Think about it

A question to take with you

Taiwan's movement chose to work mainly through law and lobbying rather than through cultural protest. What did that choice make possible, and which women's problems are hardest to solve through a new statute alone?
