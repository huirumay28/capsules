The Russian : from a women's bread strike to power

In 1917 Russia went through two revolutions in eight months. The first began on , when women textile workers in Petrograd walked out of their factories to protest bread shortages, and within a week the three-hundred-year-old was gone. The second, in , put Lenin's party in power and led to a , a one-party state and the Union. This lesson explains how a failing empire at war collapsed, why the liberal could not hold on, how the took power, what the revolution promised and delivered for women, and how 1917 looked from Beijing, Tokyo and Taipei.

Russia still used the in 1917, which ran thirteen days behind the Gregorian calendar used in Western Europe. This lesson gives Russian () dates for events inside Russia, as historians usually do, and adds the Western date where it matters. That is why the 'February' Revolution began on 8 March and the 'October' Revolution took place on 7 November by the Western calendar. Figures for crowd sizes, election results and deaths vary between sources; this lesson gives rounded figures and ranges rather than precise numbers. The meaning of 1917, whether a popular revolution, a coup by a small party, or both, is still argued over, and the lesson presents the main positions without settling the debate.

Before

An autocracy that would not share power

At the start of the twentieth century the Russian Empire was the largest country in the world, stretching from Poland to the Pacific. It was ruled by Tsar of the , which had held the throne since 1613. Nicholas believed that God had given him unlimited power and that his duty was to pass it on undiminished to his son. There was no national parliament, political parties were illegal, and newspapers were censored.

Underneath this old political system the country was changing quickly. Most Russians were still peasants, and serfdom had been abolished only in 1861. But from the 1890s the state pushed rapid industrialisation, financed by foreign loans and built around railways, mines and huge factories. St Petersburg and Moscow filled with workers who lived in crowded barracks, worked eleven-hour days and had no legal unions. Educated Russians, the , argued about how to change the country, and many turned to revolutionary ideas.

Several revolutionary parties formed. The , or , saw the peasants as the core of the revolution and wanted the land given to those who worked it. The Russian Social Democratic Labour Party followed Marx and focused on industrial workers. At its congress in 1903 it split into two factions: the ('majority'), led by , who wanted a small, disciplined party of professional revolutionaries, and the ('minority'), led by , who favoured a broader, more open party. Liberals, organised from 1905 as the or , wanted a constitutional monarchy or a parliamentary republic.

The first great warning came in 1905. Russia was losing a war against Japan in Manchuria. On 9 January 1905 a priest named led a peaceful march of workers and their families to the to present a petition to the tsar. Troops opened fire and killed many marchers. The day became known as , and it set off a year of strikes, mutinies and peasant uprisings. Workers in St Petersburg created a council of elected delegates, a , in which the young became a leading figure. To end a general strike, Nicholas issued the , which promised civil liberties and an elected parliament called the .

Nicholas never accepted the as a real partner. He dissolved the first two Dumas quickly and changed the electoral law in 1907 to favour landowners. His prime minister combined harsh repression with a land reform meant to create a class of independent peasant farmers, but Stolypin was assassinated in 1911. When Russia entered the First World War in August 1914, the regime had survived 1905 without solving any of the problems that caused it.

The war turned these problems into a crisis. The Russian army suffered enormous losses in 1914 and 1915. In September 1915 Nicholas took personal command of the army and moved to headquarters, which meant every defeat was now blamed on him. In Petrograd, as the capital had been renamed, the Empress Alexandra, who was German-born and deeply unpopular, gained influence over appointments. She trusted , a Siberian holy man who seemed able to ease the bleeding of her son Alexei, who had haemophilia. Rumours about Rasputin did great damage to the monarchy's reputation, and in December 1916 a group of aristocrats murdered him. By the winter of 1916–17 trains could not bring enough food and fuel to the cities, prices had risen sharply, and women spent hours in bread queues in the cold.

February

Five days that ended the monarchy

The revolution began on 23 February 1917, which was 8 March by the Western calendar and , a socialist holiday celebrated since 1911. In the Vyborg district of Petrograd, women textile workers walked out of their factories and called on men in the nearby metal works to join them. Local organisers had advised against striking that day because they thought the moment was not right, but the women ignored them. Around ninety thousand workers were on strike by the end of the day, and crowds shouted for bread.

Over the next two days the strike spread across the city until it became a general strike. Students and middle-class residents joined the crowds, and the slogans changed from 'Bread' to 'Down with the war' and 'Down with the '. Nicholas, at army headquarters in Mogilev, ordered the commander in Petrograd to end the disorders by force. On 26 February soldiers fired on crowds and killed dozens of people.

The turning point came on 27 February. The soldiers of the Petrograd garrison were mostly young peasant recruits and older reservists, and many of them had relatives in the crowds. Starting with the Volhynian Regiment, unit after unit refused to fire, killed or chased away their officers, and joined the demonstrators. Once the army went over to the people, the government had nothing left. Ministers fled or were arrested.

Two new authorities formed on the same day, in two wings of the same building, the Tauride Palace. Members of the created a committee that became the . Socialist activists, mostly and , revived the 1905 model and created the of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies. On 1 March the issued its , which told soldiers to elect committees and obey only those orders that did not contradict the . It badly weakened officers' authority across the army.

Generals and leaders persuaded Nicholas that only his abdication could keep the army fighting. On 2 March 1917 he abdicated for himself and his son. His brother Grand Duke Michael declined the throne the next day. After more than three hundred years, the Romanov monarchy had ended in less than a week, with surprisingly little bloodshed outside Petrograd.

Dual power

Why the Provisional Government failed

The was led first by Prince , a liberal known for his work in local government, with the Kadet historian as foreign minister and the lawyer as minister of justice. It quickly introduced freedoms Russia had never had: freedom of speech, press and assembly, an amnesty for political prisoners, the end of religious and ethnic discrimination in law, and the abolition of the death penalty. It promised that a elected by universal suffrage would decide the country's permanent constitution.

Real power, however, was shared with the , which controlled the railways, the telegraph and the loyalty of the soldiers. Lenin later called this situation 'dual power'. of workers, soldiers and peasants sprang up across the country, along with factory committees and village assemblies. Many people expected the revolution to bring immediate change in their own lives: land for peasants, an eight-hour day and control over factories for workers, peace for soldiers, and self-government for Finns, Ukrainians and other nationalities.

The delayed on almost all of these. It said that land reform and the national question must wait for the , and it kept postponing the elections. Most importantly, it kept Russia in the war. In April a note by Milyukov assuring the Allies that Russia would fight to victory caused street protests and forced him to resign. Socialists from the then joined a coalition government. In June Kerensky, now minister of war, launched a major offensive that collapsed within weeks, and desertion grew rapidly.

Lenin had returned to Petrograd on 3 April after the German government allowed him and other exiles to cross Germany in a , hoping they would weaken Russia's war effort. At the and in his he called for no support for the , an end to the war, and 'all power to the '. At first even many thought this was too radical, but his simple slogans of 'peace, land and bread' fitted the mood of the cities and the army better than any other party's programme.

In early July soldiers and workers held armed demonstrations in Petrograd demanding that the take power. The failed. The government published claims that Lenin was a German agent, arrested several leaders including Trotsky, and Lenin fled to Finland. Kerensky became prime minister. The seemed beaten.

They were rescued by the . In late August the army's commander-in-chief, General , sent troops towards Petrograd. Whether he meant to overthrow Kerensky or believed he was acting with Kerensky's approval is still debated. Kerensky turned to the for help, armed workers' militias, the , and released prisoners. Railway workers stopped the troop trains and Kornilov's advance dissolved. Afterwards the right blamed Kerensky for betraying Kornilov and the left suspected him of plotting with him. The , who had been at the front of the defence, won majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow in September, and Trotsky became chairman of the .

October

How the Bolsheviks took power

From Finland, Lenin wrote letters insisting that the must seize power at once, before the elections and before Kerensky could recover. On 10 the Central Committee voted for an armed uprising. Two senior members, and , opposed it and even made their objections public in a newspaper, so the plan was hardly a secret.

The uprising was organised through the 's , chaired formally by an but run largely by Trotsky. Using the defence of the capital as its justification, the committee won the loyalty of most of the garrison. On the night of 24–25 its forces took bridges, railway stations, the telephone exchange and the state bank with very little fighting. On the morning of 25 Lenin issued a proclamation that the had been overthrown, although its ministers were still sitting in the . Kerensky had already left the city to look for loyal troops.

The fell in the early hours of 26 . The fired a blank shot as a signal, and some shells were fired from the Peter and Paul Fortress. The palace was defended by military cadets, Cossacks and part of a women's battalion, most of whom drifted away during the evening. The attackers entered and arrested the ministers. Only a few people were killed. The great storming of the that most people picture comes largely from 's film , made for the tenth anniversary in 1927, and from mass re-enactments staged in 1920.

At the same time the Second All-Russian Congress of was meeting at the . Most and right walked out in protest against the seizure of power, which left the and the in control. The congress approved a new government, the , with Lenin as chairman, Trotsky in charge of foreign affairs and in charge of nationalities. It passed a calling for an end to the war without annexations and a that turned landlords' estates over to peasant committees, essentially recognising seizures that peasants were already carrying out.

Moscow fell only after about a week of fighting. Elections to the went ahead in November, and they showed the limits of support. The , strongest among the peasants, won the most votes, close to forty percent, while the won about a quarter, mostly in the cities and the army. When the Assembly met on 5 January 1918 and refused to accept power, closed it the next day and it never met again. Women and the revolution

The vote, the family code and the Zhenotdel

Women started the , and the question of what the revolution would do for women ran through all of 1917. Russia had an organised women's rights movement before the war. Its liberal wing, including the , had campaigned for the vote since 1905. When the 's first plans for the elections did not clearly include women, the League organised a large march to the Tauride Palace on 19 March 1917. The marchers, numbering in the tens of thousands, refused to leave until leaders of both the government and the promised support. During the summer the confirmed the right of women over twenty to vote and stand for office, and Russia became the first great power to give women equal voting rights.

Socialist women had a different emphasis. Kollontai argued that legal equality meant little for a working woman who still did all the housework and childcare and earned less than a man. In her view, women would only be free when society took over cooking, laundry and childcare through public canteens, laundries and nurseries, and when marriage was based on free choice rather than economic dependence. Some of these arguments return decades later in the debates covered in the lesson on Wages for Housework.

The new government moved quickly on law. Decrees in December 1917 and a family code in 1918 replaced church marriage with civil registration, made divorce easy to obtain for either partner, abolished the legal category of illegitimate children, and declared men and women equal before the law. In November 1920 Russia became the first country in the world to legalise abortion on request, performed by doctors in hospitals. In 1919 the party created the , a women's department headed first by and then by Kollontai, which sent organisers into factories and villages, ran literacy classes and recruited women into local . In Central Asia its activists campaigned against veiling, sometimes at great risk.

The results were uneven. The and famine destroyed the money needed for canteens and nurseries, and easy divorce often left women raising children alone with little support. Many male party members saw women's issues as a distraction. Stalin closed the in 1930, and in 1936 abortion was banned again, except for medical reasons, as the regime turned to promoting large families. The early experiment nevertheless influenced feminist and socialist thinking worldwide, and the state made 8 March an official holiday, which helped fix on that date.

A story

Kollontai, the first woman commissar

was born in 1872 into a wealthy noble family in St Petersburg. As a young woman she married an engineer and had a son, but a visit to a huge textile factory near Narva, where she saw how the workers lived, changed her direction. She left her marriage, studied economics in Zurich, and became a Marxist writer. During 1905 she began organising working women and argued with liberal feminists, whom she thought ignored class.

She spent the years from 1908 to 1917 in exile, travelling and speaking across Europe and the United States. She helped promote the idea of an at the socialist women's conference in Copenhagen in 1910, which proposed. When the broke out on that very day, she was in Norway. She returned to Petrograd in March 1917, joined Lenin in supporting the , and was arrested after the .

After she became People's Commissar of Social Welfare. Her ministry was responsible for pensions, the disabled, orphans and maternity care, and she worked on plans for state support for mothers before and after childbirth. Her clash with the Orthodox Church over the Alexander Nevsky Monastery, which she tried to take over for a home for disabled veterans, caused a public outcry. She resigned in March 1918 in protest against the .

In the early 1920s she wrote essays and stories about love and sexuality, such as 'Make Way for Winged Eros' and the collection , which made her famous and also made her an easy target for critics who accused her of preaching free love. She supported the inside the party in 1921, which argued for more power for trade unions. That cost her influence. From 1923 she was sent abroad as a diplomat, to Norway, Mexico and Sweden, where she served as envoy and helped negotiate the end of the war between the Union and Finland in 1944. Diplomacy kept her far from Moscow during Stalin's purges, which killed many of her old comrades. She died in Moscow in 1952.

Elsewhere

1917 around the world

The happened in the middle of a world war, and its effects spread quickly. Looking at what else was happening that year shows how many parts of the world were being reshaped at the same time.

  • China: The Republic created by the Xinhai Revolution was falling apart. In July 1917 the general Zhang Xun briefly restored the last emperor, Puyi, for about twelve days. In August the Beijing government declared war on Germany, and Sun Yat-sen set up a rival government in Guangzhou. News of the revolution reached Chinese intellectuals such as , who wrote about it in 1918. Together with anger at the Paris peace settlement, it fed the May Fourth Movement of 1919, and advisers helped found the Chinese Communist Party in 1921.
  • Japan: Japan joined the Allied intervention in Siberia in 1918 and kept troops there until 1922, longer than any other power. In the summer of 1918 the spread across Japan as prices soared, bringing down the government and leading to the first party cabinet under Hara Takashi.
  • Taiwan: Taiwan was a Japanese colony. After the armed Tapani (Xilai'an) uprising of 1915 was crushed, Taiwanese resistance turned to political and cultural organising. The , founded in 1921, and later leftist groups were influenced by the ideas of national self-determination and socialism that spread after 1917.
  • United States and Europe: The United States entered the war in April 1917. Britain issued the in November 1917, supporting a 'national home for the Jewish people' in Palestine. In 1918 British women over thirty won the vote, and German women won it after the German revolution of November 1918, which was partly inspired by events in Russia.
  • Mexico: Mexico adopted a new constitution in February 1917, during its own revolution. It included land reform, workers' rights and limits on the church, and it was in some ways more radical on social rights than anything in Europe at the time.

Compared with the Paris Commune of 1871, covered in an earlier lesson, the drew a clear conclusion: the Commune had failed, Lenin believed, because it had been too hesitant and too democratic in dealing with its enemies. Compared with the Xinhai Revolution of 1911, Russia's revolution went much further, destroying not only the monarchy but the old landowning class and private industry. Chinese revolutionaries of both the Nationalist and Communist parties would study the model of the disciplined party closely.

After

Civil war and the one-party state

The had promised peace, and they got it at a high price. Under the , signed on 3 March 1918, Russia gave up Poland, the Baltic lands, Finland and Ukraine, with a large share of the empire's population, industry and farmland. The left the government in protest. The government moved the capital from Petrograd to Moscow.

A followed from 1918 to about 1921. The , led by former tsarist officers such as and Admiral , fought the that Trotsky built from scratch. Britain, France, the United States, Japan and others sent troops, though in limited numbers. There were also Ukrainian, Polish, Finnish, Baltic and Caucasian independence movements, peasant 'Green' armies and the anarchist forces of . In July 1918 the local in Yekaterinburg shot , Alexandra, their five children and four servants. Both sides used terror, and after an attempt on Lenin's life in August 1918 the launched the 'Red Terror'. Grain requisitioning under 'war communism' set off peasant revolts and contributed to the famine of 1921–22. Estimates of the total number of deaths from fighting, terror, hunger and epidemics in these years run into several million.

The won, largely because they held the industrial centre and the railways, were united, and could present themselves as defenders against foreign invaders and the return of landlords. But by 1921 the economy had collapsed. In March 1921 the sailors of , once heroes of 1917, rebelled and demanded without domination. The revolt was crushed. At the same party congress Lenin introduced the , which allowed peasants to sell their grain and small businesses to operate, and banned organised factions inside the party. The Union was formally created in December 1922. After Lenin's death in January 1924, Stalin outmanoeuvred Trotsky and his other rivals and by 1929 had begun forced collectivisation and a far more brutal dictatorship.

Analysis

Revolution from below or coup from above?

Historians have argued about 1917 since it happened. During the Cold War, many Western historians, such as , described as a coup by a small, conspiratorial party that hijacked a democratic revolution, and saw Stalin's terror as growing directly from Lenin's methods. Official history described it as the inevitable victory of the working class led by its party.

From the 1970s, social historians such as , and S. A. Smith studied factories, regiments and villages, and argued that the came to power because they expressed what millions of workers, soldiers and peasants actually wanted in the autumn of 1917, even if most of those people did not want a one-party dictatorship. Rabinowitch showed that the party in Petrograd was much less disciplined and more responsive to its members than the old picture suggested. Many historians now hold both views at once: rested on real popular support for 'all power to the ', and the then used that support to build a state that silenced the themselves.

For someone thinking about social movements today, 1917 offers several lessons. It shows how fast an apparently powerful state can collapse when its army and its own officials stop believing in it, and how a group that offers simple answers to urgent needs can overtake broader coalitions that keep postponing decisions. It also shows how promises made to women, workers and peasants can be undone when a movement takes power without institutions that let those people hold it to account.

Fun fact

The October Revolution happened in November

Because Russia used the until February 1918, the 'February' Revolution began on 8 March and the 'October' Revolution took place on 7 November by the Western calendar. After the government switched calendars, the anniversary of was celebrated every year on 7 November, with parades on Red Square. The same calendar gap explains why the women's strike of 23 February 1917 fell on , 8 March.
