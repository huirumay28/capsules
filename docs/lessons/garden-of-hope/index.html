Before

Before Garden of Hope: girls for sale on Huaxi Street

In the 1980s in Taipei's Wanhua district was one of the city's best-known red-light districts. Reports from church workers and journalists described girls in their early teens, many from Indigenous mountain villages, who had been sold or tricked into brothels by traffickers. Protesters accused the police and the government of tolerating the trade.

On 10 January 1987 more than thirty civic groups marched through and . Chinese Wikipedia lists the conveners as ; , president of the ; and , secretary-general of the . The march leader was the Atayal activist (), vice-chair of the . In August 1987 Tsao, Shen and Liao also helped found the , today the (), one of the nine organizations on your orgs page. Their slogans attacked the customers rather than the girls: 'buying a child prostitute is a crime', and 'the customer is more shameful than the prostitute'. A second march followed on 9 January 1988 because, the organizers said, a police crackdown announced after the first march had changed little.

Historians of the Taiwan women's movement treat these marches as a turning point. Before them, newspapers mostly reported on women's groups negatively. The child-prostitution campaign was the first women's cause that won broad public sympathy, and it pushed the government toward new child-welfare laws. ended in July 1987, between the two marches, which made street protest far less dangerous than it had been when was jailed after the in 1979.

Founding

1988: a house for girls who had nowhere to go

Rescuing a girl from a brothel solved only half the problem. Many of the girls could not go home, because home was where they had been sold. was founded in 1988 to answer that second question. The 's own history says that a group of Christians who were worried about the trafficking of girls set up Taiwan's first , called , to give rescued girls a place to live. The important shift is the one from rescue to services. An organization that began with a few girls in one house now handles domestic violence, sexual violence, teenage pregnancy, family court support, migrant women and stalking. It is no longer a protest group in the way that the is. It is a large service provider that also lobbies.

What it does

What Garden of Hope does today

According to its About page, has more than fifty service sites in fifteen cities and counties, from Taipei to Taitung and Hualien. Its 2025 annual report, published in April 2026, gives these figures for the year 2025.

  • 20,110 children, young people and women served, and 213,565 service sessions, which include social work, legal advice, medical and counseling.
  • Domestic and intimate-partner violence services in eleven branch offices under government contracts, three government-commissioned emergency short-term shelters, and family-violence service desks inside seven district courts.
  • Sexual violence services, including counseling for survivors and with removing sexual images from the internet.
  • Residential care for girls who cannot live at home: five girls' homes in Taipei, New Taipei, Taichung, Nantou and Kaohsiung, which served 54 girls in 2025; the report says 16 girls completed the service during the year.
  • Economic empowerment for women. Among women in its economic programme, the report says average annual income rose from NT$62,247 when they joined to NT$182,339 when their case closed, and 52 percent reached stable employment.
  • 151 advocacy actions (meetings, press conferences and visits to legislators) and 1,327 public education talks in schools, companies and government offices.
  • An average of 549 paid staff during the year.

These outcome numbers are reported by the about its own clients, so they are self-assessment rather than independent evaluation. They are still useful, because a partner that measures and publishes its results is easier to hold to account than one that only tells stories.

Its current advocacy, as listed in the 2025 report, includes abolishing the statute of limitations for sexual offences against children, strengthening the after three years in force (for example by adding an emergency protection order), and a legal-support team for survivors who were sued for defamation after speaking out in Taiwan's 2023 wave.

Two things stand out. First, almost half of the money comes from the government. In Taiwan, local governments often contract out protective services such as shelters and domestic-violence case management to private foundations. This gives stable funding and a large footprint, but it also means that a lot of its work is defined by government contracts, and contract rates do not always cover the real cost. In 2017, for example, joined a press conference arguing that the new 'one fixed day off and one flexible rest day' labour rule () raised costs for social-welfare groups without matching increases in government funding; some front-line social workers answered that managers were asking underpaid staff to sacrifice in the name of charity. Second, donations are the other big pillar, which is exactly where a partner like would fit.

The share of about 18 percent sounds high to many donors, but a large service organization needs accountants, auditors, IT and training, and the legal cost cap described below applies to each permitted fundraising appeal, not to the organization's whole budget. A better question than 'how low is the overhead' is whether money given for a specific project is spent on that project and reported publicly. Taiwan's laws make that question answerable.

Transparency

How Taiwan's charity laws let you check a partner

Two laws matter most for anyone who wants to give money to a Taiwanese women's organization: the of 2006 () and the of 2018 (). is a (), so both apply to it.

The governs public fundraising appeals. Its main rules are these:

  • Who may run a public appeal (Article 5): public schools, administrative corporations, public-interest associations and foundations. Other people and companies cannot launch one themselves.
  • Permit (Article 7): each appeal needs a permit from the local government, or from the if it covers more than one city or county, and it may last at most one year (Article 12).
  • Receipts (Article 16): every receipt must show the permit number, the donor, the amount and the date.
  • Cost cap (Article 17): the costs of running an appeal may be paid from the money raised only up to 15 percent if it raises NT$10 million or less, with lower percentages above that.
  • Reporting (Articles 18 and 20): within thirty days after the appeal ends, the group must publish the donors and the amount raised, and within thirty days after the money is spent it must publish how it was used, after board approval, and file the report with the regulator.
  • Purpose (Article 19): money raised must be used according to the approved plan and may not be moved to other purposes.

The covers the organization as a whole. Article 24 requires larger foundations to have internal controls and audited financial statements and to adopt an integrity code. Article 25 requires every to send its annual work plan and budget to the regulator within a month of the start of the year, and its work report and financial statements within five months of the end of the year, and to publish them within a month after the regulator receives them, together with a list of grants and donations received. 's About page links to exactly these documents for 2023 to 2026, including an accountant's audit report, and its donation-verification page () lists donors, in-kind gifts and the results of each permitted appeal.

For this has a practical meaning. A shop that sells clothes and gives part of its proceeds to a is normally acting as a donor, not running its own public appeal, but how words its promises to customers still matters. Before launching, it is worth asking the partner how it will receipt the donation, whether it will appear in the partner's published donor list, and how it will report what the money paid for.

Elsewhere

What was happening elsewhere at the same time

was part of a wave of East and Southeast Asian groups in the 1980s that turned the trafficking of women and girls into a public issue.

  • Japan, 1986. The opened () in Tokyo to mark its centenary, mainly to shelter foreign women who were victims of human trafficking. Like it came from a Christian women's tradition and moved from rescue to a broader shelter for women and children.
  • South Korea, 1983. The was founded at the start of the Korean women's movement. It runs counseling centres and shelters for survivors of domestic and sexual violence but describes itself as a movement organization, closer in spirit to Taiwan's than to a service provider.
  • Thailand, 1990. Researchers and activists in Bangkok launched () as a three-year campaign against child sex tourism. It grew into a global network of more than a hundred member organizations, and in 1996 it co-organized the first in Stockholm.

The comparison shows two paths. Some groups stayed small and political, while others, like and , became service institutions that depend on government contracts. Both paths are legitimate, but they suit different kinds of partnership. A movement group may welcome money for campaigns, while a service provider can show you exactly how many shelter nights a donation paid for.

Garden of Hope as a possible partner

Here is a plain summary of what this lesson suggests about as a partner.

  • Strengths: a thirty-eight-year record, national reach, audited accounts, detailed public reports, and services such as shelters and job training whose results can be counted.
  • Things to weigh: its scale means a small shop's donation would be a very small share of its NT$270 million in annual donations, so the story you can tell customers will be about joining a large effort rather than funding something new. Its Christian identity and its position against legalizing prostitution may or may not match 's values.
  • Questions to ask: Is there a specific project, such as the economic empowerment programme or the girls' homes, that a donation can be earmarked for? Will the donation be listed in the published donor records? Does it already have a secondhand or resale partnership model? It runs material-donation drives and its own shops, so a clothing link may be natural.

Fun fact

Daisies instead of child prostitutes
