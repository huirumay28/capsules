The concept

What pluralistic ignorance means

is a situation in which most members of a group privately reject a belief or a norm, but each of them wrongly assumes that most of the others accept it. Because everyone thinks they are in the minority, few people say what they really think, and many go along with the norm in public. The result is a norm that is followed by many people and supported by few.

The important point is that the mistake is about other people's minds, not about one's own. A student who privately thinks heavy drinking is unpleasant is not confused about their own view. They are confused about how common that view is. Psychologists sometimes describe this as an error in 'second-order beliefs', which means beliefs about what other people believe.

The error tends to keep itself going. If I hide my doubts, other people see me going along and conclude that I support the norm. They hide their doubts too, and I see them going along. Every person's public behavior becomes false evidence for everyone else. This is why a norm can last long after private opinion has moved on, a delay that researchers call the .

The concept is related to, but different from, several other ideas. The is the opposite error, in which people assume that others agree with them more than they really do. The , a theory from the German researcher , describes how people who think their opinion is unpopular stay quiet, which makes it look even less popular. and the bystander effect are often described as consequences of : in a meeting, each doubter sees the others nodding, and in an emergency, each bystander sees the others standing still and concludes that nothing is wrong.

Where the idea came from

From Allport's students to the segregation surveys

The American psychologist first wrote about the problem in 1924. He was interested in why people follow social rules that they do not personally endorse. His answer was that people usually act on their own convictions only when they believe those convictions are shared. In 1931 and his student used the term in , a report on a large survey of Syracuse University students.

Much of the later work came from sociologists and public opinion researchers rather than laboratory psychologists, because the phenomenon is easiest to see in surveys that ask people both what they think and what they believe others think. A well-known example is 's 1975 paper in Public Opinion Quarterly. Using survey data from the late 1960s, he showed that white Americans substantially overestimated how many other white Americans supported racial segregation. Private attitudes had shifted faster than people realized, and the mistaken picture of majority opinion helped keep segregationist norms in place.

In 1987 and gave the idea an experimental basis. In their studies, people who held back because they were afraid of looking foolish assumed that others who held back had a different, more confident reason. A familiar version is the classroom: a student who does not understand the lecture stays silent out of embarrassment, sees that nobody else asks a question, and concludes that everyone else understood.

Case study

Drinking at Princeton: Prentice and Miller, 1993

The best-known study of was published in 1993 by and in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, under the title 'Pluralistic ignorance and alcohol use on campus: Some consequences of misperceiving the social norm.' Drinking was a central part of social life at Princeton, and and suspected that many students were less enthusiastic about it than they appeared.

They asked students how comfortable they personally were with drinking habits on campus, and how comfortable they thought the average Princeton student was. On average, students rated their own comfort lower than the comfort they attributed to the typical student. In other words, most students thought they were more uneasy than their peers, when in fact their peers felt much the same way.

The researchers then followed students over a semester. Among men, private attitudes gradually moved toward the norm they believed existed. The men came to feel more comfortable with drinking, which is what theory would predict when people repeatedly behave in a way that does not match their attitudes. Women showed no similar shift. and also found that students who felt they deviated from the drinking norm reported more alienation from campus life, even though the deviance was mostly imagined.

The study had practical influence. It supported the 'social norms' approach to campus alcohol programs, in which universities publish survey results showing students how much their peers actually drink and how they actually feel about it. The idea is that correcting the false picture of the norm removes some of the pressure to follow it. The results of these campaigns have been mixed, and researchers still debate how well they work.

Second case

Women working outside the home in Saudi Arabia

A 2020 study in the American Economic Review by , and tested whether correcting changes real decisions. They surveyed young married men in Saudi Arabia. The large majority privately supported women working outside the home, but the men greatly underestimated how many other men like them shared that view.

The researchers then told some of the men the true level of support among their peers. Men who received this information were more likely to help their wives obtain jobs, for example by signing them up for a job-matching service. The study is valuable because it moves beyond attitudes on a questionnaire and shows that a mistaken belief about other people's views can hold back behavior, and that correcting it can release that behavior.

Why it matters

What the concept helps explain

helps explain why social change can seem to happen suddenly. When private opinion has shifted but public behavior has not, a single visible act, a published survey, or a person who speaks first can reveal that the supposed majority was never there. Researchers have used the idea to discuss the collapse of support for unpopular regimes, changing attitudes toward race and gender, and present-day issues such as climate change, where surveys suggest that many people who want stronger action believe they are in the minority.

The concept also has limits. It describes a gap between private views and perceived norms, but it does not by itself explain how that gap formed. Measuring private attitudes is difficult, because people may not report them honestly even in anonymous surveys. And not every unpopular-looking norm is held in place by misperception; sometimes people stay quiet because speaking up carries real penalties, not just imagined ones.

Something to think about

A question for you

Think of a habit or rule in a group you belong to, such as a workplace, a class, or a family. Do you suspect that more people privately dislike it than anyone admits? What would it take for someone to find out?
