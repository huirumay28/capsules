Before

Wollstonecraft, Austen and a law that erased wives

To understand why and mattered, start with the legal position of a woman in early Victorian England. Under the common-law doctrine of , a married woman had no separate legal identity. Her property, her earnings and, until the Custody of Infants Act of 1839, her children belonged to her husband. Divorce required a private Act of Parliament and was almost impossible for a wife to obtain. Middle-class women were expected to marry; if they did not, one of the few respectable jobs open to them was to become a , living in another family's house, neither servant nor equal.

Two earlier writers framed the problem. 's (1792) argued that women appeared weak and frivolous because they were trained to be ornaments rather than educated to think. , publishing anonymously between 1811 and 1817, wrote comedies of courtship in which money and marriage decide a woman's whole future. Her heroines are witty and clear-eyed, but her novels mostly stay in the drawing room and end at the wedding.

The novel itself was growing fast. Circulating libraries such as 's, founded in 1842, bought novels in expensive three-volume editions and rented them to middle-class subscribers, which made the novel a family product that had to be fit for daughters to read. Women had always written novels, but critics often treated 'lady novelists' as producers of light entertainment. This is the world into which three sisters from Yorkshire sent their manuscripts under men's names.

Deep dive

George Eliot: the most learned woman in England writes under a man's name

(1819–1880) grew up in Warwickshire, the daughter of an estate manager. As a young woman she lost her evangelical faith, which caused a long quarrel with her father. She translated David Friedrich Strauss's The Life of Jesus from German (1846), moved to London and became, in practice, the editor of the radical in the early 1850s, though her name did not appear. In 1854 she began living with the writer , who was married and could not obtain a divorce, because he had accepted his wife's children by another man as his own. Respectable society shunned her for years.

In 1856 she published an essay in the called 'Silly Novels by Lady Novelists', mocking fiction in which brilliant, beautiful heroines dazzle everyone. Her complaint was not that women wrote novels, but that bad ones gave critics an excuse to dismiss all women. When she began writing fiction herself she chose the , partly to be judged seriously and partly to protect her work from the scandal of her private life. Critics worried that such books, many written by women, were corrupting young female readers, and the very anxiety shows how much the domestic novel had become a space for questioning marriage.
