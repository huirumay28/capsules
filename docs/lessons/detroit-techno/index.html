Before

Before techno: Motor City, funk and a radio show from space

Detroit was the capital of the American car industry and one of the richest cities in the United States in the middle of the twentieth century. Its population peaked at about 1.85 million in 1950. From the 1950s onward, factories moved to the suburbs and to other states, automation replaced many assembly-line jobs, and white families left for the suburbs. The of 1967, one of the deadliest and costliest civil disturbances in American history, sped up that departure. By the 1970s Detroit had a Black majority and a shrinking tax base, and by 2020 its population had fallen to about 639,000.

Detroit already had a world-famous musical history. had turned local Black singers into international stars in the 1960s before moving to Los Angeles in the early 1970s, and 's and Funkadelic had built a whole science-fiction mythology around funk, complete with a stage-prop spaceship called the Mothership. Disco and then Chicago house showed that a DJ playing records for a dancing crowd could become a creative art of its own (see the disco and Chicago house lessons).

The most important single influence on the future producers was a radio DJ. , known on air as the , started broadcasting on WGPR in 1977 and moved to WJLB around 1982, where billboards announced the 'Landing of the Mothership' at 10 p.m. every night. Mojo ignored radio formats. He played entire albums without interruption, hours of Prince including deep cuts and B-sides, and put German electronic music by next to funk, new wave and rock. , , , , and have all named him as an influence.

Origins

The Belleville Three

was born in Detroit on 12 September 1962. His father was a concert promoter, and Atkins played guitar and bass in funk bands as a boy. When his parents separated he moved to his grandmother's house in Belleville, a small, mostly white town about 30 miles from downtown Detroit. At 15 or 16 he persuaded his grandmother to buy him a Korg MS-10 synthesizer, after hearing what did with keyboards. At school he met two younger boys: , born in 1963 and raised by a single mother, and , born in Brooklyn in 1964, who had moved to Belleville as a child.

The three became known as the . Atkins was the first to learn to mix records, and he taught the other two. They listened to an unusual mix of records: , , , Bootsy Collins, Prince, Depeche Mode and the B-52's. May later said that the suburban setting changed how they heard it: 'We'd sit back with the lights off and listen to records by Bootsy and . We never took it as just entertainment, we took it as a serious philosophy.' Atkins and May DJed at parties under the name Deep Space Soundworks, and by 1981 Mojo was playing their mixes on the radio.

Their scene had a class dimension that later writers have examined closely. Early grew out of 'prep' parties thrown by the children of Black families who had prospered from the car industry. The parties had European-sounding names such as Plush, Charivari and GQ Productions, enforced dress codes, and sometimes put up signs saying 'No Jits', a slang insult for poorer inner-city youths. began, in other words, as the music of a Black middle class that was looking to Europe and the future rather than to the street.

Deep dive

Juan Atkins, Cybotron and the word 'techno'

At Washtenaw Community College Atkins met , who recorded as , and in 1980 they formed . They released their first single, , on their own Deep Space label in 1981, and it sold about 15,000 copies, mostly in Detroit. Two more singles, Cosmic Cars and , persuaded the California label Fantasy to sign them, and their album came out in 1983. , a cold, precise electro track built on drum machine and synthesizers, is still regarded as one of the foundational records of Detroit electronic music.

The ideas behind the music came from the futurist writer , whose book Atkins first read as the textbook for a high-school course called Future Studies. (1970) and (1980) argued that industrial society was ending and an information society was beginning, and described 'techno-rebels' who would use the new technology for their own ends. Atkins said that Detroit was the most advanced city in the world in the transition away from industrialism, so it was the natural place for music about what came next.

The duo split in 1985 over creative differences. Atkins founded his own label, , and began recording as . Writers later gave the three friends nicknames: Atkins the Originator, May the Innovator and Saunderson the Elevator.

Atkins died on 31 October 2026 at the age of 64. He was found unresponsive in a hotel room in Mexico City before a scheduled performance at the YuYu Cine Club and was pronounced dead at a hospital; his agency, Greensmith Artists, announced the news the next day. He had been touring until the end and had played in Amsterdam on 23 October. Detroit's mayor, Mary Sheffield, said he 'gave the world a new sound. As a teenager with a synthesiser and a big idea, he imagined what the music of the future could be. Then he built it.'

The tools

Cheap Japanese machines that failed in the shops

depended on a set of instruments that their makers considered commercial failures. The Japanese company Roland released the TR-808 drum machine in 1980. It was one of the first drum machines that let users program their own rhythms, but its analog sounds did not resemble real drums, and professional musicians mostly rejected it. Roland built only about 12,000 before stopping production in 1983 and replaced it with the TR-909. The TB-303 Bass Line of 1981, designed by Tadao Kikumoto to replace a bass guitarist, sounded nothing like a bass guitar and was discontinued in 1984.

Those failures were good news for young musicians in Detroit and Chicago, because the machines soon appeared cheaply in second-hand shops. The 's booming bass drum became central to electro and hip-hop (see the hip-hop origins lesson), the became the standard and house drum machine, and the squelching sound of a being twisted beyond its intended use became acid house. A teenager in a basement with a drum machine, a synthesizer and a four-track recorder could now make a record without a band or a studio.

This is also a good example of why connected so easily with the rest of the world. Its producers were Black Americans using Japanese instruments to reinterpret German electronic music, and they were inspired as much by in Tokyo as by in Düsseldorf (see the krautrock lesson). May once described as 'like and caught in an elevator, with only a sequencer to keep them company.'

Second wave

Back home, needed a club. In 1988 , Alton Miller and George Baker opened the at 1315 Broadway in downtown Detroit, modelled on Chicago's house clubs. May, Atkins and Saunderson played there with and , and the club turned a scattered scene into what its regulars called a family. May remembered 'a young beautiful black crowd' and added, 'We had white kids coming, Spanish kids coming, gay kids coming, straight kids coming.' Analysis

What Detroit techno was really about

was music about the future made in a city that many Americans saw as having no future. The car factories that had employed its founders' parents were being automated, and robots were taking the jobs. The producers' answer was to take the same kind of technology and use it themselves. One writer described the process as taking technology and making it 'a black secret'. That is why is often discussed as part of , the tradition, running from Sun Ra and to , of Black artists using science fiction to imagine a different relationship with history and power.

It was also a music that wanted to escape racial labels, and that desire was complicated. Atkins said, 'to me it shouldn't be white or black music, it should be just music.' Yet the music was largely invisible in its own country. Detroit producers found their audiences in Britain, Germany, Belgium and Japan, while many Americans learned about only years later through European artists. said in 1993 that American artists sent their music to Europe because it was accepted there by a wider audience. When electronic dance music became a huge commercial industry in the 2010s, many of its best-known stars were white Europeans and Americans, and the Black Detroit origins were often overlooked.

Finally, the first and second waves represent two different politics. The first wave was aspirational and European-looking, born in suburban . The second wave, especially , spoke from the perspective of the struggling , treated independent record labels as a form of economic self-defence, and turned the music into protest. Both were responses to the same collapse of the industrial city.

Elsewhere

What was happening elsewhere at the same time

In Chicago, a few hours' drive away, house music was being born in the same years from the same ingredients: disco records, drum machines and a mostly Black and gay club audience (see the Chicago house lesson). The learned from Chicago, and British journalists at first described as Detroit's high-tech version of house.

In Britain, 1988 and 1989 brought acid house and the rave era, and by late 1988 the tabloid The Sun had turned from selling smiley T-shirts to running scare stories about drugs, a classic moral panic (see the moral panic lesson). After Detroit: from rave to festival stage

Fun fact

A record label named after a turntable slider

and John Acquaviva, working across the river in Windsor, Ontario, named their label after the pitch control on a Technics turntable, which lets a DJ speed a record up by at most 8 percent. They saw the label as a friendly rival to and kept pushing their tracks faster and harder, as if they were always playing with the slider pushed to the top.
