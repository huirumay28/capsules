Before

The machine the third wave used, and what a different machine could do

The Third Wave lesson in this archive ends in the early 2010s, when third wave was already a heading in a syllabus. That lesson notes that hashtag campaigns used a different machine from a photocopied zine, a C-SPAN weekend, or a UN platform. This lesson is about that different machine. It is a description of how digital infrastructure—smartphones, Twitter, Facebook, Instagram, later TikTok—changed what feminism could look like in public, what speed meant, what a crowd was, and who could call one.

By 2010 the machine had changed. Facebook had 608 million users. Twitter had 54 million. Smartphones put both platforms in people pockets. A photograph taken at a protest in one city could be in another city feed before the march ended. A phrase like , which had been using since 2006 in community work in Selma, Alabama, could become a global hashtag in October 2017 in the hours after an actress tweeted it. The speed was new. The platform was new. The problem—men using power to assault and silence women—was not.

The era

A police officer, a website, a bus, two investigations, and a march

On 24 January 2011 Toronto Police Constable Michael Sanguinetti spoke at a York University campus safety forum. Sanguinetti said, according to reports, women should avoid dressing like sluts in order not to be victimized. The comment was reported in the university newspaper. Heather Jarvis and Sonya Barnett organized a march for 3 April 2011. They called it . Three thousand people showed up in Toronto. Within months, sister walks took place in cities worldwide.

On 16 April 2012 Laura Bates, a British writer, launched the . She created a website where people could share experiences of sexism. She hoped for 100 stories. By April 2015, the site had logged 100,000 entries. Branches opened in 25 countries.

On 16 December 2012 Jyoti Singh, a 23-year-old physiotherapy student, boarded a private bus in Delhi. The bus occupants gang-raped her. She died from her injuries on 29 December. Protests erupted at India Gate in New Delhi. The Indian government formed the Justice J.S. Verma Committee. Parliament passed the Criminal Law Amendment Act, 2013.

On 11 May 2015 the body of Chiara Páez, a 14-year-old girl from Rufino, Argentina, was found buried. She had been pregnant. Her boyfriend, 16, confessed. Journalist Marcela Ojeda tweeted. Within days, activists organized a mass protest for 3 June 2015 under the hashtag NiUnaMenos—Not One Less. Over 200,000 people gathered in Buenos Aires.

On 21 January 2017, the day after Donald Trump inauguration, an estimated 500,000 people gathered in Washington, D.C., for the . Millions more marched in over 600 sister events worldwide. It was, at the time, the largest single-day protest in U.S. history.

On 5 October 2017 and published an investigation in the New York Times: had sexually harassed and assaulted women for decades. On 10 October published a second investigation in The New Yorker. On 15 October actress Alyssa Milano tweeted. Within 24 hours, the phrase appeared in 12 million posts. Milano had not known that had been using the phrase since 2006. Black women on Twitter credited Burke. The hashtag became a global cascade.

Elsewhere

Ni Una Menos was Argentine; Delhi was Indian; none were American exports

The hashtag NiUnaMenos went viral in Argentina two years before went global. The movement had a name, a date, and demands before the Weinstein reporting.

The December 2012 Delhi case is an Indian fact. Jyoti Singh was an Indian student. The protests were at India Gate. The Criminal Law Amendment Act, 2013 is an Indian statute. The protests forced legal reform. This was not waiting for a cue from New York or Los Angeles.

After

What a hashtag does not do

By 2026 the fourth-wave label is a classroom heading. The cascade has receded. Some of what it left behind: Weinstein in prison. Legal Defense Fund still operating. movement continuing as it always was: community-based, survivor-centered, not a hashtag. The still collecting stories.

What the hashtag did: it made the private public at a scale and speed that was new. The pattern was undeniable. It changed what could be said in workplaces. It did not end rape or harassment. It did not close the gender wage gap. Visibility is not the same as power. A cascade is not a revolution. Sometimes it becomes one. Sometimes it is just a flood that recedes.

A question

A question to keep asking

When a cascade makes a pattern visible, who decides what counts as part of the pattern, and whose work gets remembered after the hashtag stops trending?

Fun fact

Alyssa Milano 15 October 2017 tweet was not the origin of Me Too

When actress Alyssa Milano tweeted on 15 October 2017 asking people to reply if they had been harassed, she did not know that activist had been using the phrase since 2006 for community work with survivors. Within days, Black women on social media credited Burke as the founder. Milano acknowledged this. Burke had created Just Be Inc. in 2003 in Selma, Alabama. The hashtag in 2017 made the phrase global and instant. The movement is a decade older.
