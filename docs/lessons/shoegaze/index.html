Before

Before shoegaze: walls of sound, drones and feedback

did not come from . Its deepest roots are in the 1960s. Phil Spector's productions piled many instruments on top of each other until they blurred into one huge sound. The Velvet Underground showed that a band could stand almost still on stage and make a great deal of noise. Psychedelic pop taught musicians to use the studio to make sounds that seemed to float.

In the early 1980s, after punk and post-punk, two Scottish bands pointed the way forward. were formed in Grangemouth on the Firth of Forth by guitarist and bassist Will Heggie, and in 1981 they added singer , whom met at a local disco where he was the DJ. In 1988 the band, now on , released the EP in August and their first album, , in November. These records introduced what came to be called . held the guitar's vibrato bar, sometimes called a whammy bar, while he strummed, so that every chord bent slightly up and down in pitch. He combined this with a reverse reverb effect from digital units such as the Yamaha SPX90 and with unusual tunings. The result sounds like a guitar that is melting and swelling at the same time.

Live, the band became famous for volume. At the end of their concerts, in the closing song 'You Made Me Realise', they played an interlude of pure noise that could last up to half an hour and often reached about 130 decibels. later said it was so loud it was like sensory deprivation. In 2000 the music magazine Mojo rated their late-1991 tour as the second loudest in history.

Analysis

What shoegaze was really about

Most rock music before put the singer at the centre. The voice carried the words and the personality, and the band supported it. reversed this. The voices were mixed into the guitars until the words were often impossible to hear, and the guitars were treated as clouds of sound rather than riffs. That is why the musicians stood still: the point was not the performer's body or personality but the sound filling the room.

This also explains why the press disliked it. British music journalism of the time loved bands with attitude, quotes and rivalries. bands, mostly polite middle-class young people who were friends with each other, gave the papers little to write about, so the papers wrote about that instead. and then gave the press exactly what refused: personalities and conflict.

was also unusually open to women in instrumental roles. In a scene where nobody was supposed to be the star, a woman playing bass or guitar was simply part of the sound. That is a quiet contrast with riot grrrl, which was happening at the same time in the United States: riot grrrl made women's voices loud and words impossible to ignore, while made everybody's voice, men's and women's, part of the blur.

Finally, depended on technology. Cheap digital effects units, such as the reverb units used, and later samplers made these huge textures possible for young bands with small budgets. In that sense it belongs with the other machine-driven music of the late 1980s, such as house and techno, even though it was played on guitars.

Elsewhere

After

After shoegaze: from insult to inheritance

In the 2000s the word 'shoegaze' lost its insulting meaning. A new generation found the records on the internet, without the British press's opinions attached, and simply heard beautiful music. Fun fact

The genre was named after a man reading his lyrics

On 15 March 1991 , the head of Food Records, watched the band open for at the Venue in New Cross, London. The singer, Russell Yates, kept looking down at his shoes, and Ross jokingly called the band 'shoegazers'. 's guitarist Kevin McKillop later explained that Yates was not being moody at all: he was reading lyric sheets taped to the floor because he could not remember the words. A whole genre still carries the name of that small memory problem.
