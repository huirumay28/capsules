: West Germany's new music, 1968 to 1977

Around 1968 a generation of young West German musicians decided they did not want to copy British and American rock or play their parents' . In communes, castles and home studios they built a new kind of music out of repetition, electronics and tape editing, and that music went on to shape post-punk, ambient, techno and hip-hop.

Before

Before krautrock: American radio, Schlager and a silent past

West German teenagers in the 1950s and 1960s grew up with two kinds of popular music. The first was : sentimental, carefully produced German songs about love, holidays and home, played on television and family radio. The second was British and American rock and roll, heard on the radio stations of the occupying armies and in clubs such as the Star-Club in Hamburg, where the Beatles had played before they were famous. Many German bands of the early 1960s simply copied English-language beat music.

For a young person in 1968, both options felt borrowed. belonged to the parents' generation, the same generation that had lived through the Nazi era and rarely talked about it. Anglo-American rock belonged to the countries whose armies were still stationed in West Germany. The student movement of 1968 was asking hard questions about the Nazi past, about the Vietnam War and about American power. Some young musicians started asking a similar question about sound: what would a German popular music look like if it did not come from either of these places?

This is the background that writers on the period stress again and again. The musicians who became known as bands were trying to escape both American influence and conservative German entertainment, and to build a new popular culture from close to nothing. They took ideas from free jazz, from avant-garde composers such as , from psychedelic rock and from , but they tried to put them together in a way nobody had heard before.

The sound

What krautrock sounds like

is not one style. , , and sound very different from each other. What they share is a set of habits:

  • Repetition. Many tracks are built on a single groove that repeats for ten or twenty minutes, changing slowly instead of moving through verses and choruses.
  • The . A steady, driving drum pattern with a heavy kick drum that seems to push the music forward like a car on a motorway. of and of are the drummers most associated with it.
  • Electronics. Synthesizers, organs, tape machines and home-made devices, especially in , , and .
  • Long improvisation, followed by editing. Bands often recorded hours of jamming and then cut it into finished pieces on tape.
  • The studio as an instrument. The producer and engineer were part of the band's sound, not just technicians.

The music played in Germany was often called , meaning 'cosmic music', especially for the electronic groups. The word 'krautrock' came from Britain. 'Kraut' was an old British slang word for Germans, and the term seems to have started as a joke in the early 1970s, either from the DJ or from the paper , where these bands found their first enthusiastic foreign audience. even called a 1973 track 'Krautrock', and later said that when the English started talking about , they thought the English were 'taking the piss'. The writer argued that is really a British idea: a name for how British listeners heard this music, not a name the German scene used for itself.

Think about it

A question to take with you

The musicians tried to build a popular music that owed nothing to their parents or to America. Is it possible to create a truly new sound by rejecting what came before, or did they succeed mainly by combining borrowed pieces in a new way?
