Before

The value chain and the problem it could not explain

For most of the twentieth century, strategy thinking assumed that a company sits in the middle of a line. Suppliers sell it raw materials on one side, it turns them into something more valuable, and it sells the result to customers on the other side. Costs are on the left and revenue is on the right. Michael Porter's five forces, covered in an earlier lesson, describe the pressures on a company in that position: rivals, new entrants, substitutes, powerful suppliers and powerful buyers.

Some businesses never fitted this picture well. A newspaper sells copies to readers, but it also sells space to advertisers, and the advertisers pay mainly because the readers are there. A shopping mall rents space to shops because shoppers come, and shoppers come because there are shops. A credit card is useless to a shopper if no restaurant accepts it, and useless to a restaurant if no diner carries it. In each case the company is not at the middle of a line. It stands between two groups of customers who need each other, and it can collect money from either group, or both.

The credit card shows the difficulty clearly. When and started in New York in 1950, they had to persuade restaurants and diners at the same time. They began with 27 restaurants and about 200 cardholders, mostly friends and acquaintances. They charged restaurants 7% of each bill and charged cardholders $5 a year. By the end of 1950 the club had 20,000 members, and by the end of 1951 it had 42,000. The founders had solved what later writers call the : neither side wants to join until the other side is already there.

Economists had studied , the idea that a product becomes more valuable as more people use it, since at least the telephone. What they lacked was a theory for businesses with two distinct sides that pay different prices. In 2003 the French economists and published 'Platform Competition in ', which showed mathematically that a 's choice of how to split its prices between the two sides matters as much as the total price. In 2014 received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for his work on market power and regulation, which includes this research.

The framework

Subsidy side, money side and the two kinds of network effect

In October 2006, of Harvard Business School, of Tulane University and of Boston University published 'Strategies for ' in Harvard Business Review. They called the business that connects the two groups a . A provides the infrastructure and the rules for transactions between the groups. In a traditional , they wrote, cost is to the left of the company and revenue to the right. In a , cost and revenue are on both sides.

They began with two kinds of , which can each be positive or negative:

  • A is when more users on one side make the more valuable to the other side. More players make a game console more attractive to game developers, and more games make it more attractive to players. are usually positive, but they can be negative, for example when television viewers prefer fewer advertisers.
  • A is when more users on one side change the 's value for users on that same side. It is positive when Xbox owners like having friends on the same console to play with. It is often negative among sellers, who would prefer fewer direct rivals.

Because of these effects, a successful enjoys . In most businesses, growth eventually gets harder because the remaining customers are less interested. On a , a bigger network is worth more to each user, so users will pay more to join it and margins can improve as the user base grows.

The central decision, the authors argued, is pricing. A usually has a , a group of users it charges less than it would if that group were a separate market, and a , a group that pays more because it values reaching the . The question is which side to subsidise and for how long. They gave several rules:

  • Subsidise the more price-sensitive side, and charge the side whose demand grows most strongly when the other side grows. Adobe gives away , which by 2006 had about 500 million users, and charges the writers who create PDF files, because writers value that huge audience. If Adobe charged readers instead, the reader base would shrink and the network would collapse.
  • Subsidise the side that is most sensitive to quality, and charge the side that must supply quality. Game consoles have historically been priced at or below cost, while game developers pay royalties of as much as 20% of a game's retail price. The royalty acts like a tax that keeps weak games from being made at all.
  • Make sure you can capture the . Netscape gave away its browser to individuals, hoping to sell web servers to companies. But website owners could reach Netscape's users with any rival's server, so the giveaway did not pay.
  • Watch the cost of each free user. Giving away software costs almost nothing per user. Giving away hardware does not. gave consumers free computers and internet access in 1999 in exchange for watching ads that could not be hidden, found that few advertisers wanted such cost-conscious viewers, and abandoned the offer after losing $80 million.
  • Sometimes keep users out. Many auto parts makers refused to join , a business-to-business exchange set up by carmakers, because they feared it would push their prices down. stalled. Autobytel, by contrast, sent each car buyer's enquiry to only one dealer per territory, which made dealers willing to pay, and it survived the dot-com crash.
  • Win . A few high-profile participants, such as anchor stores in a mall, can attract the other side. Visa built years of advertising around places that did not take American Express.

The authors also described what happens when pricing is done with the logic of an ordinary product. Apple charged outside developers $10,000 for the kits needed to write Macintosh software, while Microsoft gave its Windows development kits away. By the time of Microsoft's antitrust trial, they noted, Windows had six times as many applications as the Mac, and that variety made Windows more attractive to consumers despite its weaknesses.

The framework, continued

Fight or share, and the danger of envelopment

The second challenge in the 2006 article is the battle. Because networks reward size, some markets end up served by a single . The authors said this is likely when three conditions hold:

  • costs are high for at least one side. means using more than one at once. Most people use only one PC operating system because running several is expensive.
  • are positive and strong, at least for the side that finds costly.
  • Neither side has a strong need for special features. Where some users have unusual needs, a smaller can survive in a niche. American Express had issued only about 5% as many cards as Visa but earned high margins because its cards had no preset spending limit, which business travellers valued.

When a market will probably have one , a company must make what the authors called a bet-the-company decision: fight for control, or share the with rivals. Sony fought for and lost. The DVD industry met all three conditions, and in 1995 the major players chose to pool their technology into one shared format, which avoided a second format war and let the whole market grow faster, because customers did not have to fear being stranded with the losing machine.

To win a fight, the authors said, a company needs cost or differentiation advantages, existing relationships with likely users, a reputation that makes people expect it to win, and deep pockets. They added that moving first helps but is not always decisive. Google arrived years after the first search engines, avoided their cluttered portals, and copied and then improved Overture's paid-listing model.

The third challenge is . A that has beaten its direct rivals can still be swallowed by a company from a neighbouring market that bundles the same function with other things. gave its streaming media player away and sold server software to content companies, and it dominated the early market. Microsoft then bundled streaming at no extra cost into its NT Server operating system, which content companies needed anyway. Because Microsoft's servers worked only with its own player, viewers followed the content. By 2003, 42% of North American internet users named Windows Media Player as their main media player, compared with 19% for Real.

The 2016 update

Pipelines, platforms and the new rules

Ten years later, , and the adviser wrote 'Pipelines, , and the New Rules of Strategy' for the April 2016 issue of HBR. They opened with mobile phones. In 2007, Nokia, Samsung, Motorola, Sony Ericsson and LG together controlled 90% of the industry's global profits. They had strong brands, large research budgets and huge scale. Apple had less than 4% of the desktop operating system market and no phone business at all. By 2015 the iPhone alone generated 92% of global profits, and all but one of the former leaders made no profit.

Their explanation was that Apple had built a , not just a phone. The handset business is a , a linear . The , which connects outside developers with iPhone owners, turns it into a . By January 2015 the store offered 1.4 million apps and had paid developers $25 billion in total. Google's Android did the same thing in a more open way. The authors stated their main claim plainly: when a enters a 's market, the almost always wins.

They described three shifts that a company must make when it adds a :

  • From controlling resources to orchestrating them. The hardest asset to copy is no longer a mine or a patent but the community of users and what they bring, such as rooms, cars, ideas or information.
  • From internal optimisation to external interaction. The creates value by helping outsiders deal with each other, so managing the rules of that ecosystem becomes a core skill.
  • From customer value to ecosystem value. A tries to maximise the lifetime value of each customer. A tries to maximise the value of the whole network, which sometimes means subsidising one group to attract another.

They also explained why this changes Porter's five forces. In a supply-side economy, scale lowers cost, which lowers price, which raises volume. In a demand-side economy, scale raises the value of each interaction, because a bigger network gives better matches and richer data. Suppliers and customers, which the treat as threats, can add value to a . But participants can also turn against it. Zynga built games on Facebook and then tried to move players to its own , and Amazon and Samsung tried to build their own versions of Android.

On growth, the authors advised starting with one valuable interaction and expanding later. Facebook began by connecting Harvard students to each other before opening to all college students and then everyone. LinkedIn began as professional networking and later added recruiting and publishing. On openness, they warned that a without rules collects noise. , which paired strangers for video chats with no access rules, grew very fast and then lost users quickly when misbehaviour drove ordinary users away. Apple's and Google Play filter out low-quality apps, and Airbnb and Uber rate and insure hosts and drivers.

Finally, they proposed new numbers to watch: interaction failure (a Lyft user seeing 'no cars available'), engagement (Facebook's ratio of daily to monthly users), match quality (how well Google's results answer searches) and negative such as congestion. They used 's purchase of Myspace as a warning. They argued that he ran it from the top down like a newspaper, focused on controlling internal operations rather than on serving the community, and the community drifted away.

After

What managers took from the debate

The three articles together changed how executives describe their businesses. Almost every large consumer company now asks whether it can add a to its , and the 2016 article named Walmart, Nike, John Deere and GE as trying to do so. The vocabulary of , , and became standard in business schools.

The arguments also carry warnings. The 2006 authors cautioned that racing to get big fast can be a mistake when a business needs skilled staff to grow or when investors may stop funding it. and concluded that Uber and faced , widespread and limited opportunities, and that network properties were beating scale. The lesson for a manager is to look at who sits on each side, which side should pay, how easily users can leave or use a rival at the same time, and whether the network is local or global, before assuming that size will protect them.
