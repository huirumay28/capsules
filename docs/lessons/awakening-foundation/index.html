Before

Before Awakening: feminism without an organization

In the 1970s Taiwan was a one-party state under . Political parties other than the Kuomintang were banned, and so were most independent associations. 's 'New Feminism' gave the idea of women's equality a public voice, through essays, talks and a small publishing house, but it was carried by individuals rather than by a lasting organization. When Lu was arrested after the of December 1979, the movement lost its best-known speaker.

, a scholar of Chinese literature who later became a professor at Tamkang University, had her own reason to care about family law. After her divorce in 1973 she lost custody of her daughter, because the of the time gave fathers priority. She and a group of women who had worked with Lu decided that the movement needed something that could survive the arrest of any one person. Under , the safest form for that was a magazine.

The earlier lesson on Taiwan's women's movement told this story from the point of view of the whole movement. This lesson follows one organization through it, because the question for cool dairy is not only what happened but who is still doing the work today and how they pay for it.

Founding

1982 to 1987: a magazine that turned into a foundation

The () began publishing in 1982. Its aim, in the founders' words, was to awaken women's self-awareness, fight for women's rights and promote gender equality. Besides the magazine, the group ran public events. In 1983 it held a week of activities called the , built around International Women's Day on 8 March, which the says drew many women into women's organizations. In 1984 it collected the signatures of 154 women and sent them, with seven women's groups, to the Legislative Yuan in support of legal abortion, which became part of the that year.

The magazine was always short of money. was lifted in July 1987, and in October raised a fund of NT$600,000 and registered as a , a . A could receive donations more easily, invite experts onto a board, and act as a permanent headquarters for campaigns. The NT$600,000 is still on the books today: the 2025 balance sheet lists exactly NT$600,000 as 'permanently restricted net assets', the founding fund that the may not spend.

The first campaigns of the show its style. In 1987 it backed the women who worked as guides at the National Dr. Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall, who were required to resign when they turned thirty or became pregnant. In 1988 it held three protests against beauty pageants, which it said displayed women like goods in a competition, and it marched with the Taiwan Women's Rescue on Huaxi Street against child prostitution, the same campaign that produced . It also began reviewing school textbooks for gender stereotypes.

Method

How an advocacy organization works: draft the law, then fight for it

, from last week's lesson, is mainly a service provider: it runs shelters, counseling and job training, often under government contracts. is mainly an . Its product is not a service delivered to a client but a change in the rules that affect everyone. Its method, repeated over four decades, has five parts.

  • Find the problem in real cases. The , opened in 1994, gives free legal advice on marriage and family problems, and the says it handles more than two thousand calls a year. The hotline has published yearly reports since 1995 on what callers ask about, which tells the which laws hurt women in practice.
  • Write the bill yourself. drafted its in 1989 and kept revising it, reaching a fifth version in 1995 that added a chapter on workplace sexual harassment and a sixth in 1998. With the it wrote its own version of the 's family chapters.
  • Show public support. In 1995 the draft went to the Legislative Yuan with 30,000 signatures. In 1999 volunteers gathered signatures for the employment bill outside Taipei Main Station.
  • Lobby and watch. organized a 'grandmothers and mothers lobbying team' () in 1995 to follow the bill through the legislature, and a 'grandmothers and mothers court-watching team' in 1998 to observe how judges handled family cases. Before elections it asks candidates to sign pledges and publishes how legislators voted.
  • Check the law after it passes. The regularly holds press conferences that review a law some years after it took effect, such as the 2009 five-year review of the and the 2016 four-year review of the ().

This method is slow. The employment bill took twelve years, from the 1989 draft to its passage in 2001. The rewrite of the rules on marital property took about eleven years. The campaign against the crime of adultery began with a petition in 2013 and ended only when the Constitutional Court struck the law down in 2020. An group's results come years after the donations that paid for them, and they are shared with many other groups, which makes them hard to count.

Record

What Awakening helped change

The 's own introduction and chronicle list the laws it helped pass, often in coalition with other groups. Each of them is a separate story, but together they show how much of Taiwan's legal framework for gender equality passed through this one small office.

  • Body and safety: the of 1984, which legalized abortion under conditions; the reform of the sexual offences chapter of the Criminal Code in 1999; and the Sexual Assault and Sexual Harassment Prevention Acts.
  • Family: the rewritten family chapters of the , including the 1996 removal of fathers' priority in custody, new marital property rules in the early 2000s, and the 2007 change allowing parents to agree on whether a child takes the father's or the mother's surname; and the , which passed in December 2011 after a draft group the set up in 1999.
  • Work: the of 2001, which covers equal treatment, sexual harassment at work and parental leave; it has been renamed since and is now called .
  • School: the of 2004, passed with a wider civil-society alliance.
  • Migrants: amendments to the Immigration Act in 2007 that protected women who came to Taiwan through marriage, pushed by the Alliance for Human Rights Legislation for Immigrants and Migrants, which helped start in 2003.
  • Government: a law passed in 2010 created a inside the Executive Yuan, which the had demanded since the early 2000s; the department opened in 2012.
  • Partnership and marriage: co-founded the in 2009, which wrote a civil-society draft of partnership and same-sex marriage laws in 2011. The Constitutional Court's in May 2017 and the same-sex marriage law of 2019 followed.

The also published practical handbooks for women, such as the 'Complete Guide to Leaving Home' series on marital property, divorce and marital violence (, 1996 and 1997), a handbook against sexual harassment, a guide to cultural sensitivity toward new immigrants, and 《做親密，愛自主》, a booklet on affirmative consent. In 2001 it made a documentary on sexual harassment, 《》 ('The War of the Roses'), and screened it at nearly a hundred campuses.

2025

What Awakening did in 2025

The 2025 work report is a short table organized by the 's working groups. It shows an organization that mostly issues statements, attends press conferences, writes reports for government reviews and trains volunteers. The main items are:

  • Marriage and family: work on divorce law reform, including two press conferences, one on Mother's Day on 8 May about the economic independence of divorced mothers and one on 29 August, with the Judicial Reform , asking why the legislature had still not reformed divorce law after a 2023 Constitutional Court ruling.
  • Body autonomy: statements on the bill to reform the Assisted Reproduction Act and allow surrogacy, which the wants handled separately and carefully; three campus talks on affirmative consent; a speech at the Taiwan Transgender March in November.
  • Care and economic security: statements against forcing women to take longer maternity leave, in favor of flexible parental leave with better support, and against relaxing the rules for hiring foreign domestic workers as a solution to women's careers.
  • Labor: a workplace gender-equality advice line with five newly trained volunteers and an average satisfaction score of 4.5 out of 5; a statement on Equal Pay Day; screenings of a documentary about the Golden Crane (金鶴) women workers.
  • Politics: the campaign that ended on 21 November 2025, when the changed the one-quarter quota of seats reserved for women in local councils into a one-third gender-ratio rule; statements criticizing sexist remarks by politicians of several parties; and a for Taiwan's fifth national report under , the UN .
  • Democratic defence: a project funded by the , a German political , with workshops on civil defence and information warfare and a policy paper on 'resilient and inclusive whole-of-society defence', presented on 25 November.
  • Training: legal courses for hotline volunteers, 18 office volunteers, 13 interns across the year, and two student assistants who wrote reports on local gender-equality committees.

Notice what is missing compared with : there are no shelters, no case numbers in the tens of thousands, and no service sites. The hotlines are the only direct services. Almost everything else is research, writing and public pressure.

Money

Where the money comes from and where it goes

The posts its budget, work report and financial statements every year on a page called , 'financial verification'. The 2025 statements were signed by the chair, (). In 2025 total income was NT$5,747,260, made up of:

  • Donations: NT$3,260,148, or 57 percent.
  • Government subsidies: NT$2,226,438, or 39 percent.
  • Interest: NT$176,666, or 3 percent.
  • Sales of goods and services, such as publications and donation gifts: NT$53,904, or 1 percent.
  • Other income: NT$30,104, or 1 percent.

Total spending was NT$8,049,523, so the ran a deficit of about NT$2.3 million and drew on its reserves. At the end of 2025 its net assets were NT$11,847,156, of which NT$600,000 is the permanent founding fund. In other words, has savings worth roughly a year and a half of spending.

The year before looked very different. In 2024 income was NT$8,891,638, of which donations were NT$7,768,625, or 87 percent, and government subsidies only NT$795,818. Donations fell by NT$4.5 million between the two years. The statements do not say why, so this lesson does not guess. For a small organization, a single large gift or one campaign year can move the totals a great deal.

Two more details are worth noticing, because they show how to read a financial statement. First, the balance sheet lists NT$7,058,674 of 'income received in advance' () at the end of 2025, up from NT$384,500. This is money that has arrived but belongs to work in a later period, so accountants count it as a liability rather than as income. Second, the split between program spending and administrative spending flipped: in 2024 program spending was NT$6,104,411 and administration NT$898,890, but in 2025 program spending was NT$1,530,896 and administration NT$6,518,627. The statement gives no explanation. The likeliest reason is a change in how staff costs were classified, but that is a guess, and the right thing to do as a possible partner is to ask.

Compare the scale with , whose 2025 income was about NT$640.7 million. 's income was less than one percent of that. A donation of NT$50,000 from cool dairy would be a rounding error for but nearly one percent of 's whole year, and about one and a half percent of its donations.

Structure

Foundation, association, alliance: three ways to organize

is a , a . Under Taiwan's (), a is built around a fund of property and run by a board. 's board members are experts on gender issues who serve without pay, and the board hires a small paid staff, which the describes as six or seven people. The requires foundations to report their budgets and accounts to the government, and also publishes its own on its website.

The , founded in 1992, and a Taipei are different legal creatures: they are , associations built around members who elect their own officers. They share the name and work with the , but they are separate organizations. If cool dairy wants to support 's work in the south, for example, it would be dealing with a different legal body from the one in Taipei.

Much of 's work also happens through alliances: the with LGBTQ groups, the immigrant rights alliance, alliances on childcare and long-term care, and joint press conferences with many women's groups. This is efficient for a tiny organization, but it means that a single victory usually belongs to several groups at once.

Elsewhere

What was happening elsewhere at the same time

's founding years overlapped with several turning points in other countries' women's movements:

  • United States, 1982. The deadline for ratifying the expired on 30 June 1982, the same year 's magazine began. American feminists turned to state laws and court cases, while Taiwan's feminists were just starting to build organizations.
  • Japan, 1985. Japan's passed in May 1985 and took effect in April 1986. Critics said it mostly asked employers to 'make efforts' rather than forbidding discrimination, and many companies responded with a two-track hiring system. Taiwan's own employment equality bill, drafted by in 1989, took until 2001 and was written to be stronger.
  • South Korea, 1987. was founded in February 1987, in the same year that both Korea and Taiwan moved toward democracy. Like , it combined the women's movement with the democracy movement and later pushed for laws against sexual violence.

The common pattern in Taiwan and South Korea is that feminist organizations grew up together with democratization, so their main tool became law reform through newly elected legislatures. That history still shapes today: it thinks of itself as a watchdog of government as much as a women's group.

For cool dairy

Awakening as a possible partner

Here is a plain summary to set beside last week's notes on .

  • Strengths: forty-four years of history and a record of laws that every Taiwanese woman lives under; it publishes its work reports and financial statements; it is independent and criticizes politicians of every party; and its issues, from divorce law to care work and body autonomy, fit a shop that wants to educate customers about women's rights.
  • Things to weigh: it is small and its income swings a lot from year to year; its 2025 accounts show a deficit and an unexplained change in how spending is classified; and its results are changes in law that take years and are shared with coalitions, so cool dairy cannot point to 'one shelter bed' or 'one girl helped'.
  • What a small donor means: a few tens of thousands of NT dollars is a real share of 's budget. It could pay for a hotline volunteer training course or a campaign's printing costs. That makes it possible to tell customers a concrete story, if the agrees to say what the money paid for.
  • Easy first steps: the lists a for electronic receipts, 3899, so shoppers in Taiwan can donate the lottery chance on their e-invoice to it. Cool dairy could print that code on its packaging before signing any formal partnership.
  • Questions to ask before partnering: Why did donations fall from about NT$7.8 million in 2024 to about NT$3.3 million in 2025? Why did administrative spending rise from about NT$0.9 million to about NT$6.5 million? What is the NT$7 million received in advance for?
