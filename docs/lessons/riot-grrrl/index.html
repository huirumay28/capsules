Before

Before riot grrrl: women in punk, and punk without women

Women had been central to punk from the start. In London in the late 1970s, of X-Ray Spex, Siouxsie Sioux, and made records that sounded nothing like male rock. In America, had come out of the Runaways, and in the 1980s of Sonic Youth and Kim Deal of the Pixies showed that a woman could play bass, sing and shape a band's sound. later said that just knowing Gordon existed made her feel less alone. A 1982 film, , about a fictional teenage girl punk band, also became a cult favourite among future riot grrrls.

But by the late 1980s, American punk had changed. , the fast and aggressive style that grew in Washington, D.C., Los Angeles and elsewhere, made the front of the stage a where young men slammed into each other. Women who wanted to stand near the band had to fight their way in and risked being groped. Very few women played in the bands. wrote in her that she felt 'completely left out', partly because 'punk rock is for and by boys mostly'.

Deep dive

Bikini Kill and "girls to the front"

What made the band different was what happened in the room. asked the young men at the front to move to the sides or the back so that girls could stand near the stage. The band handed out lyric sheets so the audience could read words about rape, incest and harassment that would otherwise be lost in the noise. sometimes jumped into the crowd herself to remove men who heckled or attacked her. 'Girls to the front' became the movement's best-known slogan.

The hostility was real. remembered that people threw chains at the band's heads, and that on one night men would shout 'take it off' while the next night crowds of girls would sing along to every word. Analysis

The media blackout and the criticism

In 1992 the mainstream press discovered . Newsweek described it as 'feminism with a loud happy face dotting the i', and USA Today wrote that 'from hundreds of once pink, frilly bedrooms comes the young feminist revolution'. Reporters focused on the barrettes, the baby-doll dresses and the words written on skin, and rarely on what the women said about sexual abuse. later said the media 'refused to do serious interviews with us' and took their writing out of context. asked members to stop talking to the mainstream press, a decision known as the . wrote in 1994 that had never called themselves a band and were not leaders of the movement.

The blackout protected the movement from being turned into a fashion, but it also meant others defined it. The term 'riot ' was attached to almost any band with a woman in it, including , Babes in Toyland and , who had never used it. A more serious criticism came from inside. In 1993 wrote in that was 'growing very closed to a very few i.e. white middle class punk girls'. and wrote about the movement from the perspective of Asian American girls, and in 1997 published the compilation Evolution of a Race Riot. In the same year, the Black punk musician started in New York with Simi Stone, Honeychild Coleman and Maya Sokora, a series of shows by and for Black women in punk. These critiques belong to the history of , the idea that sexism is experienced differently depending on race and class.

Elsewhere

What was happening elsewhere at the same time

In the same years, the second-wave legal agenda of the 1970s was giving way to what the writer in 1992 called the of feminism, and is often treated as its musical voice. In October 1991 the hearings in the United States Senate, where Hill testified that the Supreme Court nominee Clarence Thomas had sexually harassed her, made sexual harassment a national subject. Later in the decade in Britain, the mainstream answer to girl anger was the ' 'girl power', which many riot grrrls saw as a commercial version of their own ideas with the politics removed.
