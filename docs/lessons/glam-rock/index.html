Before

Before glam: the serious rock of the late 1960s

By 1970, rock in Britain and America had become serious. After psychedelia, the most respected musicians made long albums rather than singles, played extended solos, and dressed down in denim and long hair. bands such as and borrowed from classical music. Singer-songwriters such as , whose album came out in 1971, offered quiet personal confession. Rock critics prized : the idea that a musician should sing honestly about their own life and should not look as if they were trying to sell anything.

At the same time, the British singles chart and the BBC's weekly chart show were still watched by millions of teenagers, many of them younger than the album-buying rock audience. The Beatles had split in 1970. There was room for pop stars who could make three-minute singles exciting again and look extraordinary on a colour television screen. Colour broadcasting had started on BBC One in late 1969, so the way performers looked on television mattered more than ever.

grew out of this gap. Its musicians the volume and guitars of rock, the short catchy song of 1950s rock and roll and 1960s pop, and the costume and make-up of theatre and cabaret. They treated pop stardom as a performance that could be designed, rather than as an expression of an inner true self.

Analysis

What glam meant

is often remembered as a fashion, but it carried real ideas about music and identity.

  • It rejected rock . Hippie and asked musicians to be sincere. musicians invented characters, wore costumes and openly admitted that pop stardom was artificial. 's is a story about a manufactured star, sung by a manufactured star.
  • It brought back the single and the young fan. records were short, loud and built for radio and . They were aimed at teenagers and even children, at a time when serious rock had moved towards adult album buyers.
  • It made gender into a performance. Men in make-up, glitter and platform boots, and women such as in leather playing the bass, challenged what a rock man and a rock woman should look like. This happened in the same years as the new gay liberation movement: the started in New York after the of 1969, and a London branch began in 1970.
  • It had clear limits. Most stars were straight or did not say otherwise, and they borrowed camp and androgyny as style. The industry punished real openness, as 's career shows. Some was also crude and laddish, closer to a football chant than to a statement about gender.
