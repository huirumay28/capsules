The concept

What obedience to authority means: following commands from a legitimate authority even when conscience pulls the other way

is with direct commands issued by someone perceived to hold legitimate power. It is not the same as —going along with a peer group—or simple with a request from an equal. operates in a hierarchy. The occupies a role that grants the right to command: a military officer, a doctor, a police officer, a scientist in a laboratory. The subordinate occupies a role that carries the duty to obey. The mechanism depends on legitimacy. A random stranger shouting orders on the street has no authority. A person in the right role, in the right setting, with the right symbols—a uniform, a title, an institutional affiliation—can issue commands that override personal judgment.

The concept becomes urgent when the command conflicts with conscience. to a benign authority coordinating a fire drill is unremarkable. to a command that harms another person is a moral problem. Yet hierarchical systems—military, medical, corporate, bureaucratic—routinely require . The question social psychology asks is: under what conditions will ordinary people follow orders to do things they believe are wrong? And the darker question, historically grounded: how do atrocities happen when so many participants are ordinary people who say they were 'just following orders'?

designed his experiments in the shadow of that question. 's trial in Jerusalem in 1961 brought the Holocaust back into public attention. 's reporting coined the phrase 'the ': the idea that great moral wrongs can be perpetrated by people who are not monsters but bureaucrats, following orders, doing their jobs. wanted to know if of that kind could be measured, isolated, manipulated. He built a laboratory situation in which the authority was a scientist, the setting was Yale, the task was framed as research, and the command was to hurt a stranger. Then he measured how many people would do it.

The mechanism

How Milgram designed a situation in which an authority commanded harm and tracked who complied

The setup was deceptively simple. An advertisement in a New Haven newspaper invited men to participate in a study of memory and learning at Yale, offering $4.50 for one hour. Participants arrived at 's Interaction Laboratory. They met another 'participant'—actually a , a mild-mannered middle-aged accountant—and an in a gray lab coat. The explained the cover story: they were testing whether punishment improves learning. One person would be the , one the . A rigged drawing ensured the real participant was always assigned .

The was strapped into a chair in an adjacent room with electrodes attached to his wrist. The sat before an impressive : a console with 30 lever switches labeled in 15-volt increments from 15 to 450 volts, with verbal descriptors ranging from 'Slight Shock' to 'Danger: Severe Shock' and finally two levers ominously marked 'XXX.' The generator was fake, but it looked real. The gave the a sample 45-volt shock—real—so the experienced what the would supposedly feel, scaled up.

The task began. The read word pairs; the tried to recall them. For each wrong answer, the administered a shock and increased the voltage. At first the responded correctly. Then errors began. The flipped switches. At 75 volts, the grunted. At 120 volts, he shouted that the shocks hurt. At 150 volts, he demanded to be released. The often looked to the . The , calm and firm, delivered standardized : 'Please continue.' 'The experiment requires that you continue.' 'It is absolutely essential that you continue.' 'You have no other choice, you must go on.'

The 's protests escalated—scripted, often prerecorded. At 180V: 'I can't stand the pain!' At 300V: desperate banging on the wall. After 330V: silence. Many teachers were visibly distressed: sweating, trembling, laughing nervously, biting their lips. Yet in the baseline — in the next room, vocal protests audible—26 of 40 participants () continued all the way to 450 volts. They did not do so calmly. Many protested, hesitated, questioned. But when the insisted, most obeyed.

When the had to physically hold the 's hand on a shock plate (touch-proximity), fell to 30 percent. When the left the room and issued commands by telephone, plummeted to about 21 percent—and some participants lied, saying they were raising the voltage when they were not. When the experiment was moved from Yale's prestigious lab to a modest office in Bridgeport, dropped but remained substantial, around 48 percent. When two experimenters disagreed about continuing, collapsed to near zero. The situational levers were clear: physical presence of authority, proximity of , legitimacy of setting, unanimity of command.

The case

New Haven, ordinary adults, and the shock generator at Yale in 1961–1962

Picture the participant's experience. You answered a newspaper ad for a memory study at Yale. You arrive at a Gothic campus, walk into a psychology building, and meet a scientist in a lab coat and another volunteer. The scientist explains the experiment: punishment and learning. You draw slips of paper; you will be the . The other man—friendly, middle-aged—is strapped into a chair. Electrodes are attached. You return to the main room. Before you is an imposing piece of equipment: the . The scientist gives you a real sample shock. You feel it: sharp, unpleasant. That is 45 volts. The machine goes to 450.

The task starts. Word pairs. The makes mistakes. You flip the first switch: 15 volts. Then 30. At 75, he grunts. You glance at the . He nods: continue. At 120 volts, the shouts, 'Hey, this really hurts!' You hesitate. The : 'Please continue.' You are not sadistic. You do not want to hurt anyone. But the is a scientist. This is Yale. He says it is essential. At 150 volts the yells, 'Experimenter, get me out of here! I won't be in the experiment anymore! I refuse to go on!' You turn to the . He meets your eyes, calm and authoritative: 'The experiment requires that you continue. You have no other choice.' You continue.

At 300 volts the pounds on the wall, screaming. Then at 330, silence. Nothing. You ask: 'Should I keep going? What if something happened to him?' The : 'The shocks may be painful, but they are not dangerous. Please continue.' You feel sick. Your hands are shaking. You flip the switch. 345 volts. 360. 375. The label now says 'Danger: Severe Shock.' No sound from the next room. You reach 450 volts: XXX. The tells you to continue at 450 for several more errors. Finally, he says, 'The experiment is over.' He opens the door. The walks in, unharmed, smiling. It was fake. No shocks. The scientist explains: this was a study of . You followed orders. So did most people.

That is the structure built, and that is what happened in session after session, with men from New Haven—postal clerks, high school teachers, salesmen, engineers—and later with women (who obeyed at nearly the same rate). The headline result— went to 450 volts—became one of psychology's most famous and disturbing findings. But the number does not capture the texture. Participants were not calm. They argued. They sweated. Some laughed nervously—a stress response. One man had a seizure-like fit. Many said afterward they had felt trapped: they wanted to stop but the kept saying continue. A few stopped early and walked out. About a third refused at some point before 450 volts. Most did not.

He later sent follow-up surveys; over 80 percent of respondents said they were glad to have participated, that the experience was valuable. That does not erase the , but it complicates the ethical accounting. The participants were not permanently harmed; most said they learned something important. Yet they were deceived, distressed, and placed in a situation where they believed they were hurting someone. That is the moral knot the experiments tied.

Contemporaneous elsewhere

Hofling's hospital nurses and real-world obedience in 1966

While was publishing his laboratory findings, , a psychiatrist, wondered if the mechanism extended to real professional settings where the stakes were not laboratory abstractions but actual patient safety. In 1966, Hofling and colleagues conducted a field experiment in hospital wards. A researcher posing as 'Dr. Smith' called nurses on duty and ordered them to administer 20 milligrams of a drug called Astroten to a patient. The order violated multiple rules: the caller was unknown to the nurse, the dosage was twice the maximum listed on the bottle, hospital policy required written orders, and Astroten was not an authorized medication (it was a placebo created for the study).

Twenty-one of 22 nurses complied. They prepared the medication and were walking toward the patient's room when a researcher intercepted them and explained the true nature of the call. In follow-up interviews, nurses said they had felt uncomfortable but believed doctors had authority and that questioning an order might lead to reprimand. The study was published in 1966 in the journal Clinical Pharmacology and Therapeutics. It demonstrated that operates in high-stakes, real-world hierarchies, not only in contrived laboratory paradigms. The has its own ethical critics—nurses were deceived and put in a position where they nearly harmed a (fictitious) patient—but it stands as contemporaneous evidence that 's findings were not just lab curiosities.

Later partial replications of Hofling under slightly different conditions found lower rates, and debates continue about how hospital protocols and authority structures have changed. But the 1966 result, conducted just a few years after 's baseline publications, showed crossing from experimental psychology into medical sociology. Both and Hofling were asking versions of the same question: when does the role override the conscience? The laboratory and the hospital ward gave different but parallel answers: often.

After

Replications, ethics debates, Burger's partial replication, and what the archives revealed

's paradigm became one of the most replicated and debated studies in psychology. Over the decades, researchers in the Netherlands, Germany, Australia, Poland, South Africa, and elsewhere conducted variations. Results varied by country, era, and procedural details, but the core finding held: a substantial proportion of people comply with an authority's commands to harm a stranger, especially when the authority is perceived as legitimate, the setting is institutional, and the incremental escalation makes each step seem small. 's meta-analyses across studies found rates of full ranging from 28 percent to over 90 percent depending on conditions, with a rough average near 60 percent.

The ignited almost immediately. 's 1964 critique in argued that had violated participants' trust, caused them , and potentially damaged their self-image and faith in authority. replied that was thorough, that follow-up surveys showed most participants valued the experience, and that the knowledge gained—understanding the situational roots of —justified the procedure. The debate never fully resolved. By the 1970s, institutional review boards tightened oversight, and studies like 's became difficult or impossible to conduct under new ethical standards. The experiments themselves became a case study in research ethics courses: a landmark finding achieved by methods now considered unacceptable.

In 2009, at Santa Clara University published a partial in , the first authorized in the United States in decades. modified the procedure to meet modern ethical standards: participants were screened to exclude those at risk of , they were told multiple times they could withdraw, and the procedure stopped at 150 volts—the point where 's first loudly demanded release. reasoned that at 150 volts predicted to 450 in 's original data, so stopping there captured the effect without the maximum-shock . The result: 70 percent of 's participants were willing to continue past 150 volts, closely matching 's baseline rate at that point. The mechanism, it seemed, was alive in the 21st century.

Meanwhile, historians and psychologists began digging into 's archives at Yale's Sterling Memorial Library. 's 2013 book Behind the drew on audio recordings, transcripts, and unpublished condition data. Perry found that not all sessions followed the standard script perfectly; some experimenters prodded participants more aggressively than the official protocol specified. Some participants suspected the shocks were fake. The figure, while real, came from a specific condition among many; other variations had lower rates. Textbook oversimplifications—sometimes claiming 'almost everyone obeyed'—were corrected. The experiments were more variable, messier, and more context-dependent than the legend suggested.

Yet the core insight persists. Ordinary people in an ordinary hierarchy with a credible authority will often follow orders that violate their own moral intuitions, especially when responsibility is diffused ('the is in charge'), the progression is incremental (each step seems small), and the authority is physically present and insistent. 's findings do not explain the Holocaust; atrocities involve ideology, dehumanization, group dynamics, and long-term socialization, not just lab . But the experiments show one mechanism that can contribute: situational pressure overriding individual conscience. That remains a lesson worth teaching, even as the methods that produced it remain ethically disputed.

A question

A question to keep asking

If you were a participant in 's —strapped into a hierarchy, assured by an authority that the procedure was safe and necessary, hearing the screams of someone in the next room—at what voltage would you have stopped, and how can you be sure? And if you believe you would have stopped early: what specific features of the situation would have given you the strength to defy the 's when two-thirds of participants in your position did not?

Fun fact

The learner's protests were scripted and recorded

In most of 's voice-feedback conditions, the 's responses were not live—they were prerecorded and played from a tape deck. At 75 volts: a grunt. At 120V: 'Hey, this really hurts!' At 150V: 'Experimenter, get me out of here! I refuse to go on!' At 300V: desperate pounding on the wall. After 330V: silence. The script was carefully calibrated to ratchet up moral pressure. 's , a middle-aged accountant, had practiced the script to sound authentic. Participants did not know they were hearing a recording; they believed they were hearing a man in pain in the next room. That scripted authenticity is part of why the procedure worked—and why it troubles ethicists.
