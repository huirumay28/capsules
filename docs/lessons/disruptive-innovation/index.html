Before

When doing everything right causes you to lose

In the decades before and Joseph Bower published 'Disruptive Technologies: Catching the Wave' in the January–February 1995 Harvard Business Review, the standard managerial advice was clear: listen to your best customers, invest in the technologies they value, improve your products along the dimensions that matter most to the market. Firms that did all of that were admired. The paradox documented was that capable, well-managed companies could follow that advice faithfully — and still be displaced by competitors they had every reason to ignore until it was too late.

The framework

Sustaining versus disruptive innovation

The core distinction is between and . Sustaining innovations make products better along the dimensions established customers already value. Disruptive innovations start by underperforming on traditional metrics but offer lower cost, more convenience, or access for nonconsumers. The 2015 essay distinguishes low-end (serving overserved customers with 'good enough' quality) from new-market (creating markets among people who previously could not afford the product). The process unfolds over years as the disruptor improves and moves upmarket.

The cases

Mini-mills, Honda, and Netflix

Steel mini-mills are 's canonical case. In the 1960s and 1970s, mini-mills using electric arc furnaces entered at the bottom with rebar, which integrated mills did not defend. Mini-mill technology improved, and producers moved upmarket into structural beams and then higher grades. Nucor is the firm most associated with this path.

Honda's entry into the U.S. motorcycle market with small, inexpensive bikes (the Supercub) is 's example of new-market . These bikes enabled nonconsumers who would not have bought large touring bikes. Netflix disrupting Blockbuster via DVD-by-mail is another teaching case, though the 2015 essay notes Netflix's streaming did not cleanly fit the original low-end path.

Limits

Uber is not disruption

The December 2015 essay exists because 'disruptive innovation' became so popular it lost its meaning. The 2015 piece insists: starts in an unattractive foothold and moves upmarket over years. Uber, despite being widely called disruptive, attacked the mainstream taxi market directly with a superior service — that is competition, not . Misusing the term hides what makes true hard for incumbents to counter.

One question

What low-end market are you ignoring?

Look at your organization or an industry you know. Is there a segment at the bottom — overserved customers who would accept 'good enough' for less, or nonconsumers who cannot afford the current offer — that you or the incumbents are rationally ignoring? What would it take to serve that segment?
