In 1969 a film about a villager who loses his cow, and then his mind, was banned by the Shah's government, smuggled to , and won a critics' prize. Between those two moments came a revolution, an eight-year war, a strict new code of what could be shown on screen, and two generations of filmmakers — , , , , , , and many others — who turned plain stories about children, villages and ordinary people into some of the most admired films in the world. Before

Filmfarsi, a poet with a camera, and the 1960s

Cinema reached Iran early. In 1900 Mozaffar ad-Din Shah saw the cinematograph in France and had his court photographer buy one, and Tehran had cinemas within a few years. But by the 1950s and 1960s the films most Iranians actually watched belonged to what critics call : cheaply made melodramas, comedies and tough-guy stories with songs and dances, borrowing freely from Hollywood and Indian cinema. Censorship under the Pahlavi monarchy was strict, and films that showed poverty, corruption or politics in a serious way had a hard time getting made or shown.

The film that people usually point to as the seed of something different was not a feature at all. In 1962 , already one of the most important and most controversial poets in Persian, went to the leper colony near Tabriz with a small crew from 's studio. The result, , is about twenty minutes long. It shows the patients plainly — eating, praying, going to school, getting married — and sets the images against 's own voice reading from the Old Testament, the Quran and her own poetry. In 1963 it won the documentary grand prize at the in West Germany, the same festival where, a year earlier, young German filmmakers had issued the that this site covered in the New German Cinema lesson. died in a car accident in February 1967, at 32.

Other precursors followed in feature films. 's (1964) was a dark comedy set in Tehran. 's (1965), in which a taxi driver finds an abandoned baby in his cab, looked at the city at night with a seriousness did not have. 's (1967) was a modernist retelling of an episode from the . None of these was a box-office hit, but together they showed that another kind of Iranian film was possible.

Historians note that never had a manifesto or a magazine of its own, and its directors came from different backgrounds and had different styles, so it is better described as a moment than as an organised school.

The break

The Cinema Rex fire, the revolution, and new rules

Cinemas were among the targets of the revolutionary movement of 1978, because many religious opponents of the Shah saw them as symbols of Western immorality. The worst attack happened on 19 August 1978 in the oil city of . During an evening screening of 's , four men barred the doors of the and set it on fire. Between 377 and 470 people died; the exact figure is still disputed. At the time many protesters blamed , the Shah's secret police, and the fire increased anger against the government. Later trials and investigations pointed instead to a radical Islamist group, and was identified as one of the four men who set the fire.

After the Shah left in January 1979 and the Islamic Republic was established, it was not obvious that cinema would survive at all. One story often told is that had seen and admired , and that this helped persuade the new leadership that film could serve good purposes rather than only corrupt ones. Many people from the old industry left the country or stopped working. went to Paris in 1981 and stayed several years.

In the 1980s the new state built its own system. became minister of culture and Islamic guidance in 1982. The was set up in 1983 to support production, and the has been held in Tehran every February since 1983. Scripts, productions and final cuts needed approval, and there was a code of conduct for what could be shown. Actresses had to wear the and loose clothing on screen at all times, even in scenes set inside a home or in bed, where real Iranian women did not cover their hair. Men and women who were not related in real life could not touch, even when they were playing husband and wife. Women could not sing or dance on screen. All of this took place during the , which lasted from 1980 to 1988 and killed hundreds of thousands of people.

  • Children as main characters. Stories about children avoided many of the problems of showing adult men and women together, and children could move between homes and streets in a way adults on screen could not. 's experience in children's film helped here.
  • Real places and non-professional actors. Many films were shot in villages and city streets with local people playing roles close to their own lives, a method critics often compared to .
  • Long takes and simple stories. A search for a notebook, a goldfish, a pair of shoes or a balloon could carry a whole film, and the larger subjects — poverty, war, the position of women — sat in the background.
  • Films about film. Several directors began to put the making of the film itself on screen, blurring the line between documentary and fiction.

The second wave

From the late 1980s: the films the world noticed

By the late 1980s a second wave had formed, mixing veterans of the 1970s with new directors who had come of age during . 's , made in 1986 but held back until 1989, tells of an Afro-Iranian Arab boy from Khuzestan in the south whose parents are killed in a bombing raid in the war. He escapes on a cargo truck and ends up in the green Caspian north. A Gilak farming woman named Na'i, played by , takes him in although he speaks Arabic and she speaks Gilaki and her neighbours call him a stranger. Iranian filmmakers worked under rules that told them what they could not show, and many of them made that limit part of their style: children instead of couples, long shots from a car instead of intimate scenes, a story about a notebook that is really about how adults treat children. Think about a time you had to say something important under a restriction — at work, in a family, or online. Did the limit make what you said weaker, or did it force you to find a sharper way to say it?

Elsewhere

What else was happening at the same time

The first wave sat in the middle of the worldwide wave of new cinemas that this site has followed. (1969) appeared a year after the Prague Spring ended the Czechoslovak New Wave, while Brazil's Cinema Novo and the New German Cinema were at their height and New Hollywood was beginning in the United States. Like several of these movements, the was a reaction against a commercial cinema that ignored ordinary life, and it was strongly shaped by , whose methods — real locations, non-professional actors, stories of the poor — Iranian directors adapted to their own country.

The second wave overlapped with a great period for Chinese-language film. At the 1997 Cannes festival where won, won best director for Happy Together, the same year 's Hana-bi won the . In Denmark, Lars von Trier and Thomas Vinterberg launched in 1995, a set of rules that banned studio sets and added music. It is worth comparing: the Danish directors chose their restrictions as an artistic game, while Iranian directors had theirs imposed by the state, and both groups ended up with films of handheld realism and plain storytelling.

Fun fact

The most famous kiss in Iranian cinema was on a cheek

almost missed Cannes in 1997: the held back its export permit so long that the reels arrived after the festival had begun. When the film then shared the — the first for an Iranian film — presented the prize, and she and exchanged a polite kiss on the cheek. In France that was ordinary manners. In Iran, conservative critics treated a public kiss with a woman who was not his wife as an offence, the scandal overshadowed the prize, and when flew home he was taken through customs and out a side door to avoid protesters waiting at the airport.
