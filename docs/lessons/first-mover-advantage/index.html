Before

The belief that the first company wins

By the 1980s, many managers treated being first to market as one of the most dependable rules in business. The idea felt intuitive. The first company in a new category gets to set the standard, sign the best suppliers, take the best shelf space and teach customers what the product is. Later companies then have to persuade those customers to switch.

Research seemed to support the rule. and later summarised this earlier work in their 1996 article. Several studies of the (, a large survey of American businesses) found average market shares of around 30 percent for , 19 percent for early followers and 13 percent for late entrants. data also suggested that more than 70 percent of current market leaders had been . A study using a different database, called , produced similar numbers. Some writers even advised companies to announce products early so that they could claim the 's rewards.

Strategy scholars and , writing in 1988, took the idea more carefully. They did not ask whether being first is good. They asked what, exactly, would allow a first mover to earn more than its rivals, and what could take that edge away.

The framework

Three ways a pioneer builds a lead, and four ways it loses one

and defined as the ability of a pioneering firm to earn positive economic profits, meaning profits above its cost of capital, because it moved early. They argued that such an advantage comes from three main mechanisms.

  • . The can move down the first, so its costs fall before rivals have started producing. It can also win patents and keep research results to itself for a time.
  • . The can take things that later entrants cannot easily get: the best locations, key raw materials, the most useful suppliers, or space in customers' minds and on store shelves. It can also build extra capacity that makes a newcomer's investment look risky.
  • . Once customers have learned to use a product, signed a contract or built their habits around a brand, changing to a rival costs them time, money or effort. The is the first company to collect these locked-in customers.

The same paper listed four . These are the reasons a later entrant, often called a or , can do better than the .

  • . A follower can copy the 's product, hire its trained staff and benefit from the 's work educating customers, without paying the same cost.
  • Resolution of uncertainty. The has to guess what customers want and which technical design will win. A follower can wait, watch and enter once the answer is clearer.
  • Shifts in technology or customer needs. A new technology can make the 's investment obsolete, and buyers' tastes can move away from what the built.
  • . Having invested in particular factories, products and habits, the may be slow to change. It may also hesitate to replace its own successful product with a newer one.

The value of the framework is that it turns a slogan into a checklist. A manager can ask which of the three advantages is actually available in a given market, and how strongly each of the four disadvantages applies. If none of the advantages is available, being first simply means paying to teach the market for someone else.

The evidence

Golder and Tellis go back to the historical record

In 1993 and argued that the earlier studies had three weaknesses. The and databases surveyed only firms that still existed, so failed were invisible. Those databases identified the by asking a current employee, and employees of successful firms tend to remember their company as having been first. And the studies relied on a small number of databases.

and used a different method: historical analysis of old trade magazines, newspapers, company histories and other records. They studied about 500 brands in 50 product categories. They distinguished the (the first firm to develop a working product) from the (the first firm to sell it), and both from the current leader.

Their results were very different from the earlier picture. Almost half of , 47 percent, had failed, meaning they had stopped selling in the category under the brand they entered with. The failure rate for durable goods was more than twice as high as for nondurable goods, which the authors linked to faster technological change. In their 1996 MIT Sloan Management Review article, 'First to Market, First to Fail?', and asked what the enduring leaders had in common. They named five factors: vision, persistence, commitment, innovation and , meaning the use of strengths built in a related business. In other words, the winners were often the firms that saw how big a market could become and spent heavily to build it, not the firms that happened to arrive first.

A decision tool

Suárez and Lanzolla's calm and rough waters

and , writing in Harvard Business Review in April 2005, said that is more than a myth but far less than a sure thing. They based this on the research literature and on more than 30 cases of early entry. Their key point is that two outside forces, which no single company controls, matter as much as the company's own resources: the pace at which the product's technology evolves and the pace at which the market for it grows.

Putting those two speeds together gives four situations.

  • Slow technology and slow market growth, which they call . This is the best setting for a . It can build a lasting lead even without exceptional skills or deep pockets. Their example is in vacuum cleaners.
  • Slow technology but a fast-growing market. A with large resources can build a lasting lead, because it can keep up with demand and build its brand and distribution. Their example is Sony's . A with few resources will probably only get a short-term benefit.
  • Fast technology but a slowly growing market. Neither a lasting nor a short-term advantage is likely, because the product keeps changing before the market is ready. Only companies with very deep pockets survive. Their example is Sony and the digital cameras it pioneered.
  • Fast technology and a fast-growing market, which they call . This is the worst setting for a lasting lead. A can still make a worthwhile short-term gain if it knows when to get out. Their example is , which agreed to be acquired by AOL.

Their advice is to analyse the environment and your own resources before entering a new market, and then decide which kind of , lasting or temporary, is realistic. They end with the line that once you have gone into the water, you have no choice but to swim.

The matrix also explains a pattern in and 's data. Durable goods such as video recorders, fax machines and personal computers sat in fast-moving technology, where the disadvantages of going first, especially technology shifts and the follower's chance to learn, were strongest. Their failed much more often than in slow-changing household products.

After

What managers took from the debate

The debate changed the question managers ask. The old question was how to get to market first. The newer question is whether this particular market rewards being first, and if so, which of and 's three advantages the company can actually capture.

It also connects to other frameworks in this series. The Seven Powers lesson describes , scale economies and network economies as durable sources of advantage; these are close cousins of and 's mechanisms, and a only keeps its lead if it turns early entry into one of them. The disruptive innovation lesson shows the problem from the other side. The core competence lesson shows how a company can use skills built elsewhere, which is what and meant by .

One question

Should this company go first?

Pick a new product category you have seen appear in the last few years. Who was the first company to sell it, and who leads it today?
