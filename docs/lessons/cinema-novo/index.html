: Brazil's cinema of hunger, 1960 to 1972

In the early 1960s a group of young Brazilian directors, most of them in their twenties, decided that their country's films should stop copying Hollywood and start showing Brazil as it was: the drought-stricken northeast, the of Rio, the hungry and the powerless. With little money and handheld cameras they made some of the most important political films of the century. Then a military in 1964, and a harsher crackdown in 1968, forced the to change shape, turn to allegory and finally scatter.

Before

Before Cinema Novo: carnival comedies and a failed Hollywood

In the 1940s and early 1950s, most Brazilian films that ordinary people saw were . These were cheap, fast musical comedies, many of them made by the in Rio de Janeiro, which opened in 1941. They were full of carnival songs, mistaken identities and parodies of Hollywood hits, and they made stars of comedians such as and . Audiences loved them. Critics and young intellectuals tended to see them as silly and as proof that Brazil could only imitate foreign films badly.

In 1949 a group of São Paulo industrialists, led by the Italian-born producer and , tried a different route. They founded the in do Campo and hired the Brazilian director , who had built his career in France and Britain, to run it. imported foreign technicians and aimed for the polish of a Hollywood studio. It produced more than forty features, and one of them, 's bandit film (1953), won a prize at . But the costs were too high for the Brazilian market, distribution was controlled by others, and the company stopped production in 1954.

For the young cinephiles who would start , both models had failed. The was popular but, in their view, empty. was serious but expensive and foreign in spirit. They found a third model in , which had shown after the war that a poor country could make great films on real streets with non-professional actors. applied that lesson in (1955), which follows five boys from a who sell peanuts around Copacabana, Sugarloaf Mountain and the Maracanã stadium on a hot Sunday. He followed it with (1957). Many of the younger directors came to see him as the 's father.

The wider country was also changing fast. In the late 1950s, under President , Brazil was building and promising 'fifty years of progress in five'. At the same time, millions of people in the northeast lived in extreme poverty, and peasant leagues were organising for land reform. was giving Brazilian music a modern, international sound. Young people in the student believed that art should help the country understand itself, and many expected real political change soon.

The idea

A camera in hand and an idea in the head

means simply 'new cinema'. It was a loose group of friends and rivals rather than a school with rules, and its members disagreed often. Its most famous slogan, usually credited to , was 'a camera in hand and an idea in the head'. The point was that a filmmaker did not need a studio, stars or a big budget. What mattered was having something urgent to say about Brazil and the will to go out and film it.

  • Real places and real poverty. The films went to the , the dry of the northeast, and to the of Rio, places that commercial cinema mostly ignored.
  • Low budgets and visible roughness. Handheld cameras, harsh natural light and rough editing were not hidden. The directors argued that a poor country's cinema should look like the conditions it came from.
  • Politics. Most of the directors were on the left. Their films dealt with landowners and peasants, hunger, religious fanaticism, bandits, populist politicians and the failures of the middle class.
  • Brazilian culture as raw material. Films drew on folk ballads, popular religion, carnival, Afro-Brazilian history and Brazilian modernist literature instead of Hollywood genres.
  • . Like the , believed the director was the author of the film, and each director had a strong personal style.

Historians usually divide the into three phases. The first, from about 1960 to 1964, focused on the rural poor and the and was full of hope that cinema could help bring revolution. The second, from the to about 1968, turned to the failures of intellectuals and the middle class, and to how the left had lost. The third, from about 1968 to 1972, after censorship tightened, turned to colourful allegory, comedy and what was called the or style.

The 's central theory came from 's 1965 manifesto, . He wrote that the hunger of Latin America is not simply an alarming symptom but the essence of its society, and that 's originality was this hunger. He argued that European audiences liked Latin American misery as an exotic subject but did not understand it, and that the only honest response to hunger was a cinema that was itself hungry, rough and even violent in form. The essay became one of the founding texts of political cinema across the developing world.

Deep dive

Black God, White Devil: the sertão will become the sea

(, 1964; the Portuguese title means 'God and the Devil in the Land of the Sun') is 's most famous film and the classic of the first phase. Manoel, a poor cowherd in the , kills the landowner who cheats him out of his share of cattle. With his wife Rosa he flees and joins Sebastião, a preacher who promises his followers that the dry will turn into sea and the sea into land. When the preacher's religious fanaticism ends in a child sacrifice, Manoel turns instead to , a , or bandit, who survives his famous leader and wants revenge on the rich. Hunting them both is , a hired killer paid by landowners and the Church.

The film draws on real history. Messianic movements had appeared many times in the , most famously at in the 1890s, and the were real outlaws of the 1920s and 1930s. tells the story like a folk ballad, with a singer narrating on the soundtrack, mixed with music by . The camera is handheld and the light is blinding. Some scenes are long and static, and others explode into sudden violence.

The film's argument is that neither religion nor banditry can save the poor, because both are blind reactions to oppression rather than conscious politics. In the final scene Manoel runs across the dry land, and the image cuts to the sea, as a song repeats that the will become the sea. Many viewers read it as a call for revolution. The film was completed just before the and competed at in May of that year. To the military government that had just taken power, it was exactly the kind of film it distrusted.

A story

After the coup: Entranced Earth and the defeated poet

On 31 March and 1 April 1964 the army removed the elected president, , who had promised land reform and was accused by his opponents of moving toward communism. The generals said they would hold power briefly. They stayed until 1985. For the directors, who had expected their country to move left, it was a shock. Their films had been made for 'the people', but the people had not risen to defend the government, and the films themselves had mostly been seen by students and art-house audiences.

's answer was (, 1967). It is set in Eldorado, an imaginary Latin American country. The poet and journalist drifts between a conservative, almost mystical politician, Porfírio Díaz, and a populist provincial governor, Vieira, who speaks for the poor but backs down when real conflict comes. In the end Díaz takes power in a , and Paulo, dying, is left with nothing but his rage. In one famous scene Paulo covers the mouth of a union leader to stop him speaking, a bitter image of intellectuals who claim to speak for the people without letting them speak.

The film was a self-portrait of the left-wing intellectual and a harsh one. Censors banned it in April 1967 for tarnishing Brazil's image. After protests from filmmakers in Brazil and France, the government allowed it to be shown, and it won the at in 1967. It had a strong effect on young artists at home. The singer later said that seeing it was a turning point for him, and the music he and launched in 1967 and 1968, , shared its chaotic, satirical mix of high and low culture.

Third phase

Cannibals and tropicalism: AI-5 and allegory

On 13 December 1968 the military government issued , known as . It closed Congress, suspended habeas corpus for political crimes and greatly expanded censorship. Arrests, torture and exile became common. and were arrested at the end of that month and later left for London. Openly political films became almost impossible to make.

The directors turned to allegory and comedy. They took inspiration from the modernist writer , whose (1928) had argued that Brazilian culture should 'eat' foreign influences and turn them into something new, as the people were said to eat their enemies. After

Legacy

's (1980) followed a travelling circus across a country being changed by television and highways. returned to Brazil, made his last feature, (, 1980), and died in 1981 at 42.

The 's influence is large. It gave Latin American cinema a theory and a confidence it had not had before, and it shaped across the developing world. Later Brazilian films about poverty and violence, such as (2002), are often compared with it, sometimes unfavourably, because critics argue that they turned hunger into slick spectacle, the very thing had warned against. also showed something that other lessons in this course return to: from to , a country's first serious look at itself on screen often comes from young people with very little money and a strong sense that the official picture is false.

Fun fact

Too hot to be true

's (1955), the film usually named as 's starting point, was banned for a time soon after it was finished. A story repeated ever since, though not well documented, says the Rio police chief who acted as censor objected to the title itself, claiming the city's average temperature had never gone above 39.6 degrees. Writers and filmmakers campaigned against the ban, and the film was released.
