Before

Before Seitō: a modern state with an old idea of women

After the Meiji Restoration of 1868, Japan's leaders rebuilt the country on Western models: a constitution, a national army, railways, factories and compulsory schooling. They did not build a modern idea of women's rights at the same pace. The Civil Code of 1898 placed every family under a male head of household with legal authority over its members, so that even the lowest-ranking man in a household had more legal power than any woman in it. A wife lost control of her property on marriage, and adultery was a crime for a wife but not for a husband.

The state's ideal woman had a name: , 'good wife, wise mother'. The educator used the phrase in 1875, and over the following decades it became the official purpose of girls' schools. A girl was educated so that she could run a household and raise loyal, healthy sons for the nation. This was a new ideal, not an ancient one, and it gave women an important role, but only inside the home. In 1890 and again in 1900, laws barred women from political meetings and political organizations. of the of 1900 made it illegal for a woman even to attend a political speech.

There had been earlier challengers. In the 1880s toured the country giving speeches for the , with titles such as 'The Government as the Force over Men, and Men as the Force over Women'. , inspired by Kishida, was jailed in 1885 for her part in a radical plot and later founded the socialist women's paper Sekai Fujin. In 1901 the poet published , a book of tanka in which a woman speaks openly about her own desire. In January 1911 the anarchist journalist was hanged after the , the only woman among the twelve executed. By 1911 there were also girls' schools producing educated, literate young women, and a market of women's magazines that mostly taught them how to be better wives.

Two other events in 1911 set the scene. The actress played in 's in Tokyo, a play that ends with a wife walking out on her husband and children. Educated audiences began to ask whether Japanese women could be Noras too. And the Swedish writer 's books on love, marriage and motherhood were being read and translated by young Japanese women, among them a graduate of Japan Women's University named .

The beginning

'In the beginning, woman was the sun'

The idea for a magazine written only by women came from the critic , who suggested it to . was reluctant, but her university friend Yasumochi Yoshiko pushed her, and together with Nakano Hatsuko, Kiuchi Teiko and they became the five founders of the , . The name was a translation of 'bluestocking', the English nickname for educated women that went back to an eighteenth-century London literary circle. 's mother provided much of the starting money.

The first issue appeared in September 1911. Its cover was drawn by the painter , it opened with a poem by , and its editorial manifesto was signed with a new pen name, , 'thunderbird'. The essay began with the line that made the magazine famous: 'In the beginning, woman was the sun.' was referring to Amaterasu, the sun goddess of Japanese myth. Her argument was that women had once been a source of their own light and had become a pale moon, shining only by reflecting the light of others. The task was to recover the 'hidden sun', which she described as women's own creative and spiritual power.

It is important to see what this manifesto did and did not say. It did not call for the vote, for changes to the Civil Code or for equal pay. was a practising Zen Buddhist, and her first concern was inner freedom: that a woman should think, write and live as a full person. The society's first rules described its purpose as 'the development of women's literature'. Even so, the first printing of 1,000 copies sold, and according to the English Wikipedia account the office received around 3,000 letters in the first month asking for subscriptions and advice. Many young women were clearly waiting for something like it.

The 'new woman'

How the press made Seitō a scandal

In 1912 wrote in the magazine about drinking a 'five-coloured liquor', a layered cocktail, and about a visit to the Yoshiwara pleasure quarter with her uncle, the painter Otake Chikuha. She also wrote openly about her close attachment to . None of this was very shocking by later standards, but the newspapers seized on it. From May 1912 into the next year many papers and magazines ran mocking features about , the 'new woman'. At first the phrase had meant an awakened woman like 's . Now it came to mean a woman who drank, went where she should not and had loose morals.

Respected educators joined in. , founder of the women's English college that later became Tsuda University, forbade her students to take part in , and , founder of Japan Women's University, criticized the 'new woman' publicly. left the society under pressure. The April 1912 issue was banned by the Home Ministry because of a short story by about a married woman's affair, and the society was asked to leave the Mozume family house.

answered back. In January and February 1913 it published special supplements titled 'The and Other Women's Problems'. Several male writers defended the magazine, but the February issue was banned because an essay by the veteran activist was judged socialist. In April 1913 published 'To the Women of the World', which rejected the ideal. As translated on Wikipedia, she wrote: 'I wonder how many women have, for the sake of financial security in their lives, entered into loveless marriages to become one man's lifelong servant and prostitute.'

The attacks had an unexpected effect. Because the press insisted on treating as a feminist magazine, its members began to think of it that way themselves. In October 1913 rewrote the society's rules. Its purpose was no longer 'the development of women's literature' but 'to promote the awakening of women'. Literature remained, but the centre of the magazine had moved to women's lives.

In its pages

Chastity, abortion and prostitution: the Itō Noe years

In January 1914 left her parents' house to live with , five years younger than her, without marrying him. She published an open letter to her parents explaining why. Some writers praised it and others attacked her. Busy with a new household and short of money, she could not produce the September 1914 issue at all. , then nineteen, produced the November issue and told she would edit the magazine only if she were given complete control. From the January 1915 issue, was hers.

announced that the society would now have no rules and no fixed policy, and that the magazine would be open to all women. Under her, became a forum for arguments that respectable magazines would not print:

  • The chastity debate. Writers including Ikuta Hanayo, and herself argued about whether chastity was a real moral value or a rule that men imposed on women, and what a poor woman should do if survival and chastity came into conflict.
  • The abortion debate. 's piece on abortion led the Home Ministry to ban the June 1915 issue. Abortion was a crime in Japan, and the state treated women's fertility as a national resource.
  • The prostitution debate. argued that most prostitutes were poor women trying to survive and should not be punished. , then a young socialist, replied that licensed prostitution was a system of exploitation built on poverty and had to be abolished, not excused.

These debates mattered because they treated women's sexuality, bodies and economic survival as public questions that women themselves could discuss. They also show that never had a single line. Its writers disagreed sharply and argued in print.

The magazine could not last. Distributors had been pressured to stop carrying it, money was short, and 's private life was in turmoil. The 52nd and last issue appeared in February 1916. In April left her husband, the writer , for the anarchist , who was also involved with his wife and with another former member, . In November 1916 stabbed at a country inn. He survived, and the episode became one more story the press used to discredit the 'new women'.

The big argument

The motherhood protection debate (1918–1919)

Two years after closed, its former writers had their most important argument. The question was simple to state and hard to answer: if women are to be free, should a mother earn her own living, or should society pay her for raising children? The debate is called the , the .

It began in March 1918 with a short essay by in the magazine Fujin Kōron. argued that a woman should not marry or have children until she could support herself. Feminists already called it 'slave morality' for a wife to live off her husband in return for bearing his children, she wrote, and for the same reason women should refuse to live off the state. She accepted state help for the very poor, but she saw general state payments to mothers as a new form of dependence. had raised eleven children of her own, so she was not speaking as an outsider to motherhood.

, who had now had two children herself and had been deeply influenced by , disagreed. She argued that bearing and raising children was not a private matter but social and national work, because the nation's future depended on it. Officials, soldiers and teachers received salaries from the state without anyone calling them dependants, she wrote, so mothers who received state support should not be called dependants either. She also described the dilemma that working mothers still recognize: a woman who tries to be a good mother cannot be a good worker, and a woman who tries to be a good worker cannot be a good mother. Telling poor working women not to have children until they were independent, she said, meant telling most of them never to have children at all.

Two more writers joined. agreed with but went further: she believed women's true calling was the home and that husbands and the state owed mothers payment. then analysed both main positions. She pointed out that nobody called a paid nanny a dependant, so a mother raising her own children was also doing valuable unpaid work. She granted that both and had part of the truth. But she argued that neither economic independence under the existing system nor state protection would solve the problem, because the problem came from capitalism itself, which needed women as cheap labour and as unpaid carers at the same time.

The debate is still worth studying because its three positions are still with us. 's position resembles the liberal argument that women's freedom depends on paid work and financial independence. 's resembles arguments for paid parental leave, child allowances and the social value of care, which you met in the care ethics lesson. 's socialist argument that domestic work is unpaid labour anticipates the Wages for Housework campaign of the 1970s by more than half a century.

Into politics

From magazine to movement

Around 1919 investigated textile factories in Nagoya and was shocked by the conditions of the young women working there. The and the factory visits convinced her that inner awakening was not enough and that women needed political rights. With and she founded the , which began public work in 1920.

Because banned women from political meetings, the association had to campaign through 'lecture meetings' and petitions to the Diet. It had two main demands. The first was to amend . In 1922 the Diet did so, allowing women to attend and organize political meetings, though not to join political parties. The second demand, which failed, was a law to stop men with venereal disease from marrying and to let wives of infected men divorce them. presented it as protection for wives against a double standard that allowed men sexual freedom while demanding purity from women. Historians also note that she framed it in eugenic terms, as protecting the health of the Japanese 'race', and this remains a controversial part of her record.

The association dissolved in 1922. went to the United States, where she sought out the American suffrage leader , and in 1924 helped found the Women's Suffrage League, which campaigned for the vote through the 1920s and 1930s. During the war she worked within government-sponsored organizations supporting the war effort, a part of her life that is also debated. Women finally received the vote in November 1945 under the American occupation, and in April 1946 Japanese women voted for the first time and elected 39 women to the lower house.

A turning point

The murder of Itō Noe

On 1 September 1923 the destroyed much of Tokyo and Yokohama. In the chaos that followed, vigilantes, police and soldiers killed thousands of Korean residents and a number of left-wing activists. On 16 September military police led by Lieutenant arrested , and 's six-year-old nephew Tachibana Munekazu. All three were killed in custody without trial. Accounts differ on exactly how they died, but all agree it was a deliberate execution. was 28.

The court-martial showed how far public opinion had turned against radicals. 's lawyers argued that he had acted out of patriotism, spectators called him a hero, and thousands signed petitions for leniency. He was sentenced to ten years, the sentence was reduced in 1924, and he was released under an amnesty in October 1926. , who had edited Japan's first feminist magazine at twenty, became a symbol of how dangerous it was for a woman to challenge both the family system and the state.

Elsewhere

What was happening elsewhere at the same time

Britain. The years of were the most militant years of the British suffrage campaign. The , led by and her daughters, broke windows, set fires and went on hunger strike between about 1909 and 1914. 's writers read about them, but their own strategy was very different, partly because Japanese law did not even let women attend a political meeting. British women over thirty who met a property qualification won the vote in 1918, and all women over 21 in 1928. The 'New Woman' itself was a British and American idea of the 1890s, used by writers such as to describe educated, independent women, and Japanese newspapers borrowed the phrase.

China. 's arrived in China a few years after she arrived in Japan, through the New Culture Movement. In June 1918 the magazine published a special issue, in which praised as a model of the independent individual. In December 1923 gave a lecture at the Beijing Women's Higher Normal School called 'What Happens After Leaves Home?'. His answer was blunt: without money of her own, would either fall into poverty or go back. He told the students that economic rights mattered more than inspiring speeches. It is almost the same question that and had argued about in Tokyo five years earlier. The ideal of the 'good wife, wise mother' had also travelled from Japan to China, where and others criticized it.

Korea. Korea was a Japanese colony from 1910, and many Korean students studied in Tokyo. One of them, the painter and writer , was studying Western oil painting at the Tokyo Arts College for Women during 's years and admired . In 1918 she published 'Kyonghui', a story about a young woman who rejects the path to find her own life. She took part in the March 1st independence movement of 1919 and was jailed, and in the 1920s she wrote for , 'New Woman', the first magazine for Korean women. Korean 'new women' faced the same ridicule as the writers, with the added pressure of colonial rule.

The comparison shows that was part of an East Asian conversation, not just a copy of Western feminism. The same figures, , and the 'new woman', appeared in Tokyo, Beijing and Seoul within about a decade, and in each place women and men argued about whether a woman's freedom began with her inner awakening, her paycheque or her country's politics.

After

What followed

In the short term, failed. It lasted four and a half years, it was banned repeatedly, and the press turned 'new woman' into an insult. Its members went in very different directions: into reform politics and later the peace movement, into anarchism, into socialism, and into the long suffrage campaign. One Japanese view, quoted on the Japanese Wikipedia, is that the magazine 'did not play a large role in literary history, but its significance in impressing the woman question on society was great'.

In the long term, it changed what Japanese women could say in public. It was the first place where women discussed chastity, abortion, prostitution, love and motherhood in their own voices and argued with each other about them. The line 'In the beginning, woman was the sun' is still one of the most quoted sentences in Japanese feminist history.

For Taiwan, there is a direct link with an earlier lesson. When Taiwan's women's movement began under martial law in the 1970s and 1980s, it faced many of the same questions had faced: whether to work through literature or law, whether to stress women's independence or the protection of mothers,.
