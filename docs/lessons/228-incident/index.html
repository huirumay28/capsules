Before

From Japanese rule to Nationalist takeover: a colony changes hands

Taiwan had been a Japanese colony since 1895. By 1945, two generations of Taiwanese had grown up under Japanese rule. Many spoke Japanese better than any Chinese language. Schools taught the imperial curriculum. During the Pacific War, young Taiwanese men were conscripted into Japanese forces or sent to work in Southeast Asia. When Japan surrendered on 15 August 1945, the island's fate was decided in distant capitals: the Cairo Declaration of 1943 had promised that Taiwan would be returned to the Republic of China.

On 25 October 1945, in Taipei Zhongshan Hall, General accepted the Japanese surrender on behalf of Generalissimo and the Allied Powers. The day is still known as . Many Taiwanese lined the streets to welcome what they called the 'motherland.' Some had never lived under Chinese rule but had been taught to regard China as their ancestral home. Others, especially the educated elite, hoped that would mean an end to colonial discrimination and a chance to participate in governing their own island.

Those hopes faded within months. was named Chief Executive of the , a structure that concentrated power in his hands. The Nationalist officials and soldiers who arrived from the mainland were often unprepared for Taiwan's relatively developed economy and infrastructure. Taiwanese who had expected to be treated as equals in a Chinese Republic found themselves excluded from senior posts and treated as suspect because they had lived under Japanese rule. 's government extended the Japanese-era monopoly system to new sectors and used it to enrich Nationalist officials and mainlander entrepreneurs. The controlled tobacco, alcohol, camphor, matches and other goods, and its agents had the power to confiscate contraband and arrest violators.

Meanwhile, inflation soared. Taiwan's economy had been integrated into Japan's wartime system; cut those links abruptly, and the Nationalist government was more interested in extracting resources to fund the civil war on the mainland than in managing the island's recovery. Rice, which had been plentiful under Japanese rule, became scarce as the government requisitioned it for shipment to the mainland. Unemployment rose. Official corruption was open and pervasive. The language barrier made things worse: most Taiwanese spoke Hokkien (also called Taiwanese), Hakka or indigenous languages, while Nationalist officials spoke Mandarin and often looked down on those who did not. In schools and government offices, Japanese was replaced by Mandarin, but few Taiwanese had been taught it. Civil-service exams were conducted in Mandarin and Classical Chinese, which excluded most Taiwanese from government jobs.

By early 1947, resentment was widespread. Taiwanese used the term 'dogs go, pigs come' () to describe the transition: the Japanese, they said, had been vicious like dogs, but the Nationalists were greedy like pigs. In the countryside, rice riots broke out. In the cities, workers and students organized protests. Newspapers criticized the government's monopolies and corruption. The was especially hated. Its agents, many of them poorly paid and corrupt themselves, enforced rules that ordinary people saw as arbitrary and extractive. That tension, built over seventeen months, finally exploded on the evening of 27 February 1947.

The idea

Why the 228 Incident shaped Taiwanese identity

The is often called the defining trauma of modern Taiwan. It shaped politics, memory and identity in ways that are still visible today. Understanding why requires looking at what it destroyed, what it silenced and what it left behind.

  • It decapitated a generation of Taiwanese elites. Lawyers, doctors, teachers, journalists, local officials and civic leaders were arrested and killed in the crackdown. Many had been educated in Japan and saw themselves as bridges between Taiwan's past and its Chinese future. Their disappearance left a gap in civil society that the Nationalist government filled with mainlander appointees and a climate of fear.
  • It created the (, 'people of this province') and (, 'people of outside provinces') divide. Before 1947, tensions between Taiwanese and were about culture, language and economic competition. After 1947, they were also about violence and victimhood. For decades, Taiwanese families carried the memory of fathers, sons and brothers killed or disappeared. The Nationalist government, dominated by who had fled the Communist victory in 1949, treated any discussion of the incident as sedition.
  • It inaugurated the . , imposed island-wide in May 1949, lasted until 1987. During those 38 years, tens of thousands of people were imprisoned, executed or disappeared for real or imagined opposition to the . The was the precedent: anyone who spoke of it, commemorated it or questioned the official silence could be accused of fomenting unrest or aiding the Communists.
  • It made Taiwan's relationship with 'China' a question of survival, not just identity. Many Taiwanese had welcomed in 1945 because they believed in a shared Chinese culture. The and the decades of repression that followed taught a different lesson: that a government from China could be as oppressive as a colonial power from Japan, and that Taiwan's autonomy was a matter of life and death.
  • It forced the question: whose history is this? For more than forty years, the Nationalist government controlled the narrative. Official accounts blamed Communist agitators and Taiwanese 'hoodlums.' Victims' families were too frightened to speak. When ended in 1987, the fight to recover the truth became part of Taiwan's democratization. The , the 1992 government report, 's 1995 apology and the establishment of memorials and museums were all steps in a long process of turning private grief into public memory.

Historians today also emphasize the incident's gendered dimension. Most of the visible victims were men, but women carried much of the burden afterward. Daughters and widows such as , whose father was taken and never returned, spent decades searching for answers, collecting testimonies and preserving documents. herself, the cigarette vendor whose beating sparked the incident, was a woman trying to support her family. The shaped not only Taiwan's political identity but also its understanding of state violence, family trauma and the long work of remembering.

In depth

From a cigarette vendor's beating to island-wide crackdown

The evening of 27 February 1947 was cold and damp. At around 7:30 PM, six agents and four police officers, acting on a tip about contraband tobacco, arrived at the area near the on Yanping North Road in Taipei's Dadaocheng district. Most of the vendors had already fled, but they found , a widow in her early forties, selling loose cigarettes. The agents confiscated her cigarettes and her money. She begged them to return her goods. She had children to feed, she said. The agents refused. When she tried to grab back her money, one of them, Ye De-gen, struck her on the head with his gun barrel. She collapsed, bleeding.

A crowd gathered. People shouted at the agents. The agents tried to leave, but the crowd followed. One agent, Fu Xue-tong, panicked and fired his pistol. The bullet struck , a man who had been watching from the second-floor window of his home. Chen died the next day in hospital. The crowd, now enraged, overturned and burned the agents' truck. The agents fled to a police station and then to the garrison headquarters. By late evening, crowds were beating drums and gongs through the streets, telling everyone what had happened.

The next morning, 28 February, thousands of people gathered. Some marched to the 's Taipei office and burned its inventory. Others went to the Chief Executive's Office to demand punishment of the agents. Around noon, protesters tried to enter the Executive Office compound. Guards on the roof opened fire with machine guns. Several protesters were killed. By the afternoon, anger had spread across the city. was declared in Taipei.

Over the next few days, the unrest spread to every major city and town in Taiwan. In Kaohsiung, Tainan, Taichung and Chiayi, crowds seized government buildings, police stations and radio stations. Some armed themselves with weapons taken from armories or police posts. There were pitched battles. In Chiayi, armed civilians besieged Nationalist troops at Shuishang Airport and cut off their water and electricity. Many were beaten or killed.

At the same time, local elites tried to channel the unrest into negotiation. On 2 March, a was formed in Taipei, bringing together Provincial Assembly members, business leaders, students and others. agreed in a radio broadcast to release detained protesters, compensate victims and consider reforms. Similar committees formed across the island. For a few days, there was hope. The committee held public meetings and drafted demands. During the 7 March session, it presented 32 articles calling for provincial autonomy, elected local officials, freedom of speech and assembly, and the abolition of the Garrison Command. During that session, about ten further demands were reportedly added by infiltrators, bringing the list to 42. rejected the demands that afternoon; read them on the radio at 6 PM.

was stalling. He had already cabled Generalissimo on 2 March, reporting that Communist agents had instigated an uprising and requesting troops. Chiang approved. On the evening of 8 March, about 2,000 soldiers of the landed at Keelung. On 10 March, Chiang told a memorial service in Nanjing that the Settlement Committee's demands were unconstitutional and that Communists were behind the trouble. That same day, declared the committee illegal and was reimposed. The crackdown began.

Troops fanned out across Taiwan. The campaign was called 'clearing the countryside' (). In Taipei, Kaohsiung, Tainan, Taichung and Chiayi, soldiers arrested civic leaders, intellectuals, journalists and anyone suspected of involvement in the committees or the protests. Many were taken from their homes at night by men in civilian clothes who claimed to be from the police or the military. Most never returned. Their families were too frightened to report the disappearances or to search openly. The arrests continued into May.

Some victims are known by name because they were prominent. On 11 March, , the dean of arts at National Taiwan University, was taken from his house in his pajamas and never seen again. That same day, , who had read the committee's demands on the radio, was arrested; accounts say he was burned alive. In Tainan, , a lawyer who had helped mediate between residents and troops, was arrested, tortured and executed in public on 13 March at the site that is now Memorial Park. In Chiayi, the painter and three other city councilors were executed in front of the railway station on 25 March after they had gone to the airport under a flag of truce to negotiate with the troops.

How many died? No one knows for certain. At the time, the government gave no overall figure. In 1992, a report by an Executive Yuan task force, whose demographic appendix by Chen Kuan-cheng compared total deaths in Taiwan in 1947 and 1948 and attributed the excess male deaths in 1947 to the , estimated roughly 18,000 to 28,000 deaths. That figure is still contested. Some scholars say it is too high and that much of the excess mortality in 1947 was due to disease, not violence. By 2018, about 2,300 compensation claims had been approved out of roughly 2,900 applications, though many victims' descendants did not know what had happened or were still too afraid to come forward.

What is not contested is that the violence destroyed Taiwan's emerging civil society. The intellectuals, lawyers, doctors, journalists and local officials who had tried to negotiate with were killed, imprisoned or driven into silence. The Nationalist government filled the gap with mainlander appointees and a security apparatus that made dissent nearly impossible. On 19 May 1949, was formally imposed on all of Taiwan. It would last 38 years.

A story

Ruan Mei-shu and forty-five years of searching

was nineteen years old and newly married when her father disappeared. had been general manager of the Taiwan Shin Sheng Daily News, a government-run newspaper that covered the transition from Japanese to Nationalist rule. When the broke out, he was at home, sick with asthma.

On 12 March 1947, and her husband came to visit her parents. It was noon. Her mother had just stepped out to buy food. Five men in civilian clothes rang the doorbell. They said they needed to speak to about newspaper business. When brought her father out, still in his pajamas, the men said he had to come with them immediately. There was no time even to change clothes. Her father put on a coat and left. Her ten-year-old sister cried. 'Don't cry,' he told her. 'I'll be back soon.' Those were his last words to the family.

and her mother searched everywhere. They asked friends, went to police stations and military offices, and pleaded with anyone who might have information. No agency would admit to holding him. They even wrote to . He replied that was an important figure and that if the government knew where he was, it would send troops to rescue him. The answer was a lie. In 1960, learned from Ong Iok-tek's book The Bitter History of Taiwan that her father had been executed for rebellion.

's life became a search for the truth. She began interviewing other victims' families and recorded their testimonies on video. She collected documents, photographs and newspaper clippings. In 1990, she put her collection on public display. In 1994 she published Forty-Five Years of Solitary Anguish, about her father and her own life, and in 1996 Voices from the Dark Corners, interviews with victims' families.

On 23 March 2003, at her family's ancestral hall in Pingtung, she opened the 228 Memorial Museum, where she displayed the materials she had collected. The museum closed on 30 June 2006, and its holdings were later split among Taiwan Theological College, Aletheia University Madou campus and other institutions. died in 2016, at the age of 88. Her life's work ensured that the testimonies of the victims and their families would not be lost.

Elsewhere

1946–1948: upheaval across Asia and the world

The took place during a period of violent transition across much of Asia and beyond. The end of World War II and the collapse of colonial empires created a vacuum that new governments, liberation movements and rival ideologies rushed to fill. Taiwan's tragedy was part of a larger pattern.

  • China: The Chinese Civil War, suspended during the war against Japan, resumed in 1946. By early 1947, the Nationalist government was already losing ground to the Communists. Inflation was out of control, and corruption was widespread. The request for troops that sent to pulled resources away from the civil war at a time when the Nationalists could ill afford it.
  • Korea: In 1945, Korea was divided along the 38th parallel, with Soviet forces in the north and American forces in the south. By 1947, the peninsula was moving toward two separate states. On Jeju Island, tensions between leftists and the U.S.-backed south Korean authorities led to the in April 1948, a rebellion and crackdown that killed tens of thousands. Like the , it was suppressed as a Communist plot and remained a taboo for decades.
  • India and Pakistan: On 15 August 1947, British India was partitioned into India and Pakistan. The partition triggered mass migrations and communal violence in which hundreds of thousands, possibly more than a million, people were killed. The violence was rooted in religious divisions, but like the , it was also about who would control the new state and who would be included or excluded.
  • Indonesia: The Dutch tried to reassert control over the Dutch East Indies after Japan's surrender. Indonesian nationalists declared independence in 1945, and a war followed. The Dutch launched two 'police actions' in 1947 and 1948 before finally recognizing Indonesian independence in 1949. Tens of thousands died in the fighting.
  • Vietnam: In 1946, the First Indochina War began between French colonial forces and the Viet Minh independence movement. The war lasted until 1954 and killed hundreds of thousands. Like Taiwan, Vietnam was caught between a departing colonial power and rival claimants to rule.
  • Greece: From 1946 to 1949, Greece was torn apart by a civil war between the government, backed by Britain and the United States, and Communist-led forces. The war killed tens of thousands and displaced hundreds of thousands more. It was one of the first Cold War proxy conflicts.

What these events had in common was a collision between local populations with their own hopes for the future and outside forces—whether colonial powers trying to return, new governments imposing their rule, or Cold War patrons backing their clients—that were willing to use violence to impose order. In Taiwan, the Nationalist government treated the island as a base to be exploited and a population to be controlled. The was the result, and the that followed was the method by which the government ensured there would be no second uprising.

After

Martial law, silence, and the long road to remembering

After the crackdown ended in May 1947, Taiwan fell silent. On 19 May 1949, was formally imposed. It would last until 15 July 1987, making it one of the longest periods of in modern history. Under , the government banned opposition parties, censored the press, and gave military courts the power to try civilians. Tens of thousands of people were arrested during the for real or imagined opposition to the . Many were accused of being Communists or Communist sympathizers. Some were tortured. Some were executed. Some spent decades in prison on remote islands like Green Island.

The itself became a forbidden subject. In schools, it was not taught. In families, even mentioning it could bring suspicion. Parents told their children not to ask. Victims' families lived with the knowledge that their fathers, brothers or sons had been killed, but they could not speak of it openly or demand justice. The silence was enforced not only by law but by fear. Everyone knew what had happened to those who challenged the government.

did not escape punishment, but not for his role in the . He was dismissed as Chief Executive in April 1947 and appointed governor of Zhejiang Province in 1948. In January 1949, as the Nationalist government was collapsing on the mainland, he wrote letters to other Nationalist commanders proposing to negotiate a 'regional peace' with the Communists. The letters were intercepted and shown to . was arrested, brought to Taiwan, tried for treason, and executed by firing squad on 18 June 1950 in Xindian (then Taipei County, now Xindian District, New Taipei). His execution was for trying to defect, not for the deaths in 1947, and the victims' families got no apology.

It was not until the 1980s, as Taiwan began its slow transition to democracy, that the silence started to break. In 1986, the Taiwan Association for Human Rights held a roundtable on the . The following year, in the fortieth anniversary year, three men—, a magazine publisher; , a psychiatrist; and , a lawyer—founded the . On 15 February 1987, they organized the first public march for the victims in Tainan, starting at the site where had been executed. It was the first time anyone had openly commemorated the incident in Taiwan since 1947.

Over the following weeks, the society held public events across Taiwan: lectures, prayer services and marches. They demanded that the government disclose the truth, apologize, compensate victims, designate 28 February as , and build a memorial. The demands were radical for a country still under , and the marches were met with police and sometimes violence. But they succeeded in breaking the taboo. Newspapers began covering the story. Families of victims began to come forward. In 1991, the Executive Yuan set up a research task force.

The breakthrough came on 28 February 1995. At the unveiling of the in Taipei's New Park, President , Taiwan's first native-born president, publicly apologized. It was the first official apology. The was promulgated on 7 April 1995 and took effect on 9 April 1995. It established a compensation process, designated 28 February as , and set up the to investigate the incident and support victims' families.

In 1996, New Park was renamed . In 1997, the former radio station building inside the park opened as the Taipei 228 Memorial Museum, and 28 February became a national holiday. Other memorials followed across Taiwan: in Chiayi, Kaohsiung, Tainan and elsewhere. In 2018, the was established to investigate authoritarian-era crimes, exonerate victims and preserve archives. By then, about 2,300 compensation claims had been approved out of roughly 2,900 applications, though the true number of victims will never be known.

The remains contested. For some, it is the origin story of Taiwanese identity, the moment when Taiwanese learned that they could not trust a government from China. For others, it is a tragedy that should be remembered but not politicized. The , which ruled Taiwan until 2000 and again from 2008 to 2016, has apologized repeatedly but remains sensitive to the charge that it committed a massacre. The Chinese Communist Party, which was not involved in 1947, now describes the incident as 'a just action by Taiwan compatriots against autocratic rule' and accuses Taiwan's pro-independence politicians of distorting it. In Taiwan itself, the is taught in schools, commemorated every year, and studied by historians. It is no longer a forbidden subject. But the wound it left, and the silence that followed, are still part of what it means to be Taiwanese.

One question

How do you remember a massacre when the state that committed it still governs you?

For more than forty years, the government forbade public discussion of the , even as the same party continued to rule Taiwan. When ended and democratization began, victims and their families faced a choice: demand full accountability, including criminal prosecution of those responsible, or accept apologies, compensation and memorialization in exchange for not pressing charges. Taiwan chose the second path. President apologized, but no one was prosecuted. The exonerated victims and declassified archives, but it did not name perpetrators in a way that would lead to trials. Was this compromise necessary for Taiwan's peaceful transition to democracy, or did it leave the work of justice unfinished? And if a society cannot or will not prosecute the authors of its own trauma, what does it mean to say it has faced the past?

Fun fact

A City of Sadness broke the silence

When 's film premiered at the Venice Film Festival in September 1989 and won the Golden Lion, it was the first major film to depict the . had ended in Taiwan only two years earlier, in July 1987, and the incident was still a deeply sensitive subject. Taiwanese audiences flocked to see it when it opened in October 1989, both because it was a prize-winning film and because it was the first time they could see the forbidden history on screen. The film's success helped legitimize public discussion of the and paved the way for the redress movement of the 1990s.
