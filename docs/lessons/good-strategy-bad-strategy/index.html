Before

Why a new definition was needed

By the 2000s, strategy had two very different meanings in business. In the academic and consulting world, built on Michael Porter's five forces and value chain, strategy meant a specific argument about how a firm would compete. In everyday corporate life, however, 'strategy' had become the name for an annual planning document. That document usually opened with a vision, a mission and a list of values, and then listed goals such as growing revenue by a certain percentage. called this , and he noticed that schools, city governments and national-security agencies had adopted it as eagerly as companies had.

, a professor at UCLA's Anderson School of Management who had also worked as a consultant, first used the phrase 'bad strategy' in 2007 at a seminar in Washington, DC, on national-security strategy. He had been invited to describe how seriously businesses approached strategy. Instead he told the audience that, in his experience, was spreading. Over the following years he refined the idea with senior executives, and in 2011 he published it in and in the book : The Difference and Why It Matters.

His starting point was simple. A strategy is a way through a difficulty. If you cannot name the difficulty, you cannot judge whether a strategy is good, and you cannot improve it. Ambition, vision and hard work all matter, but they are not substitutes for an argument about how to overcome a specific obstacle.

The framework

The kernel: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions

says every good strategy contains a with three parts. The order matters, because each part depends on the one before it.

  • : an explanation of the nature of the challenge. A good simplifies an overwhelming situation by deciding which few aspects are critical. It answers the question 'What is really going on here?'
  • : an overall approach chosen to cope with or overcome the obstacles named in the . It is not a goal. It tells people which kinds of action are in and which are out, much like a signpost rather than a destination.
  • : concrete steps that are coordinated with one another so that they support the . insists these are not mere 'implementation details'. They are where the strategy actually does its work, and they should reinforce each other rather than pull in different directions.

Two ideas sit behind the . The first is . Good strategy finds one or two pivot points in a situation where concentrated effort produces a much larger effect, and then puts resources there. The second is choice. Because resources are limited, choosing one path means giving up others. 's line, quoted by in Bloomberg View, is that strategy is scarcity's child: to have a strategy rather than vague aspirations is to choose one path and turn away from the rest.

He illustrates the whole with the in 1805. The British admiral was outnumbered by a combined French and Spanish fleet. The usual tactic was for two fleets to sail in parallel lines and fire broadsides. 's was that the enemy gunners were less experienced and would struggle in the heavy swell that day. His was to break the enemy line and turn the battle into a close melee where British experience would count. The were to split his fleet into two columns and drive them straight into the enemy line, accepting great risk for the lead ships. According to , the French and Spanish lost 22 ships and the British lost none, although himself was killed.

Bad strategy

Four hallmarks, and why they keep appearing

condensed into four hallmarks. The first is failure to face the challenge. In July 1979 produced a detailed corporate strategic plan built from five divisional plans. It projected that the company's share of agricultural equipment would rise from 16 to 20 percent and drew a profit forecast shaped like a hockey stick. It did not mention the company's grossly inefficient factories or its labour relations, which calls the worst in US industry, even though those were why its profit margin had long been about half of its rivals'. After a long strike the company declined, sold its agricultural business to Tenneco, and what remained became the truck maker .

The second hallmark is mistaking goals for strategy. describes a CEO he calls , who ran a graphic-arts company and had a '20/20 plan': 20 percent annual revenue growth and a profit margin of at least 20 percent. When asked what strength or industry change would make that possible, Logan answered with the will to win and a quotation from about reaching for the impossible. points out that also said that if you do not have a competitive advantage, you should not compete. A goal tells people where you want to end up. It does not tell them how to get past the obstacle in the way.

The third hallmark is bad strategic objectives. One version is the 'dog's dinner', a long list of things to do produced by collecting every stakeholder's wishes. met the mayor of a small city in the Pacific Northwest whose plan contained 47 strategies and 178 action items. The other version is the 'blue-sky' objective, which simply restates the desired outcome and is just as hard to achieve as the original challenge. Good objectives, by contrast, are few, pivotal and reachable with the resources the organization actually has.

The fourth hallmark is : buzzwords that dress up the obvious. quotes a retail bank whose internal memo said its fundamental strategy was 'customer-centric intermediation'. Intermediation means taking deposits and lending them out. Remove the jargon, and the sentence says that the bank's strategy is to be a bank.

Why is so common? gives two main reasons. One is the inability to choose. At a 1992 meeting of senior executives at (), one group wanted to keep building integrated computer systems, a second wanted to sell customer 'solutions', and a third wanted to focus on chips. Chief executive asked them to reach consensus, but each option was preferred by a majority over one of the others, a cycle known as . They settled on a statement that was committed to high-quality products and services and to being a leader in data processing. That was a political compromise, not a strategy. was replaced in June 1992 by , who chose chips, but by then it was too late. Compaq bought in 1998. The other reason is , the habit of filling in vision, mission, values and 'strategies' that are really aspirations.

Cases

How Nvidia, Apple, Walmart and a corner grocery used the kernel

In 1995 the start-up took the lead in fast 3-D chips for gamers, and there were rumours that Intel would enter the market too. 's summary of the is: 'We are losing the performance race.' 's was to release a faster, better chip every six months instead of the industry's usual eighteen months. The made that cycle possible: three development teams working on overlapping schedules, heavy investment in simulation and emulation so that chip fabrication and software drivers would not cause delays, and, over time, taking control of driver development back from the board makers. Intel launched a 3-D chip in 1998 but left the discrete graphics-chip business soon after, 's creditors started bankruptcy proceedings in 2000, and Forbes named its Company of the Year in 2007. adds a caution in a footnote: rivals such as ATI caught up later, so even a good strategy does not last forever.

Conventional retail wisdom said each store should be managed as its own profit centre. argues that 's insight was to treat the integrated network of stores and distribution centres, not the individual store, as the operating unit. Clustering stores in small towns around a warehouse let serve places that rivals thought were too small, at costs that rivals could not match. The and the actions followed from seeing the situation differently, not from working harder at the old model.

The smallest case is the most useful for a new venture. describes a friend, Stephanie, who ran a corner grocery near a university and faced a large 24-hour supermarket nearby. Her was that she could not beat the supermarket on range or price. Her was to serve busy professionals who had little time to cook. The were to stock more prepared meals and to open an extra checkout at 5 p.m. The strategy also told her what to stop doing. More prepared food meant less shelf space for snacks bought by students, and concentrating staff at the evening peak meant closing earlier and giving up late-night sales. That loss is the sign of a real choice.

After

How the idea is used now, and its limits

became one of the most widely recommended strategy books of the 2010s, partly because its test is easy to use in a meeting. You can take any plan and ask three questions: what is the , what is the , and do the actions fit together? Many plans fail at the first question. In 2022 followed up with , which focuses on finding of a challenge, the part that is both most important and actually solvable.

The fits well with other frameworks in this series. Five forces and the value chain help with . Playing to Win's cascade of where to play and how to win is close to a . Seven Powers describes the durable advantages that can build. What adds is a filter against self-deception: a reminder that a goal, a mission statement or a list of initiatives is not yet a strategy.

Critics note that the framework is easier to apply in hindsight than in advance. It is simple to call 's plan bad once the company has collapsed, and to call 's plan good once it has won. 's cases are also mostly his own retellings, and he says little about luck. The fair way to use the is therefore as a discipline for your own thinking rather than as a way to grade other people's past decisions.

One question

Write a kernel for cool dairy

Write a one-page for cool dairy, the vintage shop whose proceeds will support Taiwan women's organizations. Start with a : what is the single most important obstacle right now? It might be finding enough good stock, getting buyers to trust a small shop, standing out on Carousell, or making the donation story credible. Then write one that deals with that obstacle and rules some things out. Finally list three to five actions that reinforce each other. Like Stephanie's grocery, name at least one thing cool dairy will stop doing or will not do because of this choice. Then check your draft for 's four hallmarks: does it face the challenge, is it more than a goal, are the objectives few and reachable, and is there any ?
