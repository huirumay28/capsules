The concept

What learned helplessness means

describes a pattern in which a person or animal goes through bad events that it cannot , and afterwards stops trying to escape or improve its situation, even when escape has become easy. The important word is , not pain. Two people can suffer exactly the same amount of trouble, but the one who could do something about it tends to come out of it ready to act, while the one whose actions made no difference tends to come out passive, slow to learn and low in mood.

The original theory had three parts. The first was the experience: outcomes that are independent of what you do. The second was an expectation: the learner concludes that future outcomes will also be independent of its actions. The third was a set of effects: it stops starting new attempts (a motivational effect), it has trouble noticing when an action does work (a cognitive effect), and it shows signs of stress and sadness (an emotional effect). thought this triad looked very much like human depression, which is why the idea spread so quickly outside the laboratory.

is related to several ideas in earlier lessons. Like the self-fulfilling prophecy, it describes an expectation that produces the outcome it predicts: if you believe nothing will work, you stop trying, and then nothing does work. Like the fundamental attribution error and the just-world hypothesis, it is partly about explanations, because the later human version of the theory depends on how people explain why bad things happened to them. And like stereotype threat, it shows how a situation can make capable people perform below their ability.

Two cautions belong at the start. First, the theory has changed a great deal since 1967, and its own founders now say the original explanation of the animal results was backwards. Second, when the label is applied to real people, especially to women living with violence, it can describe them as passive when they are in fact making careful decisions under danger. The rest of the lesson follows both of these turns.

Case study 1

The shuttle box at Penn, 1964–1967

In the early 1960s 's laboratory at the University of Pennsylvania studied how fear conditioning combines with avoidance learning. Dogs first heard a tone paired with a shock they could not escape, and were later placed in a , a two-sided box with a low barrier in the middle, where they could escape shock by jumping to the other side. Most dogs learn this quickly. But dogs that had first received inescapable shock often did not. They lay down and whined and took the shock. When and arrived as first-year graduate students in 1964, this odd failure was a nuisance for the lab. They decided it was the interesting result.

Their 1967 paper, 'Failure to escape traumatic shock', introduced what became known as the , and it is the clearest way to see what the theory claims. There were three groups of eight dogs. Dogs in the escape group were held in a hammock and received a series of shocks that they could end by pressing a panel with their heads. Each dog in the was paired with a dog in the escape group and received exactly the same shocks, of the same length, at the same moments, but its own panel did nothing. A third group received no shock in the hammock at all. The design is clever because the escape and had identical physical experiences. The only difference was whether their behaviour mattered.

The next day every dog had ten trials in the , where jumping the barrier would end the shock. The escape group and the no-shock group did about equally well. The did badly: on average they failed to escape on about seven of the ten trials, and six of the eight failed on nine or more trials, while none of the escape-group dogs did. The shocks themselves did not explain the difference, because the escape dogs had received the same shocks. What seemed to matter was that the had experienced shock that nothing they did could stop.

In a companion paper the same year, and showed that the interference was strong but faded within about two days after a single session, and that it was not simply because the dogs had learned some physical habit like freezing. and also found what they called : dogs that first had controllable shock were later protected against the effect of uncontrollable shock. They also found that helpless dogs could be cured, slowly, by physically dragging them across the barrier until they began to move on their own.

These experiments involved painful shocks to animals, and many readers today find them hard to read about. They were approved under the standards of the 1960s. They are included here because almost everything else in the lesson grows out of them, including some ideas that later helped people.

From dogs to people

Noise, anagrams and the way people explain bad events

In 1974 adapted the design for college students. Instead of shock he used an unpleasant loud noise. One group could stop the noise by pressing a button, a heard the same noise but their button did nothing, and a third group heard no noise. Afterwards everyone faced a new device, a , where moving a hand from one side to the other stopped a new noise. The students who had faced uncontrollable noise were slower to learn the easy solution and failed more often. In 1975 and showed that the effect crossed tasks: uncontrollable noise made people worse at solving anagrams, and unsolvable problems made people worse at escaping noise.

Human results soon became messy. Some people became helpless after uncontrollable events, but others tried harder, and some became helpless only on tasks similar to the first one. The original theory could not explain why. In 1978 , and published 'Learned in Humans: Critique and Reformulation', which added a new step. When something bad happens that we cannot , we ask why. The answer we give then decides how far the spreads.

They described three dimensions of explanation. An explanation blames the self ('I am stupid'), while an one blames the situation ('the test was unfair'). A explanation points to something lasting ('I will always be bad at this'), while an unstable one points to something temporary ('I was tired'). A explanation applies to everything ('I ruin whatever I touch'), while a specific one stays local ('I am not good at statistics'). The reformulated theory predicted that people who habitually explain bad events in , and ways would be most at risk of depression. This habitual pattern became known as , or .

In 1989 , and narrowed the idea further into the of depression. In their version the key ingredient is not alone but , which means expecting that bad outcomes will happen and that nothing one does can change them. , for his part, turned the explanatory-style work around and asked whether pessimistic explanations could be taught away. That question led to his 1990 book and, later, to the movement he promoted as president of the American Psychological Association from 1998.

Children and schools

Helpless and mastery-oriented children

The educational branch of the theory came mainly from . In a 1975 study she worked with children whom teachers and tests had identified as helpless, meaning that their performance collapsed after a failure. One group simply received a run of successes. Another group was trained to explain failures as a sign of not enough effort rather than not enough ability. After the training, the children who had only experienced success still fell apart after failure, while the children who had been taught a different explanation kept going or even improved. Success alone did not cure . A new way of explaining failure did.

In 1978 and watched children think aloud while solving problems that became impossible partway through. Children in the helpless group quickly blamed their own lack of ability, used worse strategies and said they expected to fail. Children in the group hardly talked about causes at all. They told themselves to slow down and try something else, and some of them seemed to enjoy the challenge. This line of work later grew into 's popular idea of the , which has its own replication debates, but the 1970s studies are the root of it.

This matters for Huiru's interest in children's education. A child who keeps failing in a system that gives no real chance to succeed, for example a child in a school with no support for a learning difficulty, may look lazy when the more accurate description is that the child has learned, correctly, that effort has not paid off. The fix is not a pep talk. It is changing the situation so that effort does pay off, and helping the child notice when it does.

Case study 2

Learned helplessness and women living with violence

The most consequential and most contested use of the idea came in the study of domestic violence. In 1979 the psychologist published , based on interviews with women who had been abused by partners. She described a with three phases: a tension-building phase, an acute battering incident, and a phase of calm, apology and affection, after which tension builds again. She argued that repeated violence that a woman cannot predict or stop can produce something like , which helps explain why some women do not leave.

's work had a clear purpose. At the time many police officers, judges and neighbours asked a hostile question: if it was so bad, why didn't she leave? answered that the abuse itself changes how a woman sees her options. In 1984 she published , and the same year, in , the New Jersey Supreme Court held that expert testimony about could be admitted to help a jury understand a woman's belief that she was in danger when she killed her abusive husband. Similar testimony was used in many courts afterwards, and it helped some women whose self-defence claims would otherwise have been dismissed.

Feminist researchers and advocates soon criticised the framing. In 1988 and published , based on records from thousands of women in Texas shelters. They found that abused women did not usually become passive. Instead, many sought help again and again, from police, clergy, relatives and agencies, and often increased their help-seeking as the violence got worse. What kept them in danger was that the help they asked for was missing or useless. and called this the . In their view the helpless party was often the system, not the woman.

Other critics added further points. Leaving an abuser is often the most dangerous moment, so staying can be a rational safety calculation rather than a sign of passivity. Money, housing, children, immigration status and social pressure all limit choices in ways that have nothing to do with a person's psychology. And a syndrome label can backfire in court, because a woman who fought back or who planned carefully may be seen as not fitting the picture of a helpless victim. Many advocates now prefer to talk about and about the structural barriers to leaving, while still using 's cycle as one way of describing how abuse can feel from the inside.

The key lesson is about where we put the explanation. The original laboratory idea located the problem in the learner, who had stopped trying. The survivor critique moved the problem to the environment, which had stopped responding. Both can be true at once, but the second points toward a practical answer: services that actually answer the phone, give money and housing, and keep a woman safe make her efforts pay off.

Fifty years later

The founders change their minds

spent much of his later career studying the brain circuits behind the original effect in rats. In 2016 he and published 'Learned at Fifty: Insights from Neuroscience' in Psychological Review. Their conclusion was striking. The original theory said that animals learned that nothing they did mattered, and that this learning made them passive. The neuroscience suggested the opposite. Passivity after prolonged shock is not learned at all. It is the default, unlearned response of the brain to long-lasting bad events, driven by activity in a brainstem area called the , which releases serotonin and makes the animal anxious and inactive.

What is learned, they now argue, is . When an animal's actions do end the bad event, an area at the front of the brain, the , detects the and sends signals down that switch off the response. With repeated experiences of , the same circuit seems to build an , which protects the animal against later stress, even uncontrollable stress. That explains the old finding from the 1960s in a new way. In short, the theory used to say that animals learn to be helpless. Now its authors say animals start out vulnerable and learn to be in charge.

This revision changes the practical message. If passivity is the default response to long stress, then the goal is not mainly to remove a bad lesson someone has learned. It is to give people real experiences of , early and often, so that the protective circuit gets built. That fits well with the classroom research and with the survivor critique. It also fits a familiar idea in social work: means helping a person make choices that actually work, not telling her to be braver.

An earlier human study points the same way. In 1976 and worked in a Connecticut nursing home. Residents on one floor were told they were responsible for themselves, were given choices about small things such as when to watch a film, and were given a plant to look after. Residents on a comparison floor received the same kind of attention, but staff made the decisions and looked after their plants for them. The residents with more choice were rated happier and more active. In an eighteen-month follow-up published in 1977, and reported that about 15 percent of the choice group had died, compared with about 30 percent of the comparison group, although they later published a correction noting that this difference was only marginally significant. Even read cautiously, the study suggests that small, real experiences of matter.

Meanwhile in Taiwan

From private shame to protection orders

Taiwan's story shows the structural side of the debate. Before the late 1990s, violence between spouses was widely treated as a private family matter, and women who asked police or relatives for help were often told to go home and keep the peace. Women's groups, including the Modern Women's Foundation and the Awakening Foundation covered in earlier Monday lessons, campaigned for change after several widely reported cases in the 1990s.

On 24 June 1998 Taiwan passed the (), the first law of its kind in Asia. Its civil protection orders, which let a court order an abuser to stay away or leave the home, took effect one year later. In learned-helplessness terms, the law changed the environment: it gave women actions that could actually produce results, such as a that police must enforce. The predicts that when such actions really work, women will use them.

Using the idea

What it means for cool dairy and for choosing partners

For choosing partner organisations, the concept gives a useful test. Does an organisation treat the women it serves as people who need to be rescued, or as people whose own efforts it is helping to pay off? Practical signs of the second approach include helping women make their own safety plans, giving direct financial help or housing so that leaving is possible, legal advocacy that makes protection orders work, and programmes that lead to jobs and independent income. Annual reports that describe outcomes such as protection orders obtained or women housed are more informative than reports that only count hotline calls.

For donors, there is a quieter version of the same problem. Many people feel that social problems are so large that nothing they do will matter, which is a kind of about giving. Some research on charitable giving suggests that people are more willing to give when an appeal shows a specific, achievable result rather than a vast problem. A small shop like cool dairy can use this honestly by showing clearly where each purchase's donation goes and what it paid for, so that customers learn, accurately, that their action had an effect.
