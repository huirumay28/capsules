: when the memory is the product

In the July–August 1998 issue of Harvard Business Review, and argued that experiences are a fourth kind of economic offering, as different from services as services are from goods. A company that sells goods competes on features, a company that sells services competes on benefits, but a company that stages an experience sells a memory, and customers will pay a premium for it. This lesson explains the framework first: the , the and the five design principles. It then follows how Starbucks, Apple, and used, lost or misjudged experience strategy, and ends with what the idea means for a small vintage shop.

Before

From goods to services, and the problem of commoditization

For most of the twentieth century, business thinking divided the economy into three kinds of output. Agriculture and mining extracted commodities such as wheat, coffee beans and iron ore. Industry turned commodities into goods, standardized products that could be stored in a warehouse and sold later. From the 1950s onward, rich countries moved into a service economy, in which a growing share of spending went to activities performed for the customer, such as banking, repair, cleaning and restaurants.

Each step up this ladder allowed firms to charge more, because the offering was more closely tailored to what the customer needed. Each step also had the same weakness. Once many firms could make a good or deliver a service to the same standard, customers began to choose on price alone. Economists and managers call this . By the 1990s and saw it happening to services: long-distance telephone calls, for example, were sold mainly on the price per minute.

had already written about mass customization, the idea that a firm can tailor products to individual customers at close to mass-production cost. With he founded , a small consultancy in Cleveland, Ohio. Their 1998 article asked what lies beyond customized services. Their answer was that when a company uses services as the stage and goods as props to engage a person in a memorable way, it is selling something new.

The framework

The progression of economic value, four realms and five principles

and open the article with the history of the birthday cake. In the agrarian economy, mothers baked cakes from farm commodities, flour, sugar, butter and eggs, that cost mere dimes. In the industrial economy, parents paid a dollar or two to Betty Crocker for a premixed box. In the service economy, busy parents ordered a cake from a bakery or grocery store for 10 or 15 dollars, about ten times the price of the packaged ingredients. In the 1990s, the authors wrote, parents neither made the cake nor threw the party. They spent 100 dollars or more to outsource the whole event to Chuck E. Cheese's or the Discovery Zone, which staged a memorable afternoon for the children and often threw in the cake for free.

The point of the story is that each stage is a different economic offering with a different logic. The article summarizes the differences in a table:

  • Commodities belong to the agrarian economy. The economic function is to extract them, they are fungible, they are stored in bulk, the seller is a trader and the buyer is a market.
  • Goods belong to the industrial economy. The function is to make them, they are tangible and standardized, they are inventoried after production, the seller is a manufacturer and the buyer is a user who values features.
  • Services belong to the service economy. The function is to deliver them, they are intangible and customized, they are delivered on demand, the seller is a provider and the buyer is a client who values benefits.
  • Experiences belong to . The function is to stage them, they are memorable and personal, they are revealed over a duration of time, the seller is a stager and the buyer is a guest who values sensations.

The word 'guest' is borrowed from . and stress that an experience exists only in the mind of the person who has it, so no two people have exactly the same experience. They also insist that an experience is not just a marketing layer on top of a product. Their test is admission: a company has truly moved into the experience business when customers would pay to enter. They point to the Star theater complex in Southfield, Michigan, which charged about 25 per cent more for a film than a local competitor because of the fun-house experience inside, and they suggest that managers ask what they would change if they had to charge admission.

To describe the range of possible experiences, the authors use two dimensions. The first is participation: at one end, passive guests such as a symphony audience do not affect the performance; at the other, active guests such as skiers create it. The second is connection: at one end, guests absorb an event from a distance, like spectators in the grandstand at the Kentucky Derby; at the other, they are immersed in it, like the crowd standing in the infield among the sights, sounds and smells. Crossing the two dimensions gives :

  • Entertainment: passive and absorbing, such as watching a show.
  • Educational: active and absorbing, such as taking a class or a ski lesson, where the guest must engage the mind or body.
  • Escapist: active and immersive, such as playing in a casino or acting in a role-play, where the guest helps shape the event.
  • Esthetic: passive and immersive, such as standing in a gallery or a beautiful landscape, where the guest takes in the setting but does not change it.

The richest experiences, such as a trip to World or an evening in a Las Vegas casino, combine all . and call that overlap the 'sweet spot'.

Finally, the article sets out five design principles, drawn from companies that were already staging experiences:

  • Theme the experience. A clear theme, like those of the Hard Rock Cafe, or the , tells guests what to expect. Without one, the authors quote Gertrude Stein on Oakland: 'There is no there there.' Their positive example is the Forum Shops in Las Vegas, a mall built as an ancient Roman marketplace.
  • Harmonize impressions with positive cues. Every detail should support the theme. The Barista Brava coffee chain in Washington, D.C. laid out its floor tiles and counters so that customers formed a line without ropes or signs. Standard Parking decorated each floor of its O'Hare Airport garage in Chicago with a different local sports team and its own song, so that travellers would never forget where they parked.
  • Eliminate negative cues. Anything that contradicts the theme should go. The authors' example is the 'Thank You' sign on fast-food trash bins, which really says 'no service here'.
  • Mix in . People pay a premium for objects that remind them of an experience, like an official concert T-shirt printed with the date and the city. The price depends less on the cost of the shirt than on the value of the memory.
  • Engage all five senses. At the , the mist rising from the rocks is first heard, then seen, then felt on the skin, then smelled and, in a sense, tasted.

In the book that followed in April 1999, the subtitle 'Work Is Theatre and Every Business a Stage' made the metaphor explicit: employees are performers, the store is a stage, and work should be scripted and rehearsed. The authors also named a fifth offering beyond experiences, , in which the customer pays to be changed, as with a fitness programme or a course of study. Later editions of the book were published in 2011 and 2019.

Cases

Starbucks, Apple, Build-A-Bear and J.C. Penney

Starbucks is the best-known example of a company charging an experience price for a commodity. described the stores as a 'third place' between home and work, a term he borrowed from 's 1989 book , and the theatre of the barista grinding beans and pulling shots was part of what customers paid for. As the company grew, efficiency decisions slowly removed that theatre. On 14 February 2007 , then chairman, sent a memo to chief executive Jim Donald and other executives titled 'The of the Starbucks Experience'. He wrote that the growth from fewer than 1,000 stores to 13,000 had required 'a series of decisions that, in retrospect, have lead to the watering down of the Starbucks experience'.

The memo reads almost like a checklist of and 's principles being broken. Tall automatic espresso machines had solved a problem of speed, but they blocked the customer's view of the drink being made and removed what called 'the romance and theatre'. Flavour-locked bagged coffee kept beans fresh, but cost the stores their aroma, 'perhaps the most powerful non-verbal signal we had'. Streamlined store designs met investment targets but produced stores that some people called 'sterile, cookie cutter'. returned as chief executive in January 2008. On 26 February 2008 Starbucks closed about 7,100 company-operated US stores for roughly three hours in the evening to retrain baristas in making espresso, a public admission that the experience, not the coffee bean, was the product.

Apple applied the same idea to computers. When its first two stores opened on 19 May 2001, at Tysons Corner in Virginia and Glendale Galleria in California, most computers were sold in warehouse-style electronics stores of the kind and had criticized for their rows of identical appliances. , who came from Target to run Apple retail, built stores where customers could use every product, staff were not paid on commission, and the offered face-to-face help. In his November 2011 HBR blog post 'What I Learned Building the Apple Store' and the December 2011 HBR interview 'Retail Isn't Broken. Stores Are', argued that a store should exist to enrich customers' lives rather than simply to move products, which is close to and 's language of guests and staging.

, founded by , opened its first store in the St. Louis Galleria in October 1997. Customers do not buy a finished teddy bear. They choose a skin, help stuff it, place a small heart inside and dress it, and the finished bear is the souvenir of the visit. In and 's terms the store moves a goods business into the escapist realm, because the child actively takes part in making the product, and it uses as the core of the sale.

Limits

When staging goes wrong

The first limit is novelty. A theme that delights on the first visit can feel repetitive on the fifth. , one of the restaurants the 1998 article named as an example of 'eatertainment', filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection on 12 October 1999, little more than a year after the article appeared. A themed setting cannot rescue an offering that customers do not want to repeat.

The second limit is cost and scale, which is the lesson of 's memo. Experiences depend on people, time and small sensory details, exactly the things that efficiency programmes remove. Each of Starbucks' decisions was reasonable on its own; together they turned a staged experience back into a service sold on convenience.

The third limit is fit. 's Apple experience assumed customers who wanted to spend time in a beautiful store. 's core customers wanted a different experience, the hunt for a deal, and the redesign removed it. The framework tells a company to design a memorable experience, but it cannot tell the company which experience its own guests value. That still requires the kind of diagnosis covered in the lesson on good and bad strategy.

Finally, many critics and some practitioners use 'experience' loosely to mean any pleasant customer service. and themselves draw a sharper line: if an event exists only to make customers prefer a product, it is marketing, not an economic offering. The admission test remains the most useful check.

After

What the idea became

The phrase 'experience economy' passed into everyday business language. Pop-up shops, flagship stores, immersive exhibitions, brand cafés and ticketed museum-style installations all follow the logic that customers will pay for time well spent and for something to remember. In e-commerce, where goods and delivery are easily compared on price, many physical shops now justify themselves by what can only happen in person.

For a business like a vintage shop, the framework offers a useful reminder. The garment is a good, and finding, cleaning and pricing it is a service, but the reason people keep coming back may be the experience of the hunt, the story of where a piece came from and the feeling of the place. That experience can be designed, and like Starbucks' aroma, it can be lost by small decisions made for efficiency.
