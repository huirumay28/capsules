Before

Before Wages for Housework: Marx treating housework as outside production, Friedan-era framing, and second-wave consciousness-raising

Karl Marx's Capital (1867) analyzed how capitalists buy —the capacity to work—and extract surplus value from its use. Marx noted that must be reproduced daily (workers need food, rest, shelter) and generationally (new workers must be born and raised). But he treated this reproduction as outside the sphere of capitalist production proper. Women's work in the home—cooking, cleaning, childcare—appeared in Marx's account as part of the worker's consumption, not as labor that produces value. This framing set the terms for a century of Marxist theory that ignored or naturalized as a precondition for wage labor rather than as labor itself.

Mid-twentieth-century liberal feminism, exemplified by 's (1963), critiqued the confinement of middle-class American women to suburban domesticity and urged women to seek careers and fulfillment in paid work. 's diagnosis was psychological and individualist: was deadening, and liberation meant entering the public world of employment. What this framing missed—and what the would later emphasize—was that capital benefits whether women do only unpaid or whether they do both paid work outside the home and unpaid work inside it. Either way, someone has to cook, clean, and raise children, and capital refuses to pay for most of that labor.

By the late 1960s, 's radical wing was practicing consciousness-raising: small groups of women sharing personal experiences to identify systemic patterns. The slogan 'the personal is political' named the insight that what seemed like private troubles— drudgery, economic dependence on men, exhaustion—were structured by political and economic relations. This grassroots practice prepared the ground for the theoretical leap the would make: that is not a personal service women perform out of love but a form of labor that capital extracts without paying.

The concept

What Wages for Housework claimed: housework as productive labor that reproduces labor-power, unwaged and thus invisible, essential to capitalist accumulation

The argument rested on a reinterpretation of Marxist value theory. In classical Marxism, is labor that produces surplus value for capital—labor performed under capitalist relations of production for a wage. seemed to fall outside: it is unwaged, done in the private home, and its products—meals, clean clothes, cared-for children—are consumed by the family, not sold as commodities. But theorists argued that this is a mystification. does produce a commodity: itself. Workers sell their capacity to work to capitalists. That capacity must be produced and reproduced daily—workers need to be fed, clothed, rested—and generationally—children must be born, raised, trained. All of this work is done disproportionately by women, unwaged, in the home.

If produces , then it is essential to capitalist accumulation. Capital pays workers a wage supposedly sufficient to reproduce , but in fact that wage covers only the commodities purchased—food, rent, utilities. The labor of transforming those commodities into usable form—cooking the food, cleaning the house, caring for children—is unpaid. It is that capital appropriates. The wage relation thus hides a vast reservoir of exploitation: women's unpaid work subsidizes capital by keeping the wage bill low. Capital does not have to pay for the full cost of reproducing the working class because it externalizes that cost onto women in the home.

The demand for wages was a political strategy, not an economic reform proposal. organizers did not expect capitalists or the state to start paying women salaries for cooking and cleaning. The demand was intended to make visible what had been naturalized as invisible. Calling 'work' and demanding a wage for it denaturalized the ideology that women do out of love, maternal instinct, or feminine nature. It named as labor and named the wage relation's refusal to pay for it as exploitation. The demand also implied the possibility of refusal: if is work, women can refuse to do it, strike against it, or demand that it be socialized and shared rather than privatized and assigned to women.

The texts

Federici's Wages Against Housework (1975): why the demand makes invisible labor visible

In 1975, published , an eight-page pamphlet printed by and . The title is deliberately ambiguous and has confused readers ever since. is not arguing against paying women for ; she is arguing for wages as a weapon against the institution of as unpaid, naturalized, women's destiny. The pamphlet is the clearest short statement of the campaign's strategy.

Calling work and demanding wages for it makes visible the economic coercion behind the appearance of love. Women do not because of maternal instinct but because they have no other means of survival. The unwaged status of is not a benign oversight; it is a political decision that benefits capital. If were waged, the cost of reproducing the working class would be visible on the ledger, and capital would have to pay for it or socialize it.

The demand is a refusal: it refuses the naturalization of women's , it refuses the ideology that links femininity to selfless service, and it refuses to let capital hide the extent of its exploitation. Once is recognized as work, it can be collectively refused, and the question becomes: Why should this labor be done by individual women in isolated homes? Why not socialize childcare, communal kitchens, shared laundry? The wage demand opens space for imagining refusal and reorganization.

The debate

Marxist feminists dispute the strategy: does demanding wages entrench the gendered division of labor or expose capital's dependence on unwaged work?

The provoked fierce debate among Marxist feminists throughout the 1970s, a conversation known as the . Some participants, including and , focused on the theoretical question of whether produces value in Marx's sense. If value is socially necessary labor time embodied in commodities, does —which produces use-values consumed in the home rather than exchange-values sold on the market—produce value? The debate became intricate and scholastic, but it mattered politically: if is unproductive in Marx's technical sense, then the wage demand makes no sense within Marxist categories.

Others worried that demanding wages would entrench rather than challenge the gendered division of labor. If the state paid women to do , would that cement the idea that is women's work, making it harder to demand that men share it or that it be socialized? Would wages make the home a site of even more intense exploitation rather than a space women could refuse? 's influential essay 'The Unhappy Marriage of Marxism and Feminism' (1979) argued that Marxist analysis alone could not explain why is assigned to women; patriarchy as a separate system also structures the division of labor. Some feminists saw the wage demand as accommodating patriarchal capitalism rather than challenging it.

's (1981) included a chapter critiquing middle-class feminist campaigns that ignored racial history. pointed out that for Black women in the United States, the home had never been a private refuge. Under slavery, Black women's labor—reproductive and productive—was coerced for white slaveowners' profit. After emancipation, Black women worked as domestic servants in white households while also doing in their own. The idea that could be a site of retreat or that women's liberation meant staying home was a white middle-class fantasy. Demanding could inadvertently reinforce the idea that women's place is in the home, ignoring Black women's long struggle for the right to care for their own families rather than being forced to work in white homes.

theorists replied that they were not advocating domesticity. The demand was a strategy for exposing and refusing exploitation, not a plan to keep women in the kitchen. and emphasized that once is recognized as work, it can be refused, organized against, and reimagined. The debate never resolved, but it clarified the stakes: any feminist challenge to capitalism must grapple with the fact that capital depends on a vast reservoir of , disproportionately done by women and racialized workers, and any strategy for liberation must decide whether to demand recognition and payment for that labor or to refuse and abolish it.

Elsewhere

Same years, parallel actions: Iceland's women's day off on 24 October 1975 and the UN International Women's Year conference in Mexico City

On 24 October 1975, approximately 90% of Icelandic women stopped work. They did not go to their paid jobs, and they did not do or childcare. The action was called ——and it was organized by women's organizations to demonstrate the indispensability of women's labor, both waged and unwaged. Schools closed because teachers were absent; telephones went unanswered; men brought children to work or left them unsupervised because there was no one to care for them. Newspapers reported that Iceland came to a standstill. The strike made visible what had been arguing theoretically: that women's labor—paid and unpaid—is essential to the functioning of society and economy, and that its unwaged status makes it invisible until women refuse to do it. In 1976, Iceland passed legislation guaranteeing equal rights for women and men.

Earlier that year, in June and July 1975, the United Nations convened the first World Conference on Women in Mexico City as part of . Over 1,000 delegates from 133 countries attended, debating development, equality, and peace. The conference produced a World Plan of Action calling for legal equality, education, employment, and political participation for women. was a marginal position at the conference—mainstream delegates focused on integrating women into development and formal employment rather than questioning the wage relation or demanding payment for . But the conference's focus on women's labor, combined with grassroots actions like the Iceland strike, reflected a global moment when feminists worldwide were making women's work—especially —politically visible.

After

After the 1970s: the Global Women's Strike from 2000, social reproduction theory in the 2010s, care-work debates, and basic income proposals

By the late 1970s, the as an organized movement had largely dissolved, though its arguments continued to circulate. In the 1980s and 1990s, neoliberal globalization restructured labor and welfare systems worldwide. Women entered formal employment in unprecedented numbers, but this did not end their responsibility for and care. Instead, it created what feminists called the 'double shift' or 'second shift': women working full-time for wages and then doing a full workday of at home. Globally, wealthy households hired domestic workers—disproportionately immigrant women and women of color—to do cleaning, cooking, and childcare at low wages, creating what scholars called 'global care chains': women from the Global South migrating to care for children in the Global North while leaving their own children with relatives or paid caregivers.

The strike revived the demand in the context of neoliberal austerity, welfare cuts, and the privatization of social services. The strike connected unwaged in the home with underpaid in hospitals, schools, and elder care facilities, arguing that capital and the state depend on women's cheap or free labor to reproduce society while cutting public spending.

She argued that —the privatization of common land—were accompanied by the privatization of reproduction: women were confined to the home and assigned unpaid responsibility for raising the next generation of workers. The witch-hunts targeted women healers, midwives, and women who resisted this new sexual division of labor. Caliban extended the analysis historically, arguing that capitalism's origins required disciplining women into unwaged .

In the 2010s, a new generation of Marxist feminists revived the analysis under the label . edited : Remapping Class, Recentering Oppression (2017), collecting essays by , , Susan Ferguson, and others. analyzed how capitalism depends on and cannibalizes the work of —care, education, community—in books like Fortunes of Feminism (2013) and Capitalism: A Conversation (2022). These theorists argued that capitalism's current crisis includes a crisis of care: austerity cuts social services, wages stagnate, and families cannot afford to pay for childcare or elder care, yet the work must still be done. brought arguments into debates about how to challenge neoliberalism and imagine post-capitalist futures.

The question of how to recognize and redistribute remains unresolved. Some feminists advocate as a contemporary analogue to : an unconditional payment that would recognize all labor, waged and unwaged. Others worry that without public services would leave privatized and still disproportionately done by women. Care-work scholarship analyzes how care is commodified, outsourced, and racialized in global labor markets. The pandemic of 2020–2021 made the crisis visible: schools and daycares closed, hospitals were overwhelmed, and millions of women left paid employment to do unpaid at home. The old slogan returned: who will pay for the work that keeps society alive?

A question

A question to keep asking

When you eat a meal someone else cooked, wear clothes someone else washed, or benefit from someone else's care for a child or an elder, do you ask who did that work, whether they were paid, and whose labor subsidizes the economy's claim to efficiency by being naturalized as invisible rather than recognized as essential?

Fun fact

Wages Against Housework argued for wages, not against them

's 1975 pamphlet title confuses readers to this day. The title does not mean 'against paying for '; it means wages as a weapon against the institution of . explains that demanding wages is a political strategy to denaturalize the idea that women do out of love, maternal instinct, or feminine nature rather than economic coercion. The wage demand makes visible the fact that is work—socially necessary labor that capital relies on but refuses to pay for. Once recognized as work, can be refused, organized against, or shared rather than romanticized as women's destiny. The title's ambiguity captures the paradox: the campaign demanded wages not because wages would liberate women, but because the demand itself would expose how capitalism depends on a vast reservoir of unpaid female labor.
