Before

A decade when poetry was supposed to serve the Party

To see why felt new, you have to see what official Chinese looked like under . At the in 1942, had said that literature and art should educate the masses and serve revolutionary politics. After 1949 that line became policy. Poems were expected to be clear, uplifting and useful. One often-quoted example of the style runs: the moon follows the earth, the earth follows the sun, oil follows our steps, and we shall always follow the Communist Party. There was little room for private doubt, private love, or private metaphor.

Then came the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976). Schools closed. Teenagers joined Red Guard factions, denounced teachers, and later many were sent 'up to the mountains and down to the countryside'. Publishing literature outside official channels was dangerous. Even so, handwritten and typed poems moved among friends. , also known as (), was writing searching, personal poems by the late 1960s and became an early model for younger writers. , still a teenager, spent time as a Red Guard, then years as a construction worker outside Beijing. The spark

Today magazine at Democracy Wall

In December 1978 and launched an unofficial literary journal called (), . It was the first non-official literary magazine in the People's Republic since the 1950s. Issues were mimeographed, posted as broadsheets, and handed out around Beijing's , the stretch of wall where people were then sticking up about politics and reform. ran for nine issues until it was banned in 1980. It also published four books and held two readings (8 April and 21 October 1979) and two exhibitions of the (星星), the first major unofficial painters' group.

The idea

What 'misty' actually meant

The word means hazy, dim, hard to see clearly. In 1980, at a national and in the literary press, older poets and critics used it as a complaint. The new poems used private symbols, skipped clear grammar, and did not end with an obvious moral. To readers trained on socialist-realist verse, that looked wilfully obscure. The label stuck — first as an attack, then as a brand.

What the poets were doing was not mysticism for its own sake. After years of public language that claimed to explain everything, they wrote poems that left space for the individual mind. Images of night, walls, islands, oaks, ships and eyes replaced slogans. Meaning often arrived through juxtaposition rather than argument. put it this way: the new began with but veered toward ; it moved from passive reaction to active creation.

  • Personal pronouns and private feeling, after a decade when the collective 'we' had dominated official verse.
  • Compressed, sometimes puzzling images instead of plain description and political punchlines.
  • Skepticism toward official truth — 's 'I don't believe' is the clearest example.
  • A link back to world (, surrealism, ) that Mao-era policy had cut off.

Deep dive

The Answer: a poem that became a password

is short enough to copy by hand and sharp enough to remember. It opens with a speaker facing a world of judgement — 'baseness is the password of the base, honour is the epitaph of the honourable' in one common English rendering — and then turns to a list of refusals: the sky is blue; the echo of thunder; that dreams don't tell the truth; that death brings no recompense. The poem ends not with a party line but with a claim that the future will choose.

Why did it matter so much? Because it put disbelief in print after a decade when public speech had demanded belief. It did not name or the . It did not need to. Readers who had lived through struggle sessions and countryside heard themselves in the refusals. When the poem moved from the mimeographed pages of into , that private feeling entered an official channel — briefly. Ten years later, during the , lines from were painted on banners again. The poem had become a password across two different openings in Chinese public life.

has often said he does not want to be read only as a political poet. The politics is real, but so is the craft: broken grammar, stacked images, a voice that refuses to explain itself completely. That combination is what made older critics call the work misty, and what made younger readers keep copying it.

Elsewhere

What else was happening in poetry at the same time

was a Chinese answer to a Chinese silence, but it was not alone in the world. In the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe, poets and banned writers had long circulated typed manuscripts outside state presses — a cousin to 's culture, though the political systems differed. In the United States the last of Beat and confessional energy had already passed; Language and other experimental scenes were arguing about whether the lyric 'I' could still be trusted. 's difficulty, which Misty critics attacked as foreign pollution, was old news in Europe.

Closer to home, Taiwan in the late 1970s and 1980s was fighting its own literature wars. Nativist writers argued for local speech and local reality against mainland-centred — a different quarrel from Beijing's, but another place where the question 'whose language counts?' was urgent. Across the Pacific, Chinese-language poets in Hong Kong and in diaspora communities read the Misty poems as they appeared in translation and in traveller's copies.

The also left a mark outside literature. China's first generation of rock musicians, especially , drew on their tone and imagery. Uyghur poets in the 1980s developed a 'gungga' (hazy) manner partly under Misty influence, including freer verse forms. The local quarrel about obscurity became, for a while, an international style signal: Chinese that sounded modern to foreign ears.

After

Exile, revival, and the argument that never quite ended

June 1989 changed the map. , already abroad, was banned from China. , , and others lived outside the country for years. In 1990 and fellow exiles revived from Stockholm as a journal for Chinese writing abroad; it later moved online. Some poets eventually returned for visits or posts — has taught in Hong Kong and has been allowed brief mainland trips — but the easy link between underground magazine and street corner was gone.

Inside China the label Misty became textbook history. New poets defined themselves against it. Think about it

A question to take with you

When a critic calls a poem 'obscure', who is being asked to change — , or the reader's idea of what is for? got its name from people who wanted clearer public speech. Its readers kept it because the unclear parts felt truer than the slogans. Where do you still see that fight — in art, in journalism, or in the way institutions ask people to explain themselves?

Fun fact

The insult that became the brand

The poets did not name themselves Misty. In 1980, at meetings and in the press, older critics attacked the new writing as — hazy, obscure, hard to understand — and the insult stuck as the public label. later flipped the charge: he said the new began in and moved toward a , from passive reaction to active creation. The English phrases 'Misty Poets' and 'Obscure ' are both translations of that same critical word.
