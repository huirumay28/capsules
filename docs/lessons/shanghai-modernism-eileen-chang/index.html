Before

A treaty port that became China's modern city

Shanghai's modern history begins with defeat. After the First Opium War, the 1842 Treaty of Nanking opened Shanghai to British trade, and foreign powers soon carved out their own districts. By the 1920s the city had two foreign-run zones, the (dominated by Britain and later also Japan and the United States) and the , wrapped around Chinese-governed districts. Chinese law did not fully apply inside the foreign zones. That made them a refuge for exiles, radicals, publishers and gangsters alike.

By the early 1930s Shanghai was China's largest city and its centre of banking, shipping, film, advertising and publishing. It had department stores, neon signs, jazz bands, dance halls, cinemas showing Hollywood films, and the country's biggest printing houses. Most of China was rural and poor; Shanghai was the place where Chinese readers first met the modern city at full speed.

Literature arrived there already divided. The of 1919 had demanded a new literature in plain vernacular Chinese that would criticise tradition and wake the nation up. At the same time, Shanghai's mass market loved what critics dismissively called the : serialised and melodramas in newspapers and magazines. 's (, 1930) was one huge hit of this kind. Serious writers looked down on such fiction, but the young read it with pleasure, and she would later combine popular melodrama with modernist psychology.

The spark

The New Sensationists: writing at the speed of the city

In the late 1920s a small circle of young writers, several of whom had studied French at the Jesuit-run , began writing fiction about the city itself. They became known as the (). The name came from Japan, where writers such as and had launched the , or , in the mid-1920s, borrowing techniques from European modernism. The Shanghai group was more interested in the unconscious, in sensation and in style than in social reform.

The surprising founder figure is (, 1905–1940). He was born in Liouying, Tainan, in Japanese-ruled Taiwan, studied in Tokyo, and moved to Shanghai in 1926. Because he read Japanese, he could bring methods straight into Chinese. His collection (, 1930) shows men and women chasing each other through racecourses, cafés and dance floors, with sentences cut up like film montage. Liu also produced films and wrote about cinema, and his 1927 diary is now held by the National Museum of Taiwan Literature in Tainan.

'Shanghai Fox-trot' () and 'Five in a Nightclub' () repeat phrases like music, jump between viewpoints and describe the city as a machine of desire and exhaustion. The poet (, 1905–1950), whose 'Rain Alley' () is still taught in schools, brought French symbolist music into Chinese verse.

Their main platform was (), the monthly magazine edited from 1932 to 1935. It published new Chinese writing alongside a large amount of translated foreign literature, much of it American and Japanese, and introduced readers to modern art movements. The fight

The Left League: literature as a weapon

Not everyone thought writing about dance halls was a good use of talent while the country faced civil war, poverty and Japanese pressure. On 2 March 1930, writers close to the Chinese Communist Party founded the in Shanghai. (), the most respected writer of the generation and not himself a Party member, gave the opening address. The League grew from about fifty members to several hundred and asked writers to destroy the old society's ways of thought and serve political movements.

The Nationalist government banned the League within months. On 7 February 1931 it executed five League members, among them the writers () and (), after a mass arrest. They became known as the . The crack

War, the 'Orphan Island' and collaboration

In 1937 Japan attacked Shanghai. After months of fighting, the Chinese districts fell, but the and the stayed under foreign control, surrounded by Japanese-held territory. This four-year period is called the (). Publishing went on inside the island, often in coded or escapist forms. Many writers left for the interior; , for example, went to Yunnan and turned to classical scholarship.

In 1940 Japan set up a collaborationist Chinese government under () in Nanjing. Both and took jobs running newspapers under that regime, and both were shot dead in Shanghai that year, Mu on 28 June and Liu on 3 September, by assassins generally attributed to Nationalist underground forces. The two men who had written the most glamorous pages of died in its most violent streets.

In December 1941, after Pearl Harbor, Japanese troops entered the foreign settlements too. From then until August 1945 all of Shanghai was occupied. Anti-Japanese writing was impossible and openly pro-Japanese writing was shameful, so the literature that thrived was about private life: marriage, money, families and love. That was exactly the space in which two young women, and , became the city's best-selling writers.

Deep dive

Eileen Chang: the aristocrat's granddaughter who wrote about everyday ruin

was born () in Shanghai on 30 September 1920. Her was old Qing nobility in decline: her paternal grandmother was a daughter of the statesman . Her father was addicted to opium and kept concubines; her mother, Huang Suqiong, left to study in Europe and later divorced him. When Chang was about ten, her mother renamed her Ailing, a transliteration of the English name Eileen, so she could enter an English-language school. At eighteen, after a quarrel, her father beat her and locked her in a room for months while she was ill with dysentery; she escaped to live with her mother. This of faded grandeur, opium, quarrels over money and trapped women became the raw material of her fiction.

Chang won a place at the University of London in 1939 but could not go because of the war, so she studied English literature at the instead. When Japan took Hong Kong in December 1941, she was one semester short of her degree. She went back to Shanghai in 1942 and began writing for magazines. In 1943 the veteran editor , a leading figure of the popular world, published her stories, and within two years she was the most talked-about new writer in the city.

Two novellas from 1943 show what she could do. () follows , a divorced woman humiliated by her in Shanghai, who goes to Hong Kong to win the playboy . Their careful game of courtship is only settled when the Japanese attack destroys the colony; the fall of a whole city, the narrator suggests, may have happened so that one woman could get married. () is darker. , a sesame-oil shopkeeper's daughter, is married into a rich to an invalid husband. She spends decades guarding the money she finally inherits, and in the process ruins the lives of her own son and daughter. The of the title is a wooden collar once used to punish prisoners; hers is made of gold. The translator and critic praised the story highly, and later called it the greatest novelette in the history of Chinese literature.

Chang's style mixed things that critics usually kept apart. Her plots are the stuff of popular melodrama: arranged marriages, mistresses, inheritances. Her sentences borrow the rhythms of the classic novel , which she loved. But her attention to clothes, furniture, light, smell and the small cruelties people inflict on each other is fully modern, and she refuses to give readers heroes or political lessons. Other well-known stories from these years include 'Sealed Off' (), set in a tram stopped during an air-raid blockade, and 'Red Rose, White Rose' (). Her stories were collected in (, 1944), which became a bestseller, and her essays in (, 1945).

Her private life became part of her legend. In 1943 she met (), a writer and official in the government, who was still married at the time. They married privately in 1944. Hu soon began other relationships, fled under a false identity after Japan's defeat, and the couple divorced in 1947. After the war, Chang's association with Hu and with occupation-era magazines damaged her reputation. Hu later lived in Japan and lectured in Taiwan in the 1970s, where he influenced the Chu sisters, a link you can follow in the Taiwan Women's Fiction lesson.

The field

Su Qing and the other women of occupied Shanghai

Chang was not alone. (, 1914–1982), born in Ningbo, had left university to marry a husband chosen by her . After an unhappy marriage of about ten years, she divorced and began supporting herself as a writer and editor. Her autobiographical novel (, 1944) described marriage, childbirth and divorce from a wife's point of view with a bluntness readers had rarely seen, and it sold extremely well. She also ran her own magazine. Chang and knew each other and were often compared, sometimes as rivals and sometimes as the two voices of women's wartime Shanghai.

Their success has always raised hard questions. Both published under Japanese occupation, in an environment where politics had been pushed out of print. Some critics have seen their focus on domestic life as an evasion. Others argue that writing honestly about women's economic dependence, loveless marriages and divorce was its own kind of seriousness at a time when nationalist literature usually treated women as symbols of the suffering nation. After 1949 stayed on the mainland, was attacked for her wartime associations, was imprisoned in 1955, and died poor in 1982.

Elsewhere

What else was happening in city literature at the same time

Shanghai's modernism was part of a worldwide moment when writers tried to capture the big city. In Berlin, 's (1929) used montage, advertising slogans and overheard speech to show a working-class man lost in the metropolis, much as did with Shanghai. In Tokyo, the writers who inspired were doing the same for their own fast-changing capital.

The idea

Why this matters for reading women's writing

  • shows that Chinese modern literature was not only about national crisis. It was also about consumer goods, cinema, sex, money and loneliness in a big city.
  • The tied China to Tokyo and Paris; one of their founders was Taiwanese, which makes this part of Taiwan's literary history too.
  • and made women's domestic and economic lives the centre of serious fiction, at a time when politics usually treated women as symbols rather than people.
  • Their careers also show how wartime politics, collaboration and personal relationships can shape a woman writer's reputation for decades, often more harshly than a man's.
  • Chang's later fame in Taiwan explains a lot about the Taiwanese women's fiction of the 1970s to 1990s that you met in an earlier lesson.

Fun fact

'Be famous early'

is still quoted all over the Chinese-speaking world for one line from the preface to the second edition of in 1944: 出名要趁早呀！ — roughly, 'You have to become famous early!' She went on to say that if it comes too late, the happiness is not as sharp. The English Wikipedia article gives it as: 'To be famous, I must hurry. If it comes too late, it will not bring me so much happiness ... Hurry, hurry, or it will be too late, too late!' She was 23 or 24 when she wrote it, and she was right about the timing: her years of greatest fame in Shanghai lasted only from 1943 to 1945.
