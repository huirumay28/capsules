Heian Women's Writing (c. 935–1060): the court ladies who invented Japanese prose

Around the year 1000, in the imperial capital that is now , the most important prose in the Japanese language was written mostly by women. Men of the court were expected to write serious things in , while women wrote in , the new phonetic script for Japanese. Out of that division came , a book of lists and opinions, and . This lesson explains why women ended up at the centre, who they were, how they competed with each other, and what happened to their work.

Before

A court that wrote in a foreign language

To understand why women wrote the great prose of this period, start with the scripts. Japan had no writing system of its own. From roughly the fifth century onward it borrowed Chinese characters, and for centuries the official written language of the state was . Laws, government records, histories and the private of male officials were all written in Chinese, or in a Japanese-flavoured version of it called . For an educated man, writing in Chinese was a sign of learning and rank, much as Latin was for a medieval European cleric.

Japanese words could also be written with Chinese characters used only for their sounds. The eighth-century poetry anthology the was written this way, and the system is called . It was slow and awkward, because one simple Japanese word might need several complicated characters. During the ninth century scribes simplified these sound characters. Writing them quickly in cursive produced , and taking pieces of them produced . Together these are called . For the first time Japanese could be written as quickly as it was spoken.

Men wrote , the thirty-one-syllable Japanese poem, in , and they exchanged constantly in courtship and friendship. What men did not usually do was write extended prose in , because prose that mattered was supposed to be in Chinese. Women, on the other hand, were not expected to study Chinese, and a woman who displayed Chinese learning could be mocked for it. That left prose as an open field.

Two other changes prepared the ground. In 894 the court stopped sending official embassies to , which was collapsing, and in the following decades court taste turned more toward native forms. Around 905 ordered the , the first imperial anthology of Japanese poetry. Its preface, by the poet and official , claimed that Japanese poetry has its seeds in the human heart. was now officially respectable, and the ability to compose a good poem on the spot became one of the most important social skills at court, for women as well as men.

Then, around 935, did something odd. Returning from a term as governor of Tosa province on the island of Shikoku, he wrote a of the journey in and opened it by saying, in effect, that are something men write, but he would see whether a woman could write one too. The is narrated by an unnamed woman in his household. It mixes daily events with poems and with grief for a daughter who died in Tosa. By pretending to be a woman, could write in Japanese about feelings, which a man's Chinese would not normally contain. The women who came after him did not need to pretend.

The idea

Why the women's writing was different

The writers in this lesson were mostly daughters of the middle aristocracy: provincial governors, scholars and minor officials. Their families were educated but not at the top of the court. Many of them served as ladies-in-waiting to an empress, which gave them a close view of court life and free time to write. Their works fall into a few kinds.

  • , or . These are not day-by-day logs. They are memoirs, usually written or revised years later, that look back on a part of the writer's life and include many of the poems she exchanged. The , the , the and the are the best known.
  • , often translated as 'following the brush'. This is a loose collection of short pieces, lists, anecdotes and opinions in no fixed order. of is the founding example.
  • , or . Earlier such as were short, fantastic stories. turned the form into a long, realistic narrative about many characters over several generations.
  • poetry runs through all of them. The poems are part of the plot: a love affair in these works is mostly a correspondence in poems, and a poorly written or late reply can end a relationship.

What these works share is attention to inner life. The male Chinese of the same court record ceremonies, appointments and the weather. , the most powerful man of the age, kept such a , the , and the original survives. The women recorded jealousy, boredom, waiting for a husband who did not come, embarrassment at a badly judged remark, and the beauty of a particular evening. Later Japanese critics gave a name to the mood of this writing. In the eighteenth century the scholar argued that the heart of was , a sensitivity to things and to their passing. The phrase is now used for the whole literature of the period.

The field

The writers and their rivalries

The first great prose work by a woman is the , usually translated as . Its author is known only as the , after her son. She was married, as one of several wives, to , who later became regent and was the father of . covers 954 to 974 and is mostly about the pain of that marriage: 's visits become rarer, he takes up with other women, and she waits. She says at the start that the old are full of made-up things, so she will write about her own life as it really was. The result is bitter, self-absorbed and very modern in feeling. One of her poems, about how long a night is for a woman who sleeps alone, is in the famous anthology .

By the 990s the court had a new kind of competition. The ruled by marrying their daughters to emperors and serving as regents for the resulting young sons. A daughter who became empress needed an attractive household around her to keep the emperor visiting, and that meant clever, witty ladies-in-waiting. Literary talent became a political asset.

The rest of the book follows a life that never matched the stories: a brief time at court, a late marriage, pilgrimages, and in the end the conclusion that she should have spent less time on fiction and more on her prayers. It is the first record in Japan of a reader whose life was shaped by a novel.

Deep dive

The Tale of Genji

is a prose narrative of fifty-four chapters, containing about eight hundred poems, that follows the life of a fictional prince and then his descendants over some seventy years. seems to have been writing it before she came to court, and chapters circulated in copies as she wrote. It is very long: the modern English translations run to more than a thousand pages.

The hero, , is the son of an emperor and a lowly ranked consort whom the emperor loves too much. The other consorts torment her and she dies when is three. Because his mother's family has no power, his father removes him from the imperial line and makes him a commoner with the surname Minamoto, or . He is extraordinarily beautiful and gifted, which is why he is called the . The first half of the book follows his love affairs. Several of them come from the same loss. He falls in love with his father's new consort because she resembles his dead mother, and they have a son who is raised as the emperor's child and later becomes emperor himself. Several things explain why readers rank it so highly. The characters have psychology: we see why repeats the same pattern, and how suffers without being allowed to complain. The book changes tone as its characters age, from bright romance to regret and religious doubt. It shows clearly how little control aristocratic women had, since even the most loved of them depend entirely on men's visits and favour. In one famous passage , teasing a young woman who loves , argues that fiction can tell more of the truth about human life than official histories, which he says show only one side of things. It is hard not to read that as defending her own work against the Chinese histories men respected.

It is often called the world's first novel. That claim depends on how a novel is defined. Earlier long prose fictions exist, such as Greek romances and by Apuleius, but few earlier works sustain realistic characters and their inner lives over such a length. It is safer to say that is one of the earliest works that readers today easily recognise as a novel.

Elsewhere

What was happening elsewhere at the same time

The situation in Japan was unusual. In most of the literate world around the year 1000, serious writing was done in a classical or sacred language that women seldom learned, and writing in the spoken language was low in status. Japan had the same pattern, but because the low-status script happened to be the one women used, women ended up producing the major prose.

  • China. The was founded in 960. Its literature was dominated by male scholar-officials who passed the civil service examinations, writing essays, shi poetry and the newer lyric form called . The greatest female poet of classical China, , was born in 1084, after the period of this lesson, and wrote , not prose fiction.
  • Korea. The dynasty, founded in 918, introduced a civil service examination in 958, and educated men wrote in Chinese. Korea had no native alphabet until introduced in the 1440s, so there was no Korean equivalent of for women to write in.
  • Persia. Around 1010 the poet completed the , a vast epic of the kings of Iran written in Persian rather than Arabic, the language of learning. Like prose, it helped establish a spoken language as a literary one, but it was a work of national history by a man, not a record of private life.
  • Europe. In England the single manuscript of was copied around this time. In Saxony the canoness , who died around 973, wrote plays and poems in Latin, which makes her one of very few known women writers in Europe in that century. Literature by women in the European vernaculars, such as the lais of , appears only in the late twelfth century.

The comparison makes the point that the Heian women did not write because their society respected women's minds. They wrote because the respected language was closed to them, and the language left to them turned out to be the one that could express what people actually felt.

After

Warriors, scrolls, scholars and translators

No woman of the following generations matched these writers. Women continued to write and poetry in the later and under the , but the centre of literature shifted. Think about it

A question to take with you

The Heian women wrote the great prose of their time partly because they were shut out of the prestigious language of men. Can you think of other cases, in any country or period, where people excluded from the official forms of culture created something that later became the most valued part of it? What made that possible, and who decided later that it mattered?
