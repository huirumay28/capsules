Before

Before feminist standpoint theory: Marxist epistemology, Hegel's master and slave, and second-wave consciousness-raising

did not appear in a vacuum. It drew on two intellectual traditions and one grassroots practice. The first tradition was Marxist epistemology: Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels had argued that the working class's position in capitalist production gives workers epistemic access to truths about exploitation that the bourgeoisie cannot see. Ideology mystifies; labor reveals. 's History and Class Consciousness (1923, English translation 1971) systematized this into the claim that the proletariat's exposes the reified, fragmented appearances of capitalism and grasps the social totality. The second tradition was Hegelian: in the Phenomenology of Spirit (1807), the shows the slave achieving fuller self-consciousness through labor and struggle, while the master's abstract dominance leaves him dependent and mystified. would transpose both structures—Marxist and Hegelian—to women's .

The grassroots practice was second-wave feminism's . In the late 1960s and 1970s, women gathered in small groups to share personal experiences—housework, sexuality, motherhood, workplace discrimination. The method was simple: speak from experience, listen for common patterns, recognize that what felt personal is actually political. produced knowledge from below, without waiting for academic permission. It was epistemology before epistemologists named it. When , , , and articulated in the 1980s, they were giving philosophical form to a practice that working-class women, women of color, and grassroots feminists had already been doing.

Academic philosophy of science, meanwhile, was debating whether value-free objectivity was achievable or desirable. Positivism said yes; its critics said facts are theory-laden and inquiry is interest-driven. Feminist philosophers entered that debate armed with the claim that conventional objectivity was neither neutral nor maximally objective—it embedded the unexamined of dominant groups. The question posed was not whether knowledge is situated (it is) but which social locations yield better, more adequate knowledge of oppressive social relations.

The concept

What standpoint theory claims: knowledge from social location, epistemic advantage when critically achieved, and strong objectivity

makes three core claims. First, all knowledge is socially situated. Knowers are not disembodied minds; they are people located in social relations of gender, race, class, sexuality, nation. Those relations shape what questions get asked, what counts as evidence, which answers feel obvious, and which contradictions become visible. There is no . Conventional science's pretense of neutrality does not escape ; it merely leaves dominant locations unexamined.

Second, some social locations—especially those of oppressed and marginalized groups—offer potential . This is the controversial claim. does not say that any woman, or any member of a marginalized group, automatically knows more about everything. It says that people whose labor sustains the social order from below, who experience the contradictions between ideology and reality most sharply, who live double lives (performing in dominant institutions while knowing those institutions misrecognize them), can develop—through collective critical reflection—a that sees through mystifications invisible from above. The slave knows both the master's world and the slave's own; the master knows only his own abstraction. Women do the concrete labor of ; men theorize as if people were self-sufficient. Marginalized knowers have to understand both the dominant culture (to survive in it) and their own subjugated knowledge (to make sense of their lives). That dual consciousness is an epistemic resource, not an automatic guarantee.

Third—and this is 's contribution— produces , not weaker. If objectivity means getting an adequate account of reality, then ignoring the knower's and interests is a failure of objectivity. Acknowledging and critically examining where the knower stands, what the inquiry assumes, whose questions are being asked, and whose experience is treated as normal all strengthen objectivity. calls the conventional ideal because it pretends to neutrality and thereby protects unexamined bias. requires starting research from marginalized lives, maximizing critical scrutiny, and recognizing that no knowledge is view-from-nowhere. is a method for epistemic responsibility, not an identity card that confers truth.

Origin

Hartsock 1983: transposing Marx's proletarian standpoint to women's labor and social position

's 'The Feminist : Developing the Ground for a Specifically Feminist Historical Materialism' appeared in 1983 in , an anthology edited by and Merrill B. Hintikka and published by Reidel (now Springer). This essay is the canonical origin text. takes Marx's claim about the proletariat's and asks: what if we transpose that structure to gender? If the working class's position in capitalist production reveals truths about exploitation, what does women's position in the sexual division of labor reveal about patriarchy?

's answer centers on labor—specifically, the reproductive and historically assigned to women. Women's work is concrete, relational, bodily: bearing and feeding children, cleaning and cooking, tending to the sick and elderly, managing emotional life. This labor sustains human beings and social order, yet it is systematically devalued, naturalized as women's instinct rather than recognized as work, and theoretically disappeared. The concepts that organize male-dominated philosophy and political economy—the autonomous individual, the rational chooser, the rights-bearer—are abstractions that presuppose someone else is doing the unglamorous work of keeping people alive. calls this : the stance of the man who can theorize as if he were self-sufficient because a woman is doing the work that makes his self-sufficiency possible.

From women's , the view from , reality looks different. Humans are not independent atoms; they are born dependent, remain interdependent, and end lives dependent again. Survival requires concrete attention to particular others, not application of universal rules to interchangeable units. The boundary between self and other is more permeable than liberal theory admits—in pregnancy, nursing, caretaking. Ideology presents a world of autonomous individuals contracting in markets; women's labor reveals a world of embodied, vulnerable, relational people whose autonomy is a fragile achievement resting on unpaid care work. That is : not a celebration of women's nurturing nature, but an epistemological claim that starting inquiry from women's activity exposes what patriarchal abstraction hides.

Elsewhere

Same years, another formation: Lélia Gonzalez and Afro-Brazilian feminist epistemology in the 1980s

While , , , and were elaborating in the United States, in Brazil was developing a parallel epistemology grounded in Afro-Brazilian women's experience. Her 1984 essay 'Racismo e sexismo na cultura brasileira' (Racism and Sexism in Brazilian Culture), published in Revista Ciências Sociais Hoje, analyzed how Black Brazilian women's reveals intersections of race, gender, class, and nation that the dominant systematically obscures.

Black women in Brazil, argued, know both the official story of racial democracy and the lived reality of racism and sexism. They occupy an (though did not use 's term) in white households and institutions. That dual knowledge is an epistemic resource: Afro-Brazilian women see what the myth hides.

Her epistemology was not a direct import of U.S. ; it emerged from a different history of slavery, abolition, and national formation. But the structure is recognizably parallel: knowledge is socially situated; marginalized positions can yield critical consciousness when collectively developed; dominant ideologies mystify; starting inquiry from Black women's lives exposes what the center cannot see. The point for this capsule's Elsewhere section is that standpoint-like epistemologies were being articulated in the same years across different imperial and racial formations—not a West-plus-Asia pairing, but a contemporaneous Latin American feminist thread that shared the epistemic turn to experience, location, and critique from below.

After

Critiques, debates, and the question of multiple standpoints: Hekman, Haraway, and postmodern challenges

By the mid-1990s, faced sustained critique from multiple directions. The most prominent was the charge. If women's rests on women's distinctive labor and , which women? Women are divided by race, class, sexuality, disability, nation, immigration status, and more. White middle-class women's experience differs from working-class women of color. Straight women's interests conflict with lesbian women's. Is there one feminist or many? If many, does the theory collapse into perspectivism—everyone has their own view, and no offers ? If one, whose experience gets to represent all women, and isn't that a reinscription of white, Western, middle-class feminism's historical mistake?

Why should feminists cling to the Enlightenment project of grounding truth in a privileged subject position? proposed that feminists should embrace multiplicity, contest, and local resistances rather than seeking a foundational . It was refined, pluralized, and applied to new contexts. and others defended as a research methodology for studying power relations: start inquiry from the lives of the less powerful, expect their accounts to reveal what dominant accounts miss, test competing claims against broader evidence. A question

A question to keep asking

When you read research, policy analysis, or political argument, do you ask: from which was this knowledge produced, whose lives did the inquiry start from, and what might become visible if the research began instead from the of those who are currently invisible in the account?

Fun fact

Standpoint is an achievement, not an automatic birthright

One common misreading treats as the claim that any woman (or any member of a marginalized group) automatically knows more just by being who they are. The actual claim is more precise and more demanding. , following Marx and , argues that a is achieved through critical consciousness and political struggle, not given by identity alone. A woman can hold a perspective shaped by her without yet developing it into a —a systematic, critical account that exposes how dominant ideologies mystify social relations. makes the same point when she distinguishes spontaneous (adding women scientists) from (starting research questions from women's lives and interrogating whose interests shape inquiry). The theory is that marginalized positions offer potential epistemic resources—contradictions, , concrete knowledge of oppression's mechanics—but turning potential into takes collective work. Critics still charge that this distinction slips in practice, but the founders insisted: is a method and an achievement, not an identity card.
