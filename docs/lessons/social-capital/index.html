The concept

What social capital means

Economists have long talked about physical capital, such as tools, factories and buildings, and about , which is the knowledge and skill a person carries. is the third kind. It is the value that exists in the relationships between people: the networks that connect them, the norms of give and take they share, and the trust that lets them rely on each other. 's short definition is that refers to 'features of social organization, such as networks, norms, and trust, that facilitate coordination and cooperation for mutual benefit.'

The word capital is chosen on purpose. Like money in the bank, is built up through investment, in this case time, favours and repeated contact. It can be drawn on later, for example when you need a job lead, a babysitter or a volunteer. It can also run down when it is not maintained. Unlike money, however, it does not belong to one person. A relationship exists between two people, and a norm of trust exists across a whole group, so is partly a private good and partly a public good. Someone who never joins a club can still benefit from living in a street where neighbours watch out for each other.

Scholars usually divide into two types. is found inside tight groups of similar people, such as families, ethnic communities, church congregations or close friends. It provides emotional support and loyalty. connects people across social divisions, such as a sports club that mixes classes or a volunteer group that brings together different generations. It spreads information and widens a person's world. used the vivid comparison that is good for 'getting by' and is good for 'getting ahead'. A third term, , is sometimes used for ties between ordinary people and those with formal power, such as officials or donors.

A practical way to see the concept is to ask what becomes cheaper when trust is high. Contracts need fewer lawyers, neighbours lend tools instead of buying them, parents share school runs, and a stranger's word is good enough to start a deal. When trust is low, every one of these exchanges needs guarantees, checks or money up front, and many never happen at all.

Before Putnam

Where the idea came from

The idea is older than the term. In the 1830s the French writer travelled through the United States and was struck by how often Americans formed voluntary associations, for religion, temperance, schools, roads and almost everything else. He argued that these associations taught citizens the habits of cooperation that made democracy possible. Later sociologists, from to the Chicago school, worried about what happened to people when such ties were broken by industrial cities.

The first known use of 'social capital' in roughly the modern sense came in 1916 from , a state supervisor of rural schools in West Virginia. He wrote about community centres in schools and described as the goodwill, fellowship, sympathy and social intercourse among families that make up a social unit. In 1961 the urban writer used the term in for the dense, informal networks of city neighbourhoods, which she saw as an irreplaceable asset that bulldozer-style urban renewal destroyed.

Two academic versions then shaped the field. The French sociologist , writing in the early 1980s, treated as one of three forms of capital alongside economic and . For him it was the sum of resources a person could reach through a durable network of relationships, such as old school ties or family connections, and its main effect was to help privileged groups keep their advantages. The American sociologist , in a 1988 paper, took a more neutral view. He described as features of social structure that make certain actions possible. His most famous example was the wholesale diamond market in New York, where merchants handed each other bags of stones worth large sums to inspect at home without formal insurance, because the traders were bound by family, community and religious ties and cheating would mean losing everything.

also tested the idea on schooling. Using a large national survey of American high-school students, he found that dropout rates between the sophomore and senior years were 14.4 percent in public schools, 3.4 percent in Catholic schools and 11.9 percent in other private schools. He argued that Catholic schools were surrounded by a community of parents who knew one another through church, which he called . Those parents could share information and enforce norms together, and that community, rather than the students' own religion, seemed to keep children in school.

Case study 1

Twenty Italian regions, 1970 onward

In 1970 Italy set up a nationwide system of regional governments. Twenty new institutions were created with almost identical formal powers and structures, but they were planted in very different places, from the industrial north to the agrarian south, from strongly Catholic areas to strongly Communist ones. , a political scientist at Harvard, and his colleagues and saw this as a natural experiment. compared it to a botanist sowing genetically identical seeds in different plots of soil and watching how each grows.

Over about two decades the team measured how well each regional government performed, using indicators such as the stability of cabinets, the speed of budgets, the number of day-care centres and family clinics created, and how quickly offices answered citizens' requests. Some governments proved inefficient and corrupt, while others created innovative programmes and satisfied their residents. Government structure could not explain the difference, because it was the same everywhere, and reported that party politics and ideology made little difference either.

The best predictor was what called the . He measured it with four indicators that were closely correlated: newspaper readership, the density of sports and cultural associations such as choral societies and football clubs, turnout in national referendums, and the use of for individual candidates. counted against , because in Italy it was associated with , in which voters trade support for personal favours. Regions such as and scored high and had effective governments. Regions such as and scored low, and described them as trapped in mistrust, where public affairs were seen as the business of bosses and politicians rather than citizens.

then made his most controversial claim. He traced the north's civic traditions back to the communal republics of medieval northern and central Italy, where citizens had governed cities together, and the south's weaker traditions to the hierarchical Norman kingdom founded in the twelfth century. Critics replied that this story was too neat. In a 2004 re-analysis of 's own data, the political scientist found that economic development and the historical distribution of land predicted the kind of participation that made government work better than associational life did. The debate showed a recurring problem with : it is hard to tell whether trust and clubs cause good institutions, or good institutions and prosperity create trust and clubs.

Case study 2

Bowling Alone: America's declining social capital

In January 1995 turned the same lens on the United States in an article in the called 'Bowling Alone: America's Declining '. He gathered evidence that many forms of civic life had peaked in the middle of the twentieth century and then fallen. Regular volunteering, measured by Labor Department surveys, dropped from 24 percent of adults in 1974 to 20 percent in 1989. Volunteers for the Boy Scouts were down 26 percent and for the Red Cross 61 percent since 1970. Membership was down in fraternal groups such as the Lions (12 percent since 1983), the Elks (18 percent since 1979), the Shriners (27 percent since 1979), the Jaycees (44 percent since 1979) and the Masons (39 percent since 1959).

The detail that gave the article its title was what called his most whimsical piece of evidence. Between 1980 and 1993 the total number of bowlers in America rose by 10 percent, but bowling in organised leagues fell by 40 percent. Nearly 80 million Americans bowled at least once in 1993, so bowling was not a trivial pastime. People were still bowling, but more of them were doing it alone or with family rather than in a weekly league where they would sit with the same neighbours and coworkers and talk about local affairs.

also noticed a counter-argument. Big national organisations such as the , the and the had grown quickly. went from 400,000 members in 1960 to 33 million in 1993. But argued that these were 'tertiary associations': for most members, membership meant writing a cheque and perhaps reading a newsletter. Members did not meet each other, so the groups created political influence without creating the face-to-face ties that make up .

The article struck a nerve, and in 2000 published a long book, : The Collapse and Revival of American Community, with far more data on voting, church attendance, union membership, dinner parties, card games and trust. He tested many possible causes. By his rough accounting, about half of the decline came from generational change, as the unusually civic generation shaped by the Depression and the Second World War was replaced by less engaged younger cohorts. About a quarter came from television and electronic entertainment, which privatised leisure time. About a tenth came from pressures of time and money, including two-career families, and about a tenth from suburban sprawl and longer commutes. He found that women's entry into paid work was only a small part of the story, and he argued that busy people often cut television, not clubs.

's later work continued the story. In a 2007 paper titled 'E Pluribus Unum' he reported an uncomfortable finding: in more ethnically diverse American neighbourhoods, people tended in the short run to trust their neighbours less, including neighbours of their own group, and to withdraw, which he called . He stressed that he expected this to change over time as new shared identities formed. In 2020, with , he published , which placed the decline inside a longer arc from individualism in the Gilded Age to community in the mid-century and back.

Criticism

The dark side and the measurement problem

The sociologist , in a widely cited 1998 review, warned that is not always good. He listed four negative effects: groups that help their own members can exclude outsiders; strong communities can demand too much from successful members; close groups can restrict individual freedom; and in some poor communities, norms can pull people down by punishing those who try to leave. A mafia family, a gang or a closed old-boys' network has plenty of .

Other critics focused on 's American evidence. The political scientist , in (2003), argued that the change was not mainly about individual habits such as television. Instead, American elites had shifted from large membership federations with local chapters to professionally managed advocacy groups run from Washington offices, which ask members for money rather than participation. Others pointed out that new forms of association, such as self-help groups, sports for children and later online communities, were harder to count than Elks lodges, and that some of the old clubs mourned had excluded women and Black Americans.

  • Definition problem: is sometimes defined by its effects, which makes it hard to test, because any group that cooperates well can be said to have it.
  • Causation problem: trust may be the result of good government and prosperity rather than their cause, as the Italian debate showed.
  • Measurement problem: clubs, survey questions about trust and voting turnout capture different things, and they do not always move together.
  • Distribution problem: 's version reminds us that networks are unequally distributed and often reproduce class privilege.

's 1973 paper 'The Strength of ' adds a useful twist. In a study of people who had recently changed jobs in a Boston suburb, he found that useful job information more often came from acquaintances than from close friends, because acquaintances move in different circles and know things your close friends already know. This supports the value of and warns that a community rich in ties can still be poor in new information.

Meanwhile in Taiwan

From 社區發展 to 社區總體營造

Taiwan's own story offers an interesting comparison. From the 1960s the government ran a programme () that was largely top-down: officials drew community boundaries that matched village and borough lines, and funds went into activity centres, nurseries and public works. After the lifting of martial law in 1987, environmental protests and local preservation campaigns showed that residents could organise themselves around shared concerns.

On 3 October 1994, , chair of the (, the predecessor of today's Ministry of Culture), presented a plan to the Legislative Yuan to promote , usually translated as or comprehensive community building. The idea, championed by the anthropologist , was to use culture, local history and local industries as entry points for residents to take part in public affairs from the bottom up. The approach was later described through five themes: people, culture, land, industry and landscape (人、文、地、產、景). In the 2000s it grew into the cross-ministry . In effect, the policy was an attempt to build on purpose, during the same years when was arguing that America's was eroding.

Taiwanese commentators also noticed the dark side described. The founder of the , often cited as a model of community building, wrote that local development associations and village elections could give factions new chances to divide and fight. This mirrors the international lesson that strong local networks can either bridge differences or harden them, depending on how they are organised.

Using the idea

What it means for cool dairy and for choosing partners

For a vintage shop that wants to give its proceeds to Taiwan women's organisations, offers two practical questions. The first is about the partner organisations. A group whose members, volunteers and service users actually meet, such as local chapters, support groups or volunteer training, is building , while a group that mainly asks for monthly donations resembles 's 'tertiary associations'. Both can do valuable work, but they create different kinds of community, and their annual reports will show different things: volunteer hours and local branches in one case, fundraising and advocacy campaigns in the other.

The second question is about the shop itself. A resale platform is a network of strangers who must trust each other about condition, sizing and payment. Transparent donation reports, real stories about the organisations and occasional face-to-face events, such as swaps or talks, can turn customers into a community with ties across age and background. That trust is an asset in the same sense meant in the diamond market: it lowers the cost of every exchange.

Fun fact

The money is in the beer and pizza

noted that the decline of league bowling worried bowling-alley owners for a very practical reason. According to his 1995 article, people who bowled in leagues consumed three times as much beer and pizza as solo bowlers, and 'the money in bowling is in the beer and pizza, not the balls and shoes.' What cared about was what happened over that beer and pizza: the conversations, sometimes about local politics, that solo bowlers never had.
