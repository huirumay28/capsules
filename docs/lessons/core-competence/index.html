Before

The 1980s: companies managed as portfolios of businesses

By the 1980s, most large Western companies were organised as collections of , or . Each unit had its own products, its own market and its own profit target. Headquarters acted like an investor: it moved money toward units that were growing and earning well, and it sold or starved the ones that were not. Portfolio tools such as the Boston Consulting Group's , created around 1970, encouraged exactly this way of thinking.

and open their 1990 article by saying that, in the 1980s, top executives were judged on their ability to restructure, declutter and de-layer their corporations. In the 1990s, they predicted, executives would instead be judged on their ability to identify, cultivate and exploit the that make growth possible.

Their argument grew out of a puzzle. Japanese companies such as , , and Sony kept entering markets that nobody expected them to enter, and they often won. The article lists examples: gave the world personal copiers, moved from motorcycles into four-wheel off-road buggies, Sony developed the 8mm camcorder, and Yamaha developed the digital piano. Western managers who studied only the of these rivals could not see where the next attack would come from.

The framework

Roots, trunk, branches and leaves

and describe a diversified company as a tree. The that customers buy are the leaves, flowers and fruit. The business units are the smaller branches. The trunk and major limbs are , meaning key components or subsystems that go into many . The root system, which feeds and stabilises everything, is the company's . A rival who looks only at another company's misses its real strength, in the same way that someone looking only at leaves misses the strength of a tree.

They define as 'the collective learning in the organization, especially how to coordinate diverse production skills and integrate multiple streams of technologies.' A competence is not a single technology or a patent. Their example is . Knowing in theory how to put a radio on a chip does not let a company build a radio the size of a business card. To do that, had to combine know-how in miniaturisation, microprocessor design, materials science and ultrathin precision casing.

The article offers three tests for deciding whether something is a :

  • It gives potential access to a wide variety of markets. Competence in display systems, for example, lets a company make calculators, miniature televisions, laptop monitors and car dashboards.
  • It makes a significant contribution to the benefits customers perceive in the . 's engine expertise is the article's example.
  • It is difficult for competitors to imitate, because it is a complex combination of technologies and production skills rather than one piece of equipment that can be bought.

The authors also warn that few companies can build world leadership in more than five or six fundamental competencies. A company that writes a list of twenty or thirty capabilities, they say, has probably not produced a list of .

sit between competencies and , and the article treats them as a separate battle. , for instance, made about 45 percent of the world's key VCR components, even though its own brands held only about 20 percent of the VCR market. By selling to other companies, a firm can earn money and learn from many markets at once, even where its own brand is weak.

Finally, the article explains how the system damages competencies. It names three problems. The first is underinvestment: no single unit feels responsible for building a competence that serves the whole company, so nobody pays for it. The second is imprisoned resources: unit managers treat their best engineers as their own property and may even hide them rather than lend them to another unit. The third is bounded innovation: each unit pursues only small extensions of its own products, so hybrid products such as fax machines or laptop computers, which draw on skills from several units, never get started.

The cure the authors propose is a , meaning a map of which competencies the company must build and which technologies make them up, combined with a that tells everyone where the company is going. For , that intent was 'C&C', the convergence of computing and communications.

It helps to compare this with Michael Porter's five forces, covered in an earlier Saturday lesson. starts outside the company and asks which industries are attractive and where to position within them. and start inside the company and ask what it is uniquely good at, then look for the markets those skills can reach. Later scholars grouped the second approach with the of strategy. The two approaches are not opposites, and most strategists now use both.

Between 1985 and 1987, the number of engines bought from outside suppliers, mainly Mitsubishi and Hyundai, rose from 252,000 to 382,000. The authors found it difficult to imagine handing the design or manufacture of such an important part to another company, and they linked 's heavy commitment to Formula One racing to the same logic: racing kept its engine engineers at the frontier.

The lesson the authors drew from is that outsourcing can save money on this year's products while weakening the skills needed for the next generation. In their words, the embedded skills that give rise to new products 'cannot be “rented in” by outsourcing and OEM-supply relationships.'

After

Limits and critiques

The idea spread quickly, but it was also misused. Because the concept is broad, many companies simply relabelled whatever they already did as a . and had warned against this when they said that a list of twenty or thirty items is not a list of .

In 1992 published 'Core capabilities and ' in the Strategic Management Journal. She studied new product development projects and found that a company's core capabilities can also hold it back. The skills, systems and values that made a company excellent in one technology can make people resist projects that need different skills. A company that has spent decades perfecting film chemistry, for example, may find it hard to take digital electronics seriously. Her point is that competencies have to be renewed, not only protected.

Another limit is that the framework says little about which industries are worth entering. A company with a strong competence can still apply it in a market where Porter's five forces leave little profit for anyone. This is why later strategists tend to combine the inside-out question, 'what are we uniquely good at?', with the outside-in question, 'where can those skills earn attractive returns?'

One question

Find the roots of a company you know

Choose a company you know well and list its five most important . Then ask what skills those products have in common. Do those skills pass and 's three tests: do they open several markets, do customers notice them, and would a rival find them hard to copy? Finally, ask 's question: which of those skills might stop the company from seeing the next change in its industry?
