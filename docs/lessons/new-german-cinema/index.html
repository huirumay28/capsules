Before

Papa's cinema, and a different Germany from Weimar

This is not the Germany of German Expressionism (c. 1919–1931), the painted studios and anxious shadows of that this site has already covered. By the 1950s that cinema, and the people who had made it, had been scattered by exile, war and the division of the country. West German cinemas in the were full of , rural melodramas set in tidy landscapes, along with comedies and musicals that did good business and asked little of the audience. The Nazi years were not absent from every film, but they were rarely faced in a sustained way.

East Germany had a different system altogether: a state studio, , and directors such as working inside it. , the subject of this lesson, is a West German story. The two industries barely shared a public, a funding model or a language of criticism.

The crisis of the commercial industry became hard to ignore in 1961, when no West German film was judged worthy of the . The federal interior minister spoke of a short, shallow flowering of tear-jerking entertainment. Young short-film directors, some of them already winning prizes at international festivals, had a ready answer: the old industry could not, or would not, make the films they wanted to make.

The break

28 February 1962: a short text and a long aftermath

The International Short Film Festival Oberhausen, in North Rhine-Westphalia, was where this discontent found a public form. On 28 February 1962 a loose group known as held a press conference titled 'Papas Kino ist tot.' read a short manifesto. chaired the discussion that followed. The declaration had been initiated by . Twenty-six people signed it: directors, cinematographers, producers and one composer. Among them were Kluge, , , , and .

The text itself is brief. It says that the collapse of conventional German film has removed the economic basis for a mentality the signatories reject, and that recent German short films, already recognised at international festivals, show that the future lies with people who speak a new film language. It demands freedom from the conventions of the established industry, from commercial partners, and from control by special interest groups. It ends: 'Der alte Film ist tot. Wir glauben an den neuen.' The signatories say they have concrete intellectual, formal and economic ideas, and that they are prepared to take economic risks.

The manifesto did not contain a production plan. What it did was put pressure on cultural policy. In 1965 the Federal Interior Ministry created the , a fund for first features. Film schools followed: Kluge, Schleiermacher and Reitz helped found the at Ulm; the opened in 1966; the Hochschule für Fernsehen und Film in Munich was established in 1966 and opened in 1967. The early years are sometimes called , to mark a more austere, experimental phase before the more widely seen films of the 1970s.

The films

What the new films were like

The directors did not share one style. What they shared was a condition of work: low budgets, personal control over the script and the shoot, and a wish to look at West German life rather than at a smoothed-down version of it. They took formal ideas from the French New Wave (c. 1958–64) and from Italian Neorealism (c. 1943–1952), and they also borrowed freely from Hollywood genres — the gangster film, the melodrama, the road movie — and then twisted them.

  • Authorship. The director was expected to write, or closely control, the film. The German word often used is : a film whose author is the director.
  • Subjects close to hand. , unhappy marriages, small-town boredom, the and its costs, American popular culture, and, again and again, the Nazi past and what the parents' generation had done or failed to do.
  • A home audience that was often smaller than the foreign one. Many of these films found their first serious praise at festivals and in art houses abroad, and only later, or never, became popular in West German cinemas.
  • Television as a partner and a problem. Until 1974, broadcasters who put money into a film often wanted a television premiere first, which hurt the film's chances in cinemas. The of 1974, an agreement between , and the federal film board created in 1968, reserved an annual sum for films that would play in theatres first and reach television only after twenty-four months.

In 1971 a group of directors formed , their own production and distribution company. It became, for much of the 1970s, the practical home of the movement. The work still depended on subsidies. A 1977 report in the New York Times put the typical subsidy share of a West German film budget at 80 percent.

Elsewhere

What else was happening at the same time

The Oberhausen date, 1962, sits in a crowded decade of 'new' cinemas. The French New Wave (c. 1958–64) was already in full force when the manifesto was read, and it was an acknowledged model: personal films, shot quickly, often in real places. Italian Neorealism (c. 1943–1952) was older, but its habit of taking the camera into ordinary streets still mattered. In Czechoslovakia, The Czechoslovak New Wave was unfolding in the same years, until the Soviet-led invasion of 1968 closed much of it down. In Brazil, Cinema Novo was arguing that a poor country needed a cinema that showed hunger rather than gloss. In the United States, New Hollywood (c. 1967–1980) was giving a generation of directors unusual freedom inside the studios, a very different economic world from the subsidy-dependent West German one.

A little later, and on the other side of the world, Taiwan New Cinema (c. 1982–1990s) and China's Fifth Generation filmmakers, 1982 to 1994, would also make their names at foreign festivals with films that looked unlike the official cinema at home. The parallel is real and should not be stretched too far. Each of these movements had its own political conditions, its own money, and its own arguments. What they share is a pattern: a group of directors, often trained together or funded by a new public mechanism, using a moment of institutional looseness to put a country's recent history and everyday life on screen, and finding that the first loud applause sometimes comes from abroad.

After

After 1982

Some directors moved toward larger, more international productions; some of those films failed commercially, which is one reason the movement is usually closed in the early 1980s. A later group of German filmmakers, often called the , emerged in the 1990s and 2000s with a quieter, observational style. They are a subsequent generation, not a continuation under the same name.

What remains from 1962 to 1982 is a body of films that returned German cinema to international critical life for the first time since , and a record of how one country's artists tried to look at prosperity, guilt, gender, migration and political violence without smoothing them away. The manifesto had promised a new film. The films that followed did not form a single school. They did, for two decades, make it possible to speak of a and be understood outside Germany.

Many of these films are about a younger generation trying to live with a national past that the people who raised them had not fully faced. If you think about films, books or family stories from Taiwan after martial law, what do they do with an unprocessed past — name it, circle it, or leave it just off screen? What would count, for you, as facing it honestly?

Fun fact

The slogan that is not in the manifesto

The phrase everyone associates with Oberhausen is 'Papas Kino ist tot' — 'Papa's cinema is dead.' It was the title of the press conference at which the manifesto was read, on 28 February 1962, but it does not appear in the manifesto itself. The text, about 150 words in German, ends with a different line: 'Der alte Film ist tot. Wir glauben an den neuen' — 'The old film is dead. We believe in the new one.'
