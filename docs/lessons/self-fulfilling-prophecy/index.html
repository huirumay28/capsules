The : when a false belief makes itself come true

A belief about a person or a situation can change how people act, and that change can produce the very outcome the belief predicted. This lesson explains Robert 's concept first, then follows the famous classroom experiment and the long argument about what it really showed.

The concept

What a self-fulfilling prophecy is

A is a belief or prediction that causes itself to come true. The belief is false, or at least not yet true, when people first hold it. But because people believe it, they act differently, and their new behavior creates the situation the belief described. Afterwards, the outcome seems to prove that the belief was right all along.

The American sociologist gave the idea its name in a 1948 essay in . His definition is still the standard one: a is 'a false definition of the situation evoking a new behavior which makes the originally false conception come true.' The key word is false. A correct prediction that comes true is just a good prediction. A is a mistaken prediction that becomes correct because people acted on it.

built on an older idea from and . In their 1928 book they wrote that 'if men define situations as real, they are real in their consequences.' This sentence, known as the , makes a simple point: people respond to what they believe about a situation, not only to the situation itself. 's contribution was to trace what happens when that belief is wrong and the response then changes the facts.

The process usually has three steps. First, someone forms an expectation about a person, a group, or an institution. Second, the expectation changes how they treat that target, often without their noticing. Third, the target responds to that treatment in a way that matches the original expectation. The loop then closes, because the person who held the expectation sees the result and takes it as confirmation.

Psychologists often separate this from two related ideas. An inside research, sometimes called the experimenter effect, happens when a researcher's expectations shape the results of a study. Perceptual confirmation, or , happens when people only notice or remember what fits their expectation, even though the other person has not changed at all. In a true , the target's behavior really does change. That is why the idea matters so much: it is not just a mistake in perception, it is a mistake that rebuilds reality to fit itself.

There is also a reverse case. A is a prediction that prevents itself from coming true, for example a warning about a disease outbreak that leads people to take precautions, so that the outbreak never happens. The philosopher , writing about the influence of predictions on events, called the general phenomenon the 'Oedipus effect', after the Greek legend in which the oracle's prophecy sets in motion the very events it foretold.

The first example

Merton's bank run

illustrated his idea with a story set in 1932, during the Great Depression. He described a fictional bank, the Last National Bank, run by a banker he called Cartwright Millingville. The bank is healthy. Its assets are sound, and it could pay its depositors over time. Then a rumor spreads that the bank is about to fail. Depositors rush to withdraw their savings, and because no bank keeps all its deposits in cash, the rush drains the bank and it collapses. The rumor was false when it started, but it became true because people believed it.

Bank runs are still the clearest real-world case. In March 2023, depositors at in California tried to withdraw tens of billions of dollars in a single day after news spread that the bank had lost money on its bond holdings. Regulators closed the bank the next day. Economists still debate how weak the bank already was, but the speed of the collapse came from the run itself: each depositor who pulled money out made it more reasonable for the next one to do the same. Modern deposit insurance exists largely to stop this loop, by removing the reason to rush.

was most interested in a more troubling use of the idea, which was prejudice. He pointed out that in the United States of his time, many white trade unions excluded Black workers on the grounds that they were strikebreakers who undermined labor. Because they were excluded from unions, some Black workers could find jobs only by crossing picket lines during strikes. The exclusion produced the behavior that was then used to justify the exclusion. summed up this pattern in a well-known line: 'the specious validity of the perpetuates a reign of error.' He argued that the loop can be broken only by changing the institutions that turn the false belief into behavior, not by lecturing people about their attitudes.

Case study

Pygmalion in the Classroom

The most famous test of the idea took place in a school. In the early 1960s the Harvard psychologist had become interested in how researchers' expectations affect their results. In one study with , students were told that some lab rats were 'maze-bright' and others 'maze-dull', although the rats had been assigned to those labels at random. The rats labeled bright performed better, apparently because the students handled and trained them differently.

wondered whether the same thing happened to children. He was contacted by , the principal of a public elementary school in South San Francisco, which their book later called Oak School. At the start of the study the children were given a nonverbal intelligence test, which teachers were told was a new test that could predict which children were about to 'bloom' intellectually. The researchers then gave teachers the names of children who were supposedly about to bloom. In fact, these names had been chosen at random. The only real difference between these children and their classmates was what the teachers had been told.

When the children were retested later, the 'bloomers' as a group had gained more IQ points than the other children. The difference was concentrated in the youngest children, in the first and second grades, and was small or absent in the older grades. and published the results in 1968 in a book titled . The title refers to the Greek myth of the sculptor whose statue came to life, and also to 's 1913 play , in which a phonetics professor turns a London flower seller into someone who passes as a lady.

The book reached a very wide audience. At the end of the 1960s, American schools were arguing about why poor and minority children did worse on tests, and the study seemed to offer a striking answer: perhaps teachers' low expectations were part of the cause. The idea entered teacher training, education policy debates and popular writing, and the phrase 'the ' became the common name for the idea that high expectations raise performance. Its opposite, the idea that low expectations lower performance, later became known as the .

The argument

What the Pygmalion study really showed

The study was criticized almost immediately. In a 1968 review in the American Educational Research Journal, the educational psychologist argued that the test results were too unreliable to support the book's claims. He pointed out that in one ordinary class the average reasoning score was so low that it fell in the range normally associated with intellectual disability, which was very unlikely for a typical class. 's point was that if some scores were clearly wrong, none of them could be fully trusted. Later critics also noted that very low starting scores tend to rise on a second test anyway, a statistical effect called .

Other researchers tried to repeat the experiment, with mixed results. In 1984 combined the results of eighteen such experiments in a , a study that pools the results of many studies. He found a clear pattern. When teachers received the false information before they had got to know their students, there was a small effect on IQ. When teachers had already spent a couple of weeks with the class, the effect was close to zero. In other words, a teacher's own direct knowledge of a child tended to override a label from outside.

In 2005 and reviewed about thirty-five years of research on teacher expectations in Personality and Social Psychology Review. Their conclusion was more careful than the original book. in classrooms do happen, but the effects are usually small and often fade over time. Most of the link between teacher expectations and student performance is explained by accuracy: teachers often expect what they do because they have correctly read how a student is doing. The review also found some evidence that may be stronger for students from stigmatized groups and for students who are already doing poorly, which is exactly where they would matter most.

So the honest summary is this. The dramatic version of the story, in which a few words from a researcher can raise a child's intelligence, is not well supported. The more modest version, in which expectations can nudge how teachers treat children and how children perform, is supported, especially when teachers do not have much other information. That modest version is still important, because small effects can add up when they repeat over years of school.

Beyond the classroom

Self-fulfilling prophecies in interviews, conversations and training

Some of the clearest evidence for the mechanism comes from laboratory studies of everyday interaction. In 1974 , and ran a two-part study at Princeton. In the first part, white participants interviewed Black and white job applicants, who were actually trained confederates. With Black applicants, the interviewers sat farther away, made more speech errors and ended the interviews sooner. In the second part, the researchers trained interviewers to treat white applicants in one of those two styles. Applicants who received the colder style performed worse and were judged less competent. The study showed how a prejudiced expectation could produce a weaker performance and then appear to justify itself.

In 1977 , and had men talk on the telephone with women they had never met. Each man was shown a photograph that he believed was of his partner, either an attractive or a less attractive woman. The photographs did not actually show the women. Independent listeners who heard only the women's side of the conversation rated the women whose partners believed they were attractive as warmer and more friendly. The men's belief had changed how they spoke, and the women responded in kind.

The idea has also been tested in workplaces and the military. In a 1982 study, and told instructors in an Israel Defense Forces training course that some trainees had high command potential, although these trainees had been chosen at random. Those trainees scored higher on objective tests at the end of the course. Later work by and others suggested that part of the effect ran through the trainees' own expectations of themselves, which rose when they were treated as promising.

These studies help explain why the concept appears in discussions of education, hiring, policing and medicine. They also connect to earlier lessons on this site. Stereotype threat describes how awareness of a negative stereotype can lower performance in the moment. Pluralistic ignorance describes how wrong beliefs about what others think can keep a norm alive. The adds a third piece: wrong beliefs about a person can change that person's behavior, and then look like they were right.

Think about it

Questions to take away

A useful habit is to ask, when a prediction about a person or group seems to have been confirmed, how the people who held the prediction behaved before the outcome. If their behavior changed because of the prediction, the confirmation is weaker evidence than it looks.

Think of a label you were given as a child, at school or at home, such as 'the smart one', 'the shy one' or 'bad at math'. Did people treat you differently because of it? Did it change what you tried to do?

Fun fact

A horse helped start the research

often traced his interest in back to , a horse in Berlin in the early 1900s that seemed to answer arithmetic questions by tapping its hoof. In 1907 the psychologist showed that was not counting. The horse was reacting to tiny, unconscious changes in the posture and face of the person asking, who relaxed when reached the right number. The questioners' expectations were producing the answers, which is the same basic problem later studied in labs and classrooms.
