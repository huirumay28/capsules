Taiwan's Women's Fiction (c. 1976–1995): from newspaper prizes to and

Between the late 1970s and the mid-1990s, a generation of Taiwanese women novelists moved from the edge of the literary world to its centre. They won the big newspaper fiction prizes, were dismissed by some critics as 'boudoir writers', and then wrote books about marriage, money, violence, sex and, by the 1990s, queer love that the island had never read before. This lesson explains how that happened, who the writers were, and why 's () is still the book everyone argues about.

Before

Modernists, nativists and an island under martial law

To see what changed, start with the literary world of the 1960s and early 1970s. Taiwan was under , and anything that sounded like criticism of the Kuomintang government or sympathy for communism could end a career or a life. Serious fiction mostly followed two paths. The modernists around the journal (), founded in 1960 by () and his classmates at National Taiwan University, wrote psychological stories influenced by Joyce, Kafka and existentialism. The nativists, whom you met in the lesson on Taiwan's Nativist Literature, wrote realist stories about farmers, workers and small towns, and in 1977 fought a public battle over whether literature should serve the Taiwanese people.

Women were present in both camps but rarely at the centre of the arguments. (), born in () in 1945, published her first story at seventeen and was praised by for its nightmarish atmosphere. She helped found the journal in 1966 with Chen Ying-chen, Huang Chun-ming and others. Her younger sister, born Shih Shu-tuan in 1952 and later known as (), started publishing modernist stories as a high-school student in the late 1960s. Both sisters grew up in the same old port town, and would become the setting for much of 's later work.

There was also a huge popular market for romance fiction. () had been the best-selling novelist in the Chinese-speaking world since the 1960s, with love stories that were turned into films and television series. Critics treated this kind of writing as entertainment for girls. That prejudice matters, because when serious women writers arrived in large numbers at the end of the 1970s, many critics read them through the same lens.

Two other forces were moving underneath. One was the women's movement. In 1971 () began publishing essays on what she called (), and in 1976 she and were among the founders of the (), the first publishing house run entirely by women. The secret police harassed it until it stopped in 1977, and Lu was jailed after the of 1979. The other force was money. Taiwan's economy was growing fast, women were entering offices and universities, and the two great newspapers, the () and the (), had become powerful cultural institutions with daily literary supplements read by millions.

The idea

How newspaper prizes made a generation, and what 'boudoir literature' meant

The key institution of this period was the newspaper literary prize. The started its short-story prize in 1976, and in 1979 added novellas and novels. The Literary Award followed in 1978, run by the editor , who had also invented as a newspaper genre. Winning stories were printed in the supplements, collected in annual anthologies by publishers such as 爾雅 and 洪範, and often bought by film and television producers. A young woman with no connections could become a national name in a single season.

Between about 1979 and 1984 women won these prizes again and again. , , , , , and were all prize winners, and their books sold well. The wave was so obvious that journalists and critics gave it a name: , which can be translated as 'boudoir literature' or 'the literature of well-bred young ladies'. The word 閨秀 refers to a daughter of a good family who stays in the inner rooms of the house.

The label was partly descriptive and partly a put-down. It suggested that these writers came from comfortable homes, wrote about love, marriage and family rather than politics or class, and wrote in a polished, lyrical style. Some of that was true of some writers. But the label lumped together very different people and hid what many of the stories were actually doing, which was looking hard at what marriage, money and family did to women in a society that was changing faster than its rules.

It helps to sort the writing of this period into a few kinds:

  • Stories of the new urban woman. 's ('A Sky of One's Own', 1980) follows a wife whose husband leaves her; she gets a job, makes her own decisions and finds she does not want him back. The title deliberately echoes 's A Room of One's Own. 's (1983) shows an ordinary office worker drawn into years as a married man's mistress.
  • Family sagas about mothers and daughters. 's (1982) takes its title from a Taiwanese saying that a daughter is like a , which grows wherever it is scattered, meaning a girl's fate depends on whom she marries.   • Lyrical stories of love and loss shaped by . 's (1979) and are cool, precise accounts of women who give everything to love. The critic () later described her as having the essence of , writing hot passion with a cold pen.
  • Fiction that broke taboos about sex and violence. 's (1983) is the most famous example, and the one that made the 'boudoir' label impossible to maintain.

Chang had become famous in Japanese-occupied Shanghai in the 1940s with sharp, ironic stories about women, money and marriage, and after she was rediscovered in the late 1960s she became a model for Taiwanese writers. Deep dive

The Butcher's Wife (殺夫, 1983)

was originally titled , 'A woman kills her husband'. It is set in (), a fictional town modelled on 's own , in an unspecified past that readers usually place in the early twentieth century. The heroine, (), is an orphan. When she was a child, her starving mother was caught having sex with a soldier in exchange for food and was punished by the clan, and never saw her again. Her uncle marries her off to (), a pig butcher, in exchange for a regular supply of pork.

The novella describes her marriage in direct physical detail. rapes her, starves her when she resists, and enjoys her screams. The slaughterhouse where he works is described with the same unflinching attention as the bedroom, so that the killing of pigs and the treatment of echo each other. Around her, the women of the neighbourhood, led by an old widow named (), gossip, judge and watch, enforcing the rules that keep her trapped rather than helping her. In the end, half mad from hunger and fear, kills her husband with his own butcher's knife and cuts up his body as he would cut up a pig.

The Shanghai papers of the time portrayed her as a depraved woman with a secret lover; others argued she had been driven to it. moved the story to rural Taiwan and kept the central question: what does a society do to a woman before she does something terrible, and who is responsible?

When the novella won the novella prize and was serialised in the paper from 22 September 1983, it caused a storm. Some readers and critics called it pornographic and disgusting, and attacks on 's character followed her for years. Others saw it as the first Taiwanese novel to show domestic violence, marital rape and the economic dependence of women without softening any of it. A story

The crocodile who invented a word

By the early 1990s was gone, the women's movement was openly campaigning, and a new generation of writers was taking on subjects that had been unthinkable a decade earlier. In 1994 two books about gay and lesbian lives appeared in the same year.

The first was 's . Its narrator, (), a forty-year-old gay man, travels to Japan for the funeral of his childhood friend (), who has died of complications from AIDS. Looking back on his lovers, he writes a dense, meditative notebook about desire, aging and death. That a writer once filed under wrote it, and that it won the very first , said a great deal about how far the field had travelled.

The second was 's . It is narrated by a student called () at National Taiwan University in the late 1980s, who falls painfully in love with another young woman and cannot accept herself. Between the chapters runs a comic fable about a crocodile who has to wear a human suit to pass in society, watched by journalists and television presenters who are obsessed with finding out what crocodiles are. Readers understood the crocodile at once as a lesbian hiding inside a straight world.

The book won a recommendation award and became a cult classic. In 1995 Qiu took her own life in Paris at the age of 26, and , written in letters to a former lover, appeared the following year. Her work was translated into English by and , and is now read and taught far beyond Taiwan.

Elsewhere

What was happening elsewhere at the same time

Think about it

A question to take with you

Critics once filed these writers under 'boudoir literature', a label that made their work sound small and private. Yet the private subjects they wrote about, such as marriage, violence at home, money and desire, turned out to be political issues that later shaped Taiwan's laws. Do labels like 'women's fiction' or 'chick lit' still shrink serious work today? Which recent book or film would you defend against a label like that, and what would you say?
