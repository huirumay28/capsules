, 1929 to 1934

For about four years after sound arrived, American studios made films in which women slept their way to the top floor of a bank, gangsters died in the rain as heroes, a chain-gang convict escaped into the dark with no happy ending, and joked about in nearly every line. A moral code for movies already existed on paper, but almost nobody enforced it. Then, in the summer of 1934, Catholic boycotts and a new office run by made law inside the industry, and it shaped Hollywood for the next thirty-four years. This lesson covers why existed, what the films did with their freedom, especially for women on screen, how the crackdown happened, and what was happening in Shanghai, Berlin, Tokyo and Taipei at the same time.

Before

Scandals, censors and a code nobody enforced

American films were censored long before Hollywood had a code. In 1915 the Supreme Court decided in that films were a business, 'pure and simple', and not speech protected by the First Amendment. That meant states and cities could set up their own censorship boards, and many did. A film might be cut one way in New York, another way in Pennsylvania, and banned in Kansas. For the studios this was expensive and unpredictable.

In the early 1920s a series of scandals made it worse. The comedian was tried three times for manslaughter after a woman died following a party in 1921, and was acquitted, but his career was destroyed; the director was murdered in 1922 and the case was never solved. Newspapers presented Hollywood as a place of drink, drugs and . To protect themselves from federal regulation, the studios formed a trade group, the (), and in 1922 hired , a Presbyterian elder and former Postmaster General, to run it. The press called it the .

's first tools were soft. In 1927 his office issued a list known as the 'Don'ts and Be Carefuls': eleven subjects never to show, such as profanity, nudity and ridicule of the clergy, and twenty-five to handle with care, such as sympathy for criminals and 'excessive or lustful kissing'. In 1929 the Catholic publisher and the Jesuit priest drafted a fuller document, the , and the studios adopted it in 1930. Its general principle was that 'no picture shall be produced that will lower the moral standards of those who see it', and that the sympathy of the audience 'shall never be thrown to the side of crime, wrongdoing, evil or sin.'

had rules about almost everything: adultery could not be shown as attractive, crime had to be punished, ministers could not be comic villains, and 'sex perversion or any inference to it' was forbidden, which meant gay and lesbian characters. It also banned 'miscegenation', meaning romantic relationships between white and Black characters, which tells you that protected racial segregation as well as sexual morals. But in 1930 had no teeth. It was administered by a small , led first by and then , which could advise and negotiate but could not stop a studio from releasing a film.

Why then

Sound, the Depression and a struggle for audiences

Two big changes explain why the films of 1930 to 1934 were so bold. The first was sound. After in October 1927, studios rushed to make talking pictures, and dialogue made everything sharper. A silent film could suggest with a look; a talkie could make a joke about it, and audiences heard the joke. Writers from Broadway and newspapers came to Hollywood and brought fast, cynical talk with them.

The second change was the . After the stock market crash of October 1929, cinema attendance at first held up, then fell sharply in the early 1930s. went into receivership in 1933, RKO and Fox were in serious trouble, and lost money. Studios competed for a smaller audience with what sold: , crime, violence and stories about people who had lost faith in banks, police and government. Many critics have argued that the films were bold partly because the audience was angry, and the studios gave them heroes who broke rules that seemed to have failed anyway.

The break

1933 to 1934: the Legion of Decency and Joseph Breen

By 1933 pressure was building from several directions. The , a group of social science studies of how films affected children, were published that year, and a popular summary by , , presented them as proof that films taught crime and . Women's clubs, Protestant groups and newspapers complained about and gangster films. But the decisive force was the Catholic Church.

In October 1933 the Apostolic Delegate to the United States, Amleto Giovanni , called on American Catholics, in a speech in New York, to unite against what he called the growing immorality of films. The American bishops formed the , which asked Catholics to sign a pledge to stay away from immoral films; millions did. In June 1934 Cardinal of Philadelphia told Catholics in his diocese to boycott all films. Studios that were already in financial trouble could not afford to lose a large part of their audience in big cities.

The studios gave in. In June 1934 the created the (), headed by , a Catholic journalist and publicist who had worked on 's enforcement since 1933. From July 1934 every film made or distributed by member companies needed a certificate, and a company that released a film without one faced a $25,000 fine. Because the big studios also owned most of the important cinemas, a film without a seal had almost nowhere to play. read scripts before shooting, demanded changes, and checked the finished films. that had existed since 1930 was now enforced.

The effects were immediate. Married couples in films were now usually shown sleeping in separate twin beds. 's films were rewritten until her jokes lost their edge. Stories about adultery or crime now needed what called 'compensating moral values': the sinner must suffer, the criminal must be caught.

Others see mainly the loss: women's desire and anger, gay characters and honest stories about race disappeared or went underground for decades. Which side do you find more convincing? Elsewhere

What was happening in Shanghai, Berlin, Tokyo and Taipei

Shanghai in the early 1930s had its own golden age of cinema, centred on the and Mingxing studios and a left-wing film movement that made films about workers, poverty and women's lives. The Nationalist government also had a censorship board, which banned films it considered politically dangerous or superstitious. , the biggest star of Chinese silent film, played a mother who sells to pay for her son's education in (1934), directed by , and a writer pushed to suicide by gossip in 's (1935). So in the same years when Hollywood was punishing its bold women on screen, Shanghai cinema was showing women as victims of a cruel society, and the press destroyed its greatest actress. After

Thirty-four years of the Code, and its return to view

The enforced Code lasted much longer than the years it replaced. retired in 1945 and in 1954. Two changes weakened the system. In 1948 the Supreme Court's forced the studios to sell their cinema chains, so a film without a seal could again find screens. In 1952 the Court ruled in , a case about Roberto Rossellini's The Miracle, that films were protected by the First Amendment after all, reversing the 1915 decision. released (1953) and The Man with the Golden Arm (1955) without seals, and Billy Wilder's (1959) was also released without one. Fun fact

Mae West never said the line everyone quotes

The line people remember as 'Come up and see me sometime' is not quite what says in . On screen she tells , 'Why don't you come up sometime and see me?' The shorter version spread through impressions, newspaper columns and later cartoons, and herself began using the popular version afterwards because audiences expected it. Her real line is slower and more casual, which is exactly how she wanted it to sound.
