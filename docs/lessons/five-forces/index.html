Before

The question: why do some industries make more money than others?

In the 1970s, most companies thought about competition narrowly. They watched their direct rivals, tracked market share and tried to win on price or features. Economists who studied industrial organization had a broader view: they measured how concentrated an industry was and linked that to profitability, but their work was written for antitrust regulators, not for managers who wanted to earn more.

was trained in that economics tradition at Harvard and taught at Harvard Business School. In his 1979 Harvard Business Review article 'How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy', he turned the economists' tools around. Instead of asking how to keep industries competitive for the public good, he asked a manager's question: what determines how much profit an industry can earn, and where in that industry can one company find a position that protects it?

His answer was that with direct competitors is only one of . Customers, suppliers, possible newcomers and substitute products all compete for the value an industry creates. When these forces are strong, as in airlines, almost no company earns attractive returns for long. When they are weak, as suggested for industries such as software or soft drinks in his 2008 update, many companies in the industry can be profitable.

The framework spread quickly. 's 1980 book became a standard text in MBA programmes, and the became one of the most widely taught tools in business education.

The framework

The five forces, one at a time

The first force is . reduces profits most when competitors are numerous or similar in size, when industry growth is slow, when exit barriers are high, and when competitors compete on price. stresses that price competition is the most harmful form, because it hands profits directly to customers. Competition on features, service or brand can instead expand the value an industry creates.

The second force is the . A company does not need actual new competitors to feel this force; the possibility of entry alone keeps prices down. The threat depends on : economies of scale on the supply side, network effects on the demand side, the cost to customers of switching, the capital required, incumbents' access to distribution channels, and government policy such as licences.

The third force is the . Suppliers are powerful when they are more concentrated than the industry they sell to, when their product is differentiated or essential, when switching to another supplier is costly, or when they could credibly enter the industry themselves. 's examples include labour unions with strong bargaining positions and suppliers of key components with few alternatives.

The fourth force is the . Customers are powerful when there are few of them or each buys in large volumes, when the product is standardised, when switching is cheap, and when the purchase is a large share of their own costs, which makes them shop hard on price. Buyers can be companies, such as large retailers buying from manufacturers, or individual consumers.

The fifth force is the . A substitute meets the same need through a different kind of product. 's examples include videoconferencing as a substitute for business travel and plastic as a substitute for aluminium. are easy to overlook because they look so different from the industry's own product, but they place a ceiling on prices.

  • : how fiercely current competitors fight, especially on price.
  • : how easily a newcomer could join and add capacity.
  • Suppliers: how much value input sellers can capture.
  • Buyers: how much value customers can capture.
  • : how easily customers can meet the same need another way.

In the 2008 article also warns against common mistakes. Industry growth rate, technology and government are not forces in their own right; they matter only through their effect on the five. A fast-growing industry is not automatically attractive if growth draws in entrants or if suppliers are powerful. And the purpose of the analysis is not to label an industry as good or bad, but to understand why profitability is what it is, and then to find a position where the forces are weakest or to reshape them in the company's favour.

Cases

Airlines, and how Southwest found a position within them

Airlines are the classic example of an industry where all are strong. is intense because carriers offer a largely similar product, flying the same routes at published prices, and because an empty seat earns nothing once the plane departs, so there is constant pressure to cut fares. Suppliers are concentrated: a few manufacturers build large passenger jets, and pilots and other workers are often represented by strong unions. Buyers can compare fares across airlines in seconds, and many treat flying as a commodity purchase. such as cars, trains and videoconferencing limit what airlines can charge on many trips.

described the result in his 2007 letter to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders. He called airlines an example of the worst kind of business, one that 'grows rapidly, requires significant capital to engender the growth, and then earns little or no money.' He added that 'a durable competitive advantage has proven elusive ever since the days of the Wright Brothers.'

Yet 's 1996 article 'What Is Strategy?' uses an airline as its main example of good strategy. did not try to beat the full-service carriers at their own game. It chose to serve price-sensitive travellers on short point-to-point routes between mid-sized cities and secondary airports, and it designed every activity around that choice. lists the elements: no meals, no assigned seats, no interline baggage checking, no premium classes, a fleet of a single aircraft type (the Boeing 737), and very fast turnarounds at the gate that let each plane fly more trips per day.

's point is that each of these activities supports the others. One aircraft type makes maintenance and crew training cheaper and turnarounds faster. Skipping meals and baggage transfers also speeds turnarounds. Frequent departures make the service attractive enough to compete with driving. A rival that copied one feature, such as cheaper fares, would gain little without the whole system. called this , and he argued it is what makes a position hard to imitate.

He also describes what happened when a rival tried. Continental Airlines launched a low-fare operation called Continental Lite that copied some of 's practices while keeping parts of its full-service model, such as travel agent commissions and its frequent-flier programme. writes that the attempt produced delays, congestion, and large operating losses, and that Continental eventually gave up. Trying to straddle both positions meant it got the benefits of neither.

Read together, the two articles show how the frameworks connect. The explain why the airline industry as a whole earns low returns. The positioning argument in 'What Is Strategy?' explains how one company can still choose a set of customers and activities where those forces press on it less, by making itself a cheaper alternative to driving and by keeping its costs below those of rivals who serve everyone.

Fun fact

A capitalist at Kitty Hawk

In his 2007 shareholder letter, wrote that in airlines 'a durable competitive advantage has proven elusive ever since the days of the Wright Brothers,' and that a farsighted capitalist present at Kitty Hawk 'would have done his successors a huge favor by shooting Orville down.' was joking, but the joke is a fair one-line summary of what a analysis of airlines tends to show.
