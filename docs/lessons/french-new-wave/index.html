French New Wave (c. 1958–1964): the cut that showed the cut

Critics pick up cameras in Paris; jump cuts, streets, and the idea that a film has an author.

16 August 2026 c. 1958–1964 About 14 minutes

“Nouvelle Vague” was a press label, then a useful pile of films. It was never one manifesto. Cahiers critics (Truffaut, Godard, Chabrol, Rohmer, Rivette) are one cluster. The so-called Left Bank (Varda, Resnais, Marker) is another. This capsule stays with 1958–1964 and does not pirate the features.

Before

Quality, and a magazine that hated it

Postwar French popular cinema had a manner the writers learned to insult: . Literary properties, careful craftsmen, stars in period dress, a film that did not look as if anyone in particular had wanted it. The insult is unfair to some of those craftsmen. It was also a way of clearing a desk.

In 1954 François Truffaut published “Une certaine tendance du cinéma français.” The essay argues that the director, not the screenwriter-adapter, should be the author — the . Alexandre Astruc had already called the camera a pen () in 1948. Theory here is not a seminar add-on. It is a set of fighting words that several of the fighters then had to live up to.

They were not the first people to be tired of polish. Italian neorealism had already taken cameras into streets and hired non-actors. In Britain, shorts (mid-1950s) treated factory gates and dance halls as worth filming. Paris in 1958 is one capital in a wider argument about what a film is allowed to look like.

The era

Streets, cuts, and who counts as an author

1959 is the year the press needed a name. premiered at Cannes; Truffaut, who had been banned from the festival as a critic, won Best Director as a filmmaker. Alain Resnais’s Hiroshima mon amour and Claude Chabrol’s Les Cousins belong to the same season. The “wave” is a cluster of first features, not a studio program.

It is the film admitting it is made of pieces. That is what “the cut that showed the cut” means here: as something you are allowed to notice, not only as invisible grammar.

Agnès Varda is not an extra in this story. La Pointe Courte (1955) is already a New Wave film by later definition: location, non-professionals, a director who had not come up through the industry ladder. Cléo de 5 à 7 (1962) walks a woman through real Paris time. If “” only fits the men, the word is doing advertising, not criticism.

The New Wave did not pick one church. It made the choice visible. A film could announce that it was being made — look at the camera, break the match cut, quote another movie out loud.

The same years, elsewhere. In Japan, Ōshima Nagisa’s Cruel Story of Youth (1960) and Imamura Shōhei’s early features break with studio polish while Godard is cutting — not as a later “influence,” as a contemporaneous fight inside a different studio system. In Britain, the names move into features (Saturday Night and Sunday Morning, 1960). In Italy, the generation after neorealism (Antonioni, Fellini already famous; Pasolini’s Accattone is 1961) is also arguing about modern life on screen. Paris is one city in that argument.

After

New Hollywood, and a delayed cousin

American studio films of the late 1960s and 1970s — the pile later called New Hollywood — learned that a film could look handmade: location, jumpier editing, a director whose name was part of the sale. Bonnie and Clyde (1967) is the usual bridge example; the traffic of influence is real and also over-told.

Taiwan New Cinema in the 1980s (Hou Hsiao-hsien, Edward Yang, and others) is sometimes filed under “New Wave” in English. It is a delayed cousin, not a contemporaneous peer: different decade, different political problem, a long take more often than a . It belongs in After, not in 1960.

The lasting idea is simpler than the brand. A film can announce its making. Once audiences have seen the cut, they cannot be told that films just happen.

Fun fact

Varda was already there

Agnès Varda’s La Pointe Courte was released in 1955 — four years before Les 400 coups, and before “Nouvelle Vague” was a headline. She was a photographer, not a Cahiers critic. If the story of the New Wave begins only when the boys from the magazine pick up cameras, the date is wrong.
