Taiwan's (c. 1964–1979): writing the island's own people

In the 1960s and 1970s a group of Taiwanese writers turned away from both government and the fashionable of the university journals, and wrote instead about fishermen, sandwich-board men, oxcart drivers, refinery workers and bar girls in the towns of Taiwan. Their stories became known as , or . Before

Three kinds of literature that left ordinary Taiwanese out

To see why mattered, start with who was allowed to write what in Taiwan. From 1895 to 1945 Taiwan was a Japanese colony. In the 1920s a new literature appeared there, written in vernacular Chinese rather than classical Chinese. Its central figure was , a doctor in Changhua who wrote stories such as "" about a vegetable seller ruined by a Japanese policeman. is often called the father of Taiwan's new literature, and the writers who followed him, including , and , wrote about farmers, colonial police and the humiliation of being second-class subjects.

Those writers also had an argument that the 1970s would repeat. In August 1930 published an essay called "" He said that a literature of Taiwan should describe Taiwan and should be written in the language people in Taiwan actually spoke, , rather than in the written Chinese of the mainland. For almost four years writers argued about this, and the dispute is now remembered as the . It ended without a settlement. By the late 1930s the colonial government was pushing writers to use Japanese, and many of the best Taiwanese writers of the 1940s, including the young , published in Japanese.

In 1945 Taiwan was handed to the Republic of China. The writers who had grown up under Japan now had to learn Mandarin and start again. began learning Chinese after the war and was then imprisoned for three years in the of the 1950s. had finished in 1945, a novel about a Taiwanese man who belongs nowhere: the Japanese treat him as inferior, and on the mainland he is suspected of being a Japanese spy. , a Hakka writer from Meinong, won second prize in a government novel competition in 1956 for , but the prize committee closed before it could publish the book. After the Nationalist government moved to Taiwan in 1949 and declared , the literature that received prizes and newspaper space was mostly and nostalgic writing about the mainland that the government had lost. Neither had much room for the farmers, workers and small-town families of Taiwan itself.

The third kind of literature arrived in 1960. Students in the foreign languages department of National Taiwan University, among them , , and , founded a magazine called . The idea

What "nativist" meant

The Chinese term is , literally "literature of the native soil" or "literature of the home village". It is usually translated as . The label was never precise, and the writers argued about it, but the stories grouped under it share several features.

  • Ordinary people at the centre. The main characters are fishermen, peddlers, oxcart drivers, retired soldiers, factory girls, prostitutes and small shopkeepers, not intellectuals.
  • Real places in Taiwan. wrote about the Lanyang plain around Yilan, about Hualien, about the fishing village of Badouzi near Keelung, and about the oil refinery in Kaohsiung.
  • The language people spoke. Dialogue and sometimes narration mix Mandarin with , Japanese loanwords and English, as people in Taiwan actually did.
  • Social change and its costs. The 1960s and 1970s were the years of Taiwan's export boom, of American aid and Japanese investment, of export processing zones and young people moving from villages to factories and cities. Nativist stories ask who paid for that growth.
  • with sympathy. Most nativist fiction is realist in method, close to the nineteenth-century of or the Russian novelists, but it is often funny, and it treats its poor characters with respect rather than pity.

The writers did not agree on the name. In his April 1977 essay argued that the movement should be called , not , because the point was not the countryside but social reality, and that included the workers and slums of the cities. gave a broader definition: was any writing by people living on the land of Taiwan that came out of their shared experience, whatever language they wrote in and whichever province their families came from. Under the surface was a harder question that could not be asked openly under . When these writers said "the native soil", did they mean Taiwan as one part of China, or Taiwan as a place with its own history and identity? The writers themselves gave different answers, and that disagreement would split them in the 1980s.

Deep dive

Huang Chun-ming's sandwich man and his Japanese tourists

"", collected in book form in 1969, is the best-known nativist story and a good place to see how the movement worked. Kun-shu is a young man in a small town who cannot find work. To feed his wife and baby he takes a job advertising films for the local cinema: he paints his face like a clown, puts on an old costume, and walks the streets all day wearing boards with film posters on his front and back. People laugh at him, and his relatives are ashamed. The turn of the story comes at home. One day Kun-shu comes home without his make-up, and his baby son does not recognise him and cries. The child only knows his father as the clown. Kun-shu sits down in front of the mirror and starts to paint his face again.

Nothing in the story is a speech about economics, but the economics are everywhere. Kun-shu has the job because a small town now has a cinema showing foreign films, and because there is no other work for a man like him. The clown face is both his humiliation and the thing his son loves. Huang does not ask the reader to pity Kun-shu. He shows a man who does a ridiculous job seriously because his family depends on it. That combination of humour, close observation and respect is what readers mean when they call Huang a nativist writer.

"" (the title means "goodbye" in Japanese and then in Mandarin), first published in the literary supplement in 1973, is angrier. Its narrator, an office worker named Huang who shares the author's surname, is ordered by his company to take seven visiting Japanese businessmen to his own hometown, the hot-spring town of Jiaoxi, so that they can sleep with its women. He hates what Japan did to China and Taiwan, but he needs the job. On the train back to Taipei he finds himself interpreting between the Japanese men and a Chinese literature student from National Taiwan University who admires Japan, and he deliberately mistranslates, scolding the student for worshipping Japan and pressing the Japanese men to admit what their generation did in the war. The story turns the export economy of the early 1970s, with its Japanese investors and its sex tourism, into a comedy of bad translation, and it asks what kind of independence Taiwan has when its own people serve as guides to its own humiliation.

A story

1977: "The Wolf Is Here"

For more than a decade nativist fiction was simply one kind of writing among others. In 1977 it became the subject of the most important literary argument in post-war Taiwan. The April 1977 issue of the magazine printed three essays at once. welcomed the rise of nativist writing and proposed calling it . , in an essay titled "", warned that some nativist fiction, including 's, risked becoming a tool for expressing hatred. , a novelist who had come to Taiwan with the army, asked whether a land occupied by Japan for half a century could be relied on for its loyalty to Chinese culture, and hinted that too much stress on Taiwan sounded like separatism.

On 29 August the KMT held its second conference on literature and the arts, with more than 270 participants and without any of the writers who were under attack. The answered in the magazines. wrote essays with titles such as "" and argued that writing about Taiwan's poor was part of a Chinese national literature, not a betrayal of it. The two defenders were on the same side in 1977, but their disagreement about China and Taiwan was already visible.

Then the government changed course. At an armed forces literature conference in January 1978, General , head of the military's political warfare department, said that writers should unite with the native soil, because love of one's native soil, enlarged, becomes love of the country and the nation. The official attacks stopped, and the debate ended without the arrests that many writers had feared. The change of tone came as the government faced growing isolation abroad; before the year was out, the United States announced that it would break diplomatic relations with Taipei. The novelist and critic later wrote that in all of Taiwan's post-war history there was no other time when ideas were taken so seriously.

Elsewhere

What was happening elsewhere at the same time

Taiwan was not the only East Asian society where writers under an authoritarian government turned to the lives of the poor in the 1970s. In South Korea, ruled by , the poet published the long satirical ballad "" in May 1970. It mocked the generals, officials and businessmen who had grown rich under the regime, using the rhythms of , a traditional form of sung storytelling. Kim was arrested under the Anti-Communist Law, though the poem said nothing about communism, and the magazine that printed it was closed. In 1974 he was sentenced to death, a sentence that was commuted after protests at home and abroad. Think about it

A question to take with you

In 1977 nativist writers were attacked from one side for being too close to Chinese communism, and a few years later they split among themselves over whether Taiwan was part of China at all. Can a literature that sets out simply to describe the people around it ever stay out of politics, or does the choice of whose life to describe already decide which side you are on?
