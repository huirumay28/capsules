The hinge

1974–1977: the 12-inch single, "I Feel Love," and the night the studio opened

In 1974 , a producer and remixer working in New York, asked a mastering engineer to cut a track on a 12-inch vinyl record at 45 RPM instead of the standard 7-inch 45 or 33⅓ LP format. The wider grooves on a 12-inch allowed the music to be cut louder and with more bass, which meant the record could fill a club without distortion. The format also allowed longer tracks—six, eight, ten minutes—without the compromises of an LP side. The became the format. DJs could play an entire track without interruption; dancers did not have to stop. Moulton pioneered the , taking a radio-length single and stretching it into a club version with longer instrumental sections, breakdowns, and builds. This was not just re-editing. The scene

Clubs, DJs, and the records that filled the floor

By 1977 had recognisable elements: kick drums (a steady kick on every quarter note), hi-hats marking the offbeats, a prominent bass line, orchestral strings or horns, and vocals that could be soulful, breathy, or chanted. The tempo was usually 110–130 beats per minute, fast enough to dance but not exhausting. The structure was simple: verse, chorus, instrumental breakdown, build, return to chorus. The breakdown—where the vocals dropped out and the rhythm section was isolated—was crucial. This was the moment when dancers responded, when the DJ could layer in another record, when the floor moved as a collective body.

12 July 1979

Disco Demolition Night: the records exploded in a baseball stadium

was a rock DJ at WDAI in Chicago. In December 1978 the station switched formats from rock to . Dahl was fired. He moved to WLUP, a rock station, and began an anti-disco campaign on air. He called himself a " sucks" advocate and played rock in defiance of 's chart dominance. On 12 July 1979 Dahl and the Chicago White Sox organised at . The White Sox were playing a doubleheader against the Detroit Tigers. Between games, fans who brought a record would be admitted for 98 cents. Dahl would blow up the records in centre field.

Thousands of fans brought records. Dahl, dressed in military garb, detonated a crate of records between games. The explosion left a crater in the field. The crowd—estimated at over 50,000, far more than the stadium's capacity—stormed the field, tore up the turf, stole bases, and started fires. Security could not control the crowd. The second game was forfeited to the Tigers, one of only a handful of forfeits in Major League Baseball history. The images were broadcast nationally: thousands of mostly white, mostly male fans destroying records, chanting " sucks."

The event was widely seen as more than a music critique. was associated with gay clubs, Black and Latin producers, and female vocalists.
