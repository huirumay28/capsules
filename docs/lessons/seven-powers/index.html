Before

The question: why do some companies keep winning when others cannot catch up?

spent more than twenty years as a strategy consultant and venture investor before writing in 2016, and the puzzle that drove the book was straightforward: some companies earn far more than their competitors, year after year, even when rivals have just as much capital, equally smart people, and no obvious operational mistakes. Competitors try to copy them and fail. Investors throw money at challengers and watch it burn. The incumbent keeps winning. wanted to know what conditions made that durability possible, and whether those conditions could be identified and categorized.

His answer was a framework built on two definitions. First, : the set of conditions creating the potential for . Not a vague moat or a story, but a precise claim about cash flow and time. Second, every has two parts. The is the improvement in a company's cash flow — lower costs, higher prices, or faster growth than competitors. The is the condition that stops competitors from arbitraging away that . Without a , argues, operational improvements are copied and the advantage disappears. Strategy is about building Barriers, not just running well.

He identified seven types. : unit cost falls as volume rises; the is the share needed to match. : product value rises with users; the is the installed base. : a challenger's model that the incumbent cannot copy without wrecking its existing business. : customers lose money, time, or risk if they leave. : affective valence that raises willingness to pay and resists copying. : preferential access to a scarce, valuable asset. : embedded organization or activities that deliver better cost or quality in ways competitors cannot replicate. The book illustrates each with cases: streaming originals, , DVD versus stores, and enterprise software, Tiffany, 's director bench, and 's production system.

The framework gained traction first in Silicon Valley venture and startup circles, then in business schools and corporate strategy teams. called the best book on business strategy I've read, and Spotify founder Daniel Ek praised it as a must-read. NFX, a venture firm, published a widely shared summary in 2018, and the framework began appearing in pitch decks and board discussions. himself cautions that the model is not peer-reviewed economic theory but a practitioner's synthesis, built from watching clients win and lose. Its value is in forcing precise questions: which do you have, when did it form, and what is the that keeps competitors out?

The framework

Seven Powers, each with a Benefit and a Barrier — and the claim that operational excellence is not strategy

The framework starts with a hierarchy. At the top is , defined as the set of conditions that creates the potential for . is explicit: persistent means lasting multiple years, differential means above-industry-average, and returns mean free cash flow or profit, not revenue. Every has two required components. The answers: what does this do for the company? Lower unit costs, higher willingness to pay, lower customer-acquisition cost, or faster volume growth than competitors. The answers: why can't competitors arbitrage this away? If there is no , then by definition you have operational excellence, not . Competitors copy operational improvements, prices or costs converge, and the differential return disappears.

's clearest claim is the negative one: operational excellence — running the business well, serving customers, executing efficiently — is necessary but is not strategy. If your advantage is just doing things better, he argues, competitors will eventually match you, because the improvements are visible and the incentives to copy them are huge. Real strategy is creating a situation where competitors see what you are doing, understand why it works, and still cannot replicate it because the blocks them. That is what the seven Powers describe.

The first is . The : a company's per-unit cost declines as production volume increases, giving it a cost advantage over smaller rivals. The : a competitor must reach equivalent volume to match those costs, which requires massive capital investment or years of slow organic growth, and by the time they arrive the leader has often moved further ahead. 's content spending is 's favorite modern case. In 2013, when released , told reporters that could afford big bets on originals because the cost — $100 million for two seasons — was spread over a subscriber base then above 30 million and climbing. A rival with 3 million subscribers faces ten times the per-subscriber cost for the same content. The is the subscriber-base gap.

is the second . The : the value of the product to each user increases as the number of users grows, leading to higher willingness to pay, lower churn, or faster growth. The : the installed base a competitor must overcome to offer equivalent value, creating a chicken-and-egg problem — users want to join the larger network, but the network cannot grow without users. and Office are the textbook case. Developers write software for because that is where the users are; users choose because that is where the software is. A competitor needs both sides at once. Payment networks — Visa, Mastercard — show the same structure: merchants accept the card because consumers carry it; consumers carry it because merchants accept it.

, 's favorite, is the third . The : a new entrant adopts a business model that is superior for a certain customer segment or use case. The : the incumbent cannot copy that model without damaging its existing business — specifically, without cannibalizing revenue or destroying the profit pool it depends on. DVD-by-mail versus is the case returns to in every talk. made enormous profit from late fees, which analysts estimated at roughly 16 percent of revenue or more; 's model had no due dates and no late fees. could not match 's model without giving up the late-fee profit, so it rationally chose not to — until it was too late. The entrant is against the incumbent's constraints, not its capabilities.

is the fourth . The : a company can charge more or retain customers longer because those customers face a loss if they switch to a competitor — a loss of money, time, risk, or embedded workflow. The : the accumulated investment customers have made in using the product, which grows over time. Enterprise software is the canonical example. Implementing or Oracle ERP takes years, costs millions, integrates across the organization, and stores critical transaction data. Migrating to a rival platform means re-implementation, re-training, data migration risk, and potential business disruption. Even if the competitor's product is better and cheaper, the often exceeds the of moving, so the customer stays.

, the fifth , is the most misunderstood. defines it narrowly: a has only when it creates affective valence — an emotional response that objectively increases customers' willingness to pay, beyond any rational product differences. The : higher prices or stronger loyalty. The : the years of consistent delivery and associations required to build that affective response, which competitors cannot shortcut. Tiffany is his example. The blue box, the Fifth Avenue flagship, the association with engagements and milestones — these let Tiffany charge more for a diamond or silver item than competitors charge for materially similar goods. Merely being well known or having high market share does not count as unless it measurably raises willingness to pay through affective valence.

is the sixth . The : preferential access to a valuable asset — a person, a technology, a location, a license, a supply source — that competitors cannot obtain on similar terms, leading to lower cost or higher product quality. The : the scarcity or exclusivity of the resource itself. 's case is 's director and creative-talent bench. In the decade after , developed John Lasseter, Andrew Stanton, Pete Docter, and Brad Bird, plus the animators, technical directors, and story artists around them. Competitors like DreamWorks or Disney Animation could hire individuals, but they could not replicate the tight, self-reinforcing creative team that had learned together. 's ownership of that talent pool, and the culture that kept them, was a .

, the seventh, is the most company-specific. The : a set of embedded company activities, culture, or organizational routines enables the firm to produce a better product or deliver at lower cost. The : the complexity, interdependence, and tacit knowledge in those processes make them very difficult and time-consuming for competitors to copy, even when they can observe the results. is the standard case. The Production System — continuous improvement (kaizen), just-in-time inventory, respect for workers, and pulling production based on demand — was documented in books, studied by competitors, and still took decades for others to approach. The system's parts are observable, but replicating the culture and discipline that make it work is not.

One question

Can you name your Barrier — the reason competitors cannot copy your Benefit?

Pick the advantage you believe your company or product has: lower cost, higher prices, faster growth, stronger retention. Now name the : what prevents a well-funded, competent competitor from replicating that advantage? If the answer is they have not tried yet or we execute better, would say you have operational excellence, not . If the answer is a specific condition — the scale gap, the installed base, the resource you control, the complexity — then you have a hypothesis to test. Does that actually hold, and for how long?

Fun fact

The consultant who learned from his clients

spent more than 20 years as a strategy consultant and venture investor before writing , and he credits his clients with teaching him the framework. In interviews he has said that he noticed patterns across engagements: some companies had sustainable advantages that competitors simply could not overcome, even with more capital and smarter people. He began categorizing those conditions and testing whether they held across industries. The book is his synthesis of what worked. In lectures he stresses that the framework is not academic theory but a practitioner's tool, built from observing real companies win and lose. That origin gives it both strengths (rooted in commercial reality) and limits (cases selected for clarity, not statistical rigor). The most candid admission: he titled the book not because seven is the perfect number, but because those were the distinct types he could reliably identify and explain.
