and (1624–1683)

In 1624 the built a fort on a sandbar off what is now Anping in Tainan and started governing part of an island that no Chinese state had ever ruled. Thirty-eight years later a loyalist general born in Japan, , better known as , starved that fort into surrender and turned southwestern Taiwan into the base of his own family's kingdom. Twenty-one years after that, the took the island. In six decades Taiwan went from a place mostly outside any state to a corner of a global trading empire, then a loyalist kingdom, then a prefecture, and most of the arguments about who Taiwan belongs to still lead back to this period.

Before

An island on the edge of three worlds

At the start of the seventeenth century, Taiwan was not part of any state. Its people were Austronesian-speaking indigenous communities, among them the on the southwestern plains, the Basay in the north and many others in the mountains and along the coasts. They lived in villages that governed themselves, farmed millet and rice, and hunted the huge herds of sika deer that grazed the western plains. Chinese fishermen and traders from Fujian came every season, especially to the waters around and the southwestern coast, and Japanese and Chinese pirates and smugglers used the island's harbours. But the treated Taiwan as lying outside its borders. , much closer to the mainland, was the edge of what the bothered to defend.

What changed was trade. China produced the silk and porcelain that the rest of the world wanted, Japan and Spanish America produced the silver that China wanted in return, and the government restricted who could trade and where. The Portuguese had Macau from the 1550s, and the Spanish had Manila from 1571, where Chinese junks brought goods to exchange for Mexican silver. The Dutch arrived later, as part of their long war for independence from Spain. Their , the , founded in 1602, was a private company with the power to make treaties, build forts and wage war in Asia. It wanted a share of the China trade and wanted to damage the Spanish and Portuguese while getting it.

's first attempts went badly. It failed to take Macau in 1622, then built a fort on with forced Chinese labour, and was besieged there by a army. In 1624 the offered a deal: leave , which the considered its own, and go to Taiwan, which it did not. The Dutch accepted. That small decision, made by officials who regarded the island as worthless, is how Taiwan entered written history as a colony.

The idea

Why this period matters

Historians often describe as a . The Dutch ruled, taxed and fought, but the people who actually farmed the land, grew the sugar and rice, and bought the hunting licences were mostly Chinese migrants from Fujian whom encouraged to come. The historian , whose book is the standard English account, argues that Chinese settlement of Taiwan on a large scale began under Dutch rule and because of it, not in spite of it.

The period also gave Taiwan its first written versions of several later arguments:

  • Who counts as Taiwan's ruler: a trading company, a loyalist kingdom or a Chinese dynasty. Each of the three governed the island in turn within sixty years.
  • Who the island's first people are. The Dutch records are the earliest detailed descriptions of indigenous villages, and the written language they helped create survived for a century and a half after they left.
  • What means. He is honoured in the People's Republic of China as a patriot who drove out Western colonisers, in Taiwan as a founder of Han Chinese society on the island, and in Japan as a hero born in Hirado to a Japanese mother.
  • How a state decides an island is worth keeping. The gave it away in 1624, and the nearly abandoned it in 1683.

In depth

From a sandbar trading post to a Qing prefecture

stood on , a long sandbar separated from the main island by a shallow lagoon. Across the lagoon later built , at what is now the site of in Tainan, and a Chinese town grew up around the warehouses. The colony's first business was transit trade: Chinese silk bought from Fujian merchants was shipped on to Japan for silver. Its second business was the island itself. The Dutch sold licences to Chinese hunters and exported enormous numbers of deerskins to Japan, where they were used for armour and leather goods. They also encouraged Chinese farmers to settle the plains and grow rice and sugar, and taxed them heavily, above all through a on every Chinese adult, which indigenous people did not pay.

Dutch power over indigenous Taiwan came mostly through violence and then through alliances. In the 1630s and its allies attacked villages that resisted, including and Baccluan. In 1635 they defeated the powerful village of , and in 1636 a punitive campaign on (today's ), in revenge for the killing of shipwrecked Dutch sailors years before, killed or removed nearly its entire population. After that, villages across the southwest submitted. From the 1640s held annual assemblies, called , at which village elders received staffs of office and swore loyalty. Missionaries worked alongside the soldiers, opening schools, translating parts of the Bible into and baptising thousands. In the north, the Spanish held Keelung and Tamsui from 1626, but Manila lost interest, and in 1642 the Dutch took the last Spanish fort.

The colony depended on the Zheng family. had worked as an interpreter for the Dutch in the early 1620s, then built a fleet and in 1628 accepted a commission to suppress the pirates he had once led. When the Dutch governor tried to force open the China trade by attacking Zheng's fleet in 1633, Zheng beat him at Liaoluo Bay off Kinmen. From then on, Chinese goods reached largely on Zheng terms. When the collapsed in 1644 and surrendered to the in 1646, his son took over much of the family's ships and trading network and turned it into the most important loyalist force on the coast.

Inside the colony, tension grew among the Chinese settlers. Deer were overhunted, prices fell, and the and the extortion of low-ranking Dutch soldiers became hard to bear. In September 1652 led thousands of farmers armed mostly with bamboo spears against the Dutch. The uprising lasted about five days. Dutch musketeers and indigenous warriors, who had their own grievances against Chinese settlers, killed around 4,000 Chinese, perhaps a tenth of the Chinese population of the colony. rebuilt in brick but did little about the causes.

He needed a safer base and a source of food. The interpreter , who had fled from , told him that Taiwan was fertile and its defences weak. A story

The Siraya contracts that outlived the Dutch

When arrived in in 1627, the had no written language. The missionaries who followed him wrote down in Latin letters so that they could teach prayers and parts of the Bible, and from 1636 they ran a school in the village. In 1643 reported eighty pupils at , some of whom could already write well.

The Dutch left Taiwan in 1662, but the writing stayed. For the next century and a half, , Taivoan and Makatao villagers used the script to write land leases, mortgages and sale contracts with Chinese settlers, sometimes with a Chinese translation alongside. Chinese settlers called them "" (), and scholars today call them the (). About 140 survive. The latest known one dates from 1813, more than 150 years after the last Dutch minister left.

The contracts are a small, concrete record of what the larger story did to indigenous people. Many of them deal with land passing from indigenous villages to Chinese settlers, a process that began under the Dutch, sped up under the Zheng and continued under the . In recent decades they have also become part of the revival in Tainan, where people descended from those villages have worked to relearn the language and to win official recognition as an indigenous people.

Elsewhere

The world in the age of the trading companies

Taiwan's story in these decades was part of a much wider contest over trade, religion and dynasties.

  • China: the collapsed in 1644, and the spent nearly forty years conquering the south. The fall of the Zheng in 1683 marked the end of organised loyalist resistance, two years after the crushed the .
  • Japan: the Tokugawa shogunate restricted foreign contact in the 1630s, expelled the Portuguese in 1639, and in 1641 confined the Dutch to the small artificial island of in Nagasaki. Japan's demand for Chinese silk and deerskins was one of the main reasons the Dutch wanted Taiwan.
  • The Philippines: Spanish Manila depended on Chinese traders and craftsmen, known as , but distrusted them. Spanish forces killed thousands of Chinese residents in 1603 and again in 1639, and fear of a third massacre was in the air when threatened Manila in 1662.
  • Southeast Asia: the Dutch ran their Asian empire from (now Jakarta) on Java, which they had taken in 1619, and they took Malacca from the Portuguese in 1641, the year before they took Keelung from Spain.
  • Europe: the Dutch Republic was fighting for independence from Spain until the in 1648, which is why Spanish and Dutch forts faced each other in northern and southern Taiwan. England was torn apart by civil war in the 1640s.
  • The Atlantic: the Dutch also lost a colony to a rival at almost the same moment. The Dutch West India Company surrendered to the English in 1664, two years after fell, and it became New York.

Seen next to these events, looks less like a strange episode and more like a typical seventeenth-century outpost, a fortified trading post that grew into a territorial colony. What makes it unusual is how it ended. Very few European colonies in Asia in that century were conquered outright by an Asian army, and 's victory is one of the clearest cases.

After

Three rulers and three memories

Under the , Chinese migration across the strait continued, despite official limits, and by the nineteenth century Han Chinese settlers far outnumbered the indigenous peoples of the western plains. Many plains indigenous groups, including the , were gradually absorbed into Chinese society and lost their languages. was taken apart over time for its bricks, and what survives in Anping today is mostly a later rebuilding on its foundations, though part of the original outer wall still stands.

's reputation has been reshaped by every government that has ruled Taiwan. The , who had fought him, eventually honoured him as a model of loyalty, and in 1875 the official had an official shrine built for him in Tainan. The Japanese colonial government, which ruled Taiwan from 1895, turned the shrine into a Shinto shrine and stressed his Japanese mother. The Republic of China after 1945 presented him as a symbol of retaking the mainland from Taiwan, a parallel that Chiang Kai-shek's government found useful. The People's Republic celebrates him as a national hero who drove out Western colonisers and "recovered" Taiwan. Recent historians, and many people in Taiwan, pay more attention to what all of these versions leave out: the indigenous peoples who lived through Dutch, Zheng and rule, and who were in most cases on the losing side of each change.

One question

Whose history is this?

The same sixty years can be told as the story of a European colony, a Chinese patriot's victory, the founding of Han Chinese Taiwan, or the loss of indigenous land and languages. Each version is built on real evidence. If you were writing the plaque at today, which of these stories would you put first, and what would you be leaving out?

Fun fact

Koxinga was a smash hit in Osaka

In 1715, more than fifty years after his death, became the hero of (), a puppet play by , Japan's most famous playwright. It opened in Osaka in November 1715 and ran for seventeen months, far longer than the few weeks or months most plays lasted, and it became 's most popular work. In Tokugawa Japan, which had closed itself off from most of the world, audiences loved the exotic Chinese setting and the half-Japanese hero.
