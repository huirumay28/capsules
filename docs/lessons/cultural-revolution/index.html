The (1966–1976)

In May 1966 launched a campaign he called the . Students and workers formed , attacked party officials and intellectuals, and fought each other in the streets. The party-state nearly collapsed, millions of urban youths were sent to the countryside, 's chosen successor died fleeing the country, and the decade ended only after 's death and the arrest of the . Historians still argue about the death toll, but most serious estimates for the first five years alone run into the hundreds of thousands or more than a million.

The is still politically sensitive in China, and many local archives remain closed, so numbers and motives are contested. Death-toll estimates vary widely. This lesson uses the range derived by and from thousands of county and city annals: about 1.1 to 1.6 million deaths in 1966–1971, with most deaths caused by repression after restored order rather than by street fighting. Higher figures exist in older secondary sources; treat them as speculative. The movement's official end is dated to the arrest of the on 6 October 1976, though the Communist Party later fixed its verdict in 1981. Personal stories of persecution are better documented for cities than for the countryside. There are no images today rather than risk pictures that do not match their captions.

Before

A failed leap, a damaged chairman, and a party that no longer obeyed him

By the mid-1960s the People's Republic of China was seventeen years old. had led the Communist Party to victory in 1949, remade land ownership, fought the United States to a stalemate in Korea, and built a one-party state. He was still the party's chairman. But his authority inside the leadership had been damaged by the disaster of the .

Between 1958 and 1962 drove China into a crash programme of backyard steel furnaces, people's communes and exaggerated grain targets. The result was famine. Demographic studies put excess deaths in the tens of millions. 's colleagues forced him to step back from day-to-day economic work. State President and General Secretary ran the recovery: they restored private plots, brought back technical experts, and emphasised production over political campaigns.

did not accept that retreat as final. He feared that the Soviet Union under had become "" — socialist in name, capitalist in practice — and that China might follow. In 1962 he told a party conference that class struggle continued under socialism and that the bourgeoisie might reappear inside the Communist Party itself. Defence Minister turned into a school of . From May 1964 the circulated a pocket anthology, , later printed in hundreds of millions of red vinyl copies.

By early 1966 had decided that culture and education were controlled by people who did not share his line, and that the party apparatus under Liu and was blocking him. The campaign that followed was not a spontaneous student revolt that then joined. It was a power struggle he started from the top, using students, workers and as instruments against his own party.

The idea

What "Cultural Revolution" was supposed to mean

The official name was the . 's of 1966 claimed that representatives of the bourgeoisie had sneaked into the party, the government, and the cultural world, and that some of them were people of the type still nestling beside the leadership. The remedy was not a quiet purge by the security services. It was to mobilise the masses, especially the young, to expose and overthrow those "".

  • Attack the : old ideas, old culture, old customs and old habits. In practice this meant temple idols, classical books, family heirlooms, shop signs and anyone labelled a landlord, rightist or intellectual.
  • : 's phrase for attacking the party and state organs that he said were practising a bourgeois dictatorship.
  • Rebellion is justified: a slogan that licensed students and workers to defy local party committees, form and rebel groups, and seize power.
  • as the measure of everything: the , loyalty dances, and public in which victims had to confess political sins.
  • : new governing bodies supposed to unite rebels, old cadres and after power seizures. In many places they became arenas for factional war.

The stated goal was to prevent China from changing colour and to remake the minds of a generation. The immediate political goal was to destroy 's authority, purge and other pragmatists, and restore as the unchallenged centre of the system. Both goals produced chaos that himself later had to stop with .

In depth

From big-character posters to army rule

The opening move was bureaucratic. On 16 May 1966 the Central Committee issued the , dissolved 's Five Man Group that had tried to keep the campaign inside cultural debate, and created a new answerable to 's allies. Within days a at Peking University, written by and colleagues, was broadcast across the country. Work teams sent by Liu and to control campus unrest only made more determined.

On 5 August posted "". The Eleventh Plenum of the Eighth Central Committee rewrote the hierarchy: rose; fell. groups, often named after schools or factories, spread from Beijing. In August and September 1966 — later called in the capital — teachers were beaten, homes of people with landlord or rightist labels were ransacked, and temples and classical artefacts were destroyed as . reviewed waves of in Tiananmen Square and told them that rebellion was justified.

In January 1967 rebels seized power in Shanghai. Similar seizures followed elsewhere. Old party committees were replaced by that were supposed to unite rebels, cadres and soldiers. Instead, rival rebel factions fought for control of cities, factories and even arsenals. In 1967 and 1968 China came close to civil war inside a still-standing party state. authorised the to "support the left", which in practice meant picking winners and suppressing losers. By late 1968 most independent organisations were finished.

The next phase was quieter and longer. From late 1968 the state sent urban secondary-school and university graduates "". Roughly seventeen million young people were relocated over the following years. Officially they were to learn from poor and lower-middle peasants. Many lost years of education; some never returned to the cities. In the countryside, as and Su later showed from county annals, the deadliest years were often 1968 to 1971, when military-backed authorities ran investigation campaigns, and executions after the street fighting had already been stopped.

The — , , and — remained powerful in culture and propaganda, while and, briefly, tried to stabilise the economy and diplomacy. A story

The Little Red Book that outprinted almost everything

Before the became a national upheaval, it had a pocket bible. In May 1964 the General Political Department of the issued for soldiers. The first edition collected a few hundred short extracts under topical headings. 's circle had already been printing a quote in newspaper every day; the book made that practice portable.

Demand exploded once the began. , who has studied the cult in detail, estimates that official printings of the Quotations between 1966 and 1969 ran just over one billion copies inside China, not counting local reprints, foreign-language editions, army-internal versions, or handwritten and mimeographed collections. In 1966 alone more than 200 million copies were printed. Red vinyl covers, plastic wrappers and the ritual of waving the book at rallies made it the visual signature of the decade.

The book mattered because it turned politics into a script anyone could recite. , factory meetings and school assemblies quoted at opponents. After 's death his preface was stripped from later printings, a reminder that even the sacred text was a weapon inside elite conflict. The physical object outlasted the man who had done most to promote it.

Elsewhere

While China turned inward, 1968 shook other capitals

The overlapped with a worldwide year of revolt, but the directions were different. In Czechoslovakia, Alexander Dubček's tried to loosen censorship and introduce "socialism with a human face". In August 1968 Warsaw Pact tanks crushed that experiment. 's China denounced Soviet "" and condemned the invasion as great-power bullying, while at home was already being used to end anarchy. Reform from below in Prague and radicalisation from above in Beijing were not the same project.

In Western Europe and the United States, 1968 meant student occupations, anti-war marches and New Left fascination with Maoist slogans. Some activists waved Little Red Books without living under a state that could send them to the countryside or put them through . In Cambodia, a few years later, the would push agrarian ultra-collectivism even further than 's China, with catastrophic results; their leaders had studied in Paris and looked to both Stalin and as models.

On Taiwan, still under Kuomintang martial law, the was propaganda proof that Communist China destroyed culture and killed its own. Writers and students watched from across the Strait while Taiwan's own White Terror continued. The that appeared on the mainland after 1977, and the nativist literature debate in Taiwan in the same years, both grew out of societies asking what political violence had done to ordinary people — but under opposite regimes.

After

Arrests, a verdict, and a past that is still hard to teach

Trials of the followed. returned to power and, from late 1978, shifted China toward market reforms and opening to the outside world.

In June 1981 the party issued its . It called the a comprehensive, prolonged and severe blunder, held chiefly responsible, and still described as a great Marxist and the principal founder of the People's Republic. That formula — keep the founder, condemn the decade — remains the official line. Public museums and school textbooks treat the period briefly. Independent research, oral history and overseas scholarship have filled in local death counts, factional wars and personal testimonies that the official narrative leaves thin.

The human consequences outlasted the politics. Families broken by , careers ended by class labels, and a generation of shaped Chinese society into the reform era. The party that nearly wrecked survived by restoring hierarchy, rehabilitating many victims, and forbidding any new mass movement of that kind — while retaining 's portrait on Tiananmen Gate.

One question

If Mao feared the party had gone soft, why did unleashing the masses almost destroy the party he needed?

launched the because he believed the Communist Party apparatus was sliding toward Soviet-style and blocking his line. Yet the tools he chose — student and worker rebellion, public humiliation of cadres, dual power in the streets — disabled the very organisation that kept the country governable, until he had to bring back in. Was the a failed attempt to renew revolution from below, or a palace war that used society as its battlefield and then had to put society back in its place?
