The trigger

'We Shall Overcome': folk music and the civil rights movement

'We Shall Overcome' began as a gospel hymn, 'I'll Overcome Someday,' written by Charles Albert Tindley and published in 1901. In the 1940s it was adapted by tobacco workers on strike in Charleston, South Carolina, who sang 'We will overcome' on the picket line. In 1947 Zilphia Horton, music director at in Tennessee, learned the song from the strikers and taught it to . Seeger changed 'will' to 'shall' and added verses. After

The singer-songwriter tradition and what the folk revival left behind

By 1966 the acoustic was over. had gone electric. and were playing rock arrangements of folk songs. The civil rights movement had moved from marches to Black Power and urban uprisings, and 'We Shall Overcome' no longer seemed adequate. The Vietnam War was escalating, and the protest songs of 1963 felt quaint. The , as a commercial and cultural moment, lasted roughly from 1958 to 1965. But its legacy was durable.

The established the as a legitimate artistic role. Before the revival, pop songs were mostly written by professional songwriters (the Brill Building, Motown's in-house teams) and performed by singers who did not write their own material. The normalized the idea that a performer could be the author, that personal experience and political conviction could be the source of a song. , , James Taylor, Carole King, and the entire 1970s genre emerged from this tradition. The also created an audience that valued , sincerity, and lyrical depth over commercial polish. This audience would later support punk, indie rock, and alternative music.

The also demonstrated the commercial power of countercultural music. , , and sold millions of records while singing about injustice, war, and social change. Record labels learned that there was a market for music that challenged the status quo, as long as it was packaged well. This opened the door for the counterculture of the late 1960s—psychedelic rock, protest rock, and the idea that rock music could be art. The 's infrastructure—the coffeehouse circuit, the festival model, the college concert tour—also survived and was adapted by later genres.

The 's contradictions also persisted. It was a movement that celebrated while profiting from borrowed traditions. It was largely white and middle-class while drawing from Black and working-class sources. It claimed to be anti-commercial while producing best-selling albums and Top 40 hits. It championed the 'folk' while turning individual performers into stars. These contradictions were not resolved; they were inherited by the genres that followed. The 's most lasting contribution may be the idea that popular music can be personal, political, and profitable at once.

Harry Smith's Anthology of American Folk Music was sourced from one man's obsessive 78 rpm record collection

In 1952 released , a six-LP set of eighty-four tracks recorded between 1927 and 1932. The booklet included Smith's eccentric liner notes and illustrations. became a sacred text for the 1950s and 1960s . , , , and others learned songs from it. Smith's collection, sourced from one man's obsessive crate-digging, became the foundation of the American folk canon.

  1. The Kingston Trio — Tom Dooley (1958) Capitol Records, 1958. Traditional North Carolina murder ballad arranged by the Kingston Trio. Reached number one on the Billboard Hot 100 in November 1958 and launched the folk revival boom. Sold over three million copies.
  2. The Weavers — Goodnight Irene (1950) Decca Records, 1950. Lead Belly's song arranged by the Weavers. Reached number one in 1950. The Weavers were blacklisted in 1952 during the McCarthy era, ending the first folk wave.
  3. Joan Baez — Silver Dagger (1960) Vanguard Records, 1960. Traditional ballad from Joan Baez's debut album. Baez's clear soprano and minimal guitar defined the folk revival's purist wing.
  4. Bob Dylan — Blowin' in the Wind (1963) Columbia Records, 1963. From The Freewheelin' Bob Dylan. Became the folk revival's anthem. Peter, Paul and Mary's cover reached number two on the Hot 100.
  5. Peter, Paul and Mary — If I Had a Hammer (1962) Warner Bros., 1962. Written by Pete Seeger and Lee Hays in 1949. Reached number ten on the Hot 100. Peter, Paul and Mary were the folk revival's most commercially successful group.
  6. Pete Seeger — We Shall Overcome (1963) Columbia Records, 1963. Civil rights anthem adapted from a gospel hymn. Pete Seeger and Guy Carawan taught it to SNCC activists. Joan Baez sang it at the March on Washington.
  7. Bob Dylan — The Times They Are A-Changin' (1964) Columbia Records, 1964. Dylan's anthem of generational and political change. Title track from his January 1964 album.
  8. Phil Ochs — I Ain't Marching Anymore (1965) Elektra Records, 1965. Anti-war protest song by one of the folk revival's most committed topical songwriters. Ochs remained in the acoustic protest-song tradition after Dylan moved on.
  9. The Byrds — Mr. Tambourine Man (1965) Columbia Records, 1965. Dylan's song arranged as folk rock with twelve-string electric guitars and harmonies. Reached number one on the Hot 100. Marked the birth of folk rock.
  10. Bob Dylan — Like a Rolling Stone (1965) Columbia Records, 1965. Six-minute electric rock single from Highway 61 Revisited. Reached number two on the Hot 100. Dylan had fully moved to electric rock.
