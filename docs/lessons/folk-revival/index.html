The first folk wave: Woody Guthrie, the Weavers, and the blacklist that ended it

The trigger

November 1958: the Kingston Trio's 'Tom Dooley' and the boom begins

They sang traditional folk songs—Appalachian ballads, calypso, sea shanties—in smooth three-part harmonies with strummed acoustic guitars. Their repertoire came from Alan Lomax's field recordings and folk-song collections, but their presentation was collegiate, polished, and apolitical. On stage they wore matching striped shirts and smiled, and their image was closer to a college glee club than to a union hall.

In 1958 released 's version of 'Tom Dooley,' a traditional North Carolina murder ballad about Tom Dula, who was hanged in 1868 for the 1866 murder of Laura Foster in Wilkes County. The song had been collected by folklorist Frank Warner in the 1930s and 1940s. 's arrangement was simple: three voices, three guitars, no drums, no bass. The lyric was mournful but the delivery was smooth. In November 1958 the single reached number one on the Billboard Hot 100. 'Tom Dooley' made folk music profitable. Record labels began signing folk acts. College students, especially at elite universities, bought acoustic guitars and formed folk groups. Folk music became associated with college-educated, middle-class young people, which was a shift from its earlier association with labor organizers and rural singers. The movement

'We Shall Overcome': folk music and the civil rights movement

'We Shall Overcome' began as a gospel hymn, 'I'll Overcome Someday,' written by Charles Albert Tindley and published in 1901. In the 1940s it was adapted by tobacco workers on strike in Charleston, South Carolina, who sang 'We will overcome' on the picket line. In 1947 , music director at in Tennessee, learned the song from the strikers and taught it to . These were call-and-response songs, designed to be sung by a crowd, not by a soloist on a stage.

But the relationship was complicated. Most of the Northern folk singers were white. Most of the movement's activists and leaders were Black. White folk singers raised money, performed at rallies, and brought attention to the cause, but they did not face the same risks. Northern white college students could sing 'We Shall Overcome' at a and go home; Black activists in Mississippi and Alabama sang it in jail after being beaten. Some activists welcomed the support; others resented the attention that white folk singers received. The pattern was familiar from the blues rediscoveries: songs created in Black communities reached national audiences mostly through white performers.

After

The singer-songwriter tradition and what the folk revival left behind

By 1966 the acoustic was over. had gone electric. and were playing rock arrangements of folk songs. The civil rights movement had moved from marches to Black Power and urban uprisings, and 'We Shall Overcome' no longer seemed adequate. The Vietnam War was escalating, and the protest songs of 1963 felt quaint. The , as a commercial and cultural moment, lasted roughly from 1958 to 1965. But its legacy was durable.

The established the as a legitimate artistic role. Before the revival, pop songs were mostly written by professional songwriters (the Brill Building, Motown's in-house teams) and performed by singers who did not write their own material. The normalized the idea that a performer could be the author, that personal experience and political conviction could be the source of a song. , , James Taylor, Carole King, and the entire 1970s genre emerged from this tradition. The also created an audience that valued , sincerity, and lyrical depth over commercial polish. This audience would later support punk, indie rock, and alternative music.

The also demonstrated the commercial power of countercultural music. , , and sold millions of records while singing about injustice, war, and social change. Record labels learned that there was a market for music that challenged the status quo, as long as it was packaged well. This opened the door for the counterculture of the late 1960s—psychedelic rock, protest rock, and the idea that rock music could be art. The 's infrastructure—the coffeehouse circuit, the festival model, the college concert tour—also survived and was adapted by later genres.

The 's contradictions also persisted. It was a movement that celebrated while profiting from borrowed traditions. It was largely white and middle-class while drawing from Black and working-class sources. It claimed to be anti-commercial while producing best-selling albums and Top 40 hits. It championed the 'folk' while turning individual performers into stars. These contradictions were not resolved; they were inherited by the genres that followed. The 's most lasting contribution may be the idea that popular music can be personal, political, and profitable at once.

Fun fact

The Anthology that taught the Village its songs was technically a bootleg

Harry Smith, an experimental filmmaker and record collector, picked eighty-four commercial 78 rpm records made between 1927 and 1932 for of American Folk Music, which released in 1952. Smith chose those years because, as he wrote, 1927 was when electrical recording made accurate reproduction possible and 1932 was when the Great Depression halted folk record sales. Neither Folkways nor Smith held the rights to many of the recordings, some of which belonged to companies that still existed, such as Columbia, so the set was technically a high-profile bootleg; it was only fully licensed for the 1997 Smithsonian Folkways CD reissue, which won two Grammy Awards. The set sold poorly at first, but in it became a shared songbook. later said, 'We all knew every word of every song on it, including the ones we hated.'   1. The Kingston Trio — Tom Dooley (1958) Capitol Records, 1958 (The Kingston Trio - Topic upload). Traditional North Carolina murder ballad. Reached number one on the Billboard Hot 100 in November 1958 and is often credited with starting the folk boom.
  2. The Weavers — Goodnight Irene (1950) Fan upload of the 1950 Decca recording with Gordon Jenkins's orchestra. Lead Belly's song; number one for thirteen weeks in 1950, shortly before the blacklist ended the group's commercial run.
  3. Joan Baez — Silver Dagger (1960) Joan Baez - Topic upload. Traditional ballad that opens her 1960 Vanguard debut; voice and guitar only.
  4. Bob Dylan — Blowin' in the Wind (1963) Official Bob Dylan channel: a live television performance from March 1963, two months before the song appeared on The Freewheelin' Bob Dylan. Peter, Paul and Mary's cover reached number two on the Hot 100.
  5. Peter, Paul and Mary — If I Had a Hammer (1962) Peter, Paul & Mary - Topic upload (2004 remaster). Written by Pete Seeger and Lee Hays in 1949. The trio's 1962 version reached number ten on the Hot 100.
  6. Pete Seeger — We Shall Overcome (1963) Pete Seeger - Topic upload, from his 1963 Carnegie Hall concert album. The civil rights anthem that Guy Carawan taught at SNCC's founding workshop in 1960.
  7. Bob Dylan — The Times They Are A-Changin' (1964) Official audio. Title track of his January 1964 album of topical songs.
  8. Phil Ochs — I Ain't Marching Anymore (1965) Phil Ochs - Topic upload. Title track of his 1965 Elektra album; Ochs stayed with acoustic topical songs after Dylan moved on.
  9. The Byrds — Mr. Tambourine Man (1965) Official audio. Dylan's song with McGuinn's twelve-string electric guitar and Beatles-style harmonies; number one on the Hot 100 in June 1965.
  10. Bob Dylan — Like a Rolling Stone (1965) Columbia Records, 1965. Six-minute electric rock single from Highway 61 Revisited. Reached number two on the Hot 100. Dylan had fully moved to electric rock.
  11. Violeta Parra — Gracias a la vida (1966) Violeta Parra - Topic upload. From Las últimas composiciones (1966), recorded the year before her death; later made world-famous by Mercedes Sosa.
