The concept

What emotional labor means

is the work of managing your own feelings, and the way you show them, because your job requires it. A flight attendant who stays warm and calm with a rude passenger, a nurse who looks reassuring when she is worried, and a debt collector who sounds stern when he feels sorry for the person on the phone are all doing . The feeling, or the appearance of it, is part of what the employer is paying for.

The sociologist introduced the term in her 1983 book : Commercialization of Human Feeling. In a footnote she gave a precise definition. is 'the management of feeling to create a publicly observable facial and bodily display', and it is 'sold for a wage and therefore has exchange value'. When people do the same kind of managing in private life, with friends or family, she called it or , which has use value but is not sold.

This distinction matters. was not saying that it is strange or bad to manage feelings. Everyone does it. People try to feel happy at a wedding, sad at a funeral and grateful for a gift they do not like. Her point was that in modern service jobs a company takes this ordinary private skill, standardizes it in training, supervises it, and sells it to customers as part of the product.

described three linked ideas. are the shared social expectations about what we ought to feel in a situation, such as the rule that you should not feel jealous if you agreed to an open relationship, or that you should be happy at your own birthday party. is the effort we make to bring our real feelings into line with those rules. is what happens when an employer writes the and pays for the work.

She also estimated how common this kind of work was. In she wrote that, by her estimate, roughly one-third of American workers had jobs that subjected them to substantial demands for , and that roughly one-half of all working women did. Women, she argued, had long been expected to be the main managers of feeling in private life, so when that skill was put on the market, it was mostly women who sold it and mostly women who paid its personal costs.

The mechanism

Surface acting and deep acting

To explain how people manage feelings, borrowed from the theater, and in particular from the Russian director , whose method trained actors to produce real emotions on stage rather than simply imitate them. She described two ways of acting.

In , a person changes only the outward display. The face smiles, the voice stays polite, but inside the person still feels irritated, tired or bored. wrote that in we deceive others about what we really feel, but we do not deceive ourselves. The smile feels 'put on' and not 'part of me'.

In , a person works on the feeling itself so that the right display follows naturally. One way is to coach yourself directly, for example by taking deep breaths or telling yourself to calm down. Another way, closer to , is to use imagination or memory: you picture the situation differently, or recall a past experience, until you actually feel the emotion that is required. argued that is more convincing, which is exactly why companies prefer it.

The cost of is harder to see. If a worker succeeds in feeling what the company wants her to feel, her own emotions stop working as reliable signals about what is happening to her. called this a kind of . A worker can become from her smile, which feels like it belongs to the company, and also from her feelings, which have been trained to serve the job. She described workers who came home from long trips still unable to switch off an artificial cheerfulness.

Later psychologists turned these ideas into things they could measure. 's 2000 article in the Journal of Occupational Health Psychology connected to research on emotion regulation, and many studies since then have compared the two kinds of acting. A large by and in 2011 found that was clearly linked to worse well-being, including , and to worse job attitudes, while had much weaker links to harm and some positive links to performance. In other words, faking a feeling all day seems to be the more draining strategy, which fits the experiences recorded.

Case study

Inside the Delta training center

chose as her main case on purpose. She explained that put a higher premium on service than other airlines, had what was perhaps the best training program in the industry, was highly ranked for service, and was headquartered in the South with no union for flight attendants. For all these reasons, she wrote, exaggerated the demands placed on flight attendants everywhere, which made it a useful benchmark for measuring other jobs.

The book opens in the auditorium of 's Stewardess Training Center in Atlanta. sat in a class of 123 trainees listening to a crew-cut pilot in his early fifties. The 1980 pay scale for the job, she noted, began at $850 a month and would rise to about $20,000 a year within seven years, which was an excellent job by the standards of women's work at the time. The trainee next to her wrote in her notebook, 'Important to smile. Don't forget smile.' The pilot told the class: 'Now girls, I want you to go out there and really smile. Your smile is your biggest asset. I want you to go out there and use it. Smile. Really smile. Really lay it on.'

noticed that the pilot called the smile the flight attendant's asset, but training gradually turned it into the company's asset. The smile was supposed to express the airline's confidence that the plane would not crash, its promise that departures would be on time, and its invitation to fly again. A competing West Coast airline, , made the same promise in its radio jingle, 'Our smiles are not just painted on', and painted a smile on the nose of each of its planes.

Much of the training was about . Trainees were told to act as if the cabin were their own living room and each passenger a personal guest in it. They were also taught to reinterpret difficult passengers so that they would not become angry. A passenger who demanded constant attention could be seen as someone afraid of flying, and a drunk passenger could be seen as 'just like a child'. In a recurrent training class for experienced attendants, an instructor answered her own question about how to handle an 'irate', the attendants' word for an angry passenger. She said she imagined that something traumatic had happened in the passenger's life, and told the story of a man who had cursed at her and threatened to report her, and whose son, she later learned, had just died.

These techniques are not cruel, and many people would find them useful in daily life. 's point was about who controls them. The company decided which feelings were correct, taught the methods to produce them, and judged workers on the results. If an attendant became angry anyway, she was told to breathe deeply, talk to herself, and remember that 'you don't have to go home with him'.

then followed the money. After US in 1978, airlines competed harder on price, planes grew larger and fuller, and each attendant served more passengers in less time. called this a , and she described workers responding with a . They could not easily stop serving drinks, but they could smile less broadly, with a quick release and no sparkle in the eyes. That small withdrawal, she argued, showed how much the job had required all along. Workers also challenged company rules, such as body-weight regulations, through grievances, unions and sometimes the courts.

To show the opposite end of the same system, interviewed five , starting with the head of 's own billing department. Where flight attendants were paid to raise the customer's status with warmth, collectors were paid to lower it with distrust and sometimes anger, and they had to suppress sympathy for the people they called. She called the flight attendant and the 'the toe and the heel' of capitalism, two extremes of what jobs can demand of feeling.

The argument

Why gender sits at the center

argued that is not the same job for women and men. She introduced the idea of a . People with higher social status are protected, to some degree, from having their feelings treated badly. People in lower-status categories, which she listed as women, people of color and children, have a weaker shield, so their feelings are more easily dismissed or attacked.

On the airplane this meant that a female flight attendant absorbed more rude speech and more complaints than a male colleague doing the same work. She noted that a day spent with people who resist authority in women is a different experience for a woman than for a man, and that about 85 percent of flight attendants at the time were women. Passengers also brought their expectations from home, asking women why they were not married and leaving more of the nurturing work, such as handling babies and elderly passengers, to them. Female attendants, wrote, became the airline's main shock absorbers for customer frustration.

This is why the concept connects so naturally to feminist arguments about invisible work. 's next famous book, (1989), studied how working mothers came home to a second full shift of housework and childcare. It also explains why the idea later moved out of the workplace and into the home, as the next section shows.

After the book

How the term grew, and why Hochschild objected

For about thirty years, was mainly a term used in sociology, psychology and management research, applied to nurses, call-center workers, teachers, retail staff and many other jobs. Then in 2017 the writer published an essay in Harper's Bazaar titled 'Women Aren't Nags—We're Just '. She used to describe the unpaid planning, remembering and nagging that women in heterosexual relationships often do at home, such as booking appointments, tracking birthdays and noticing what needs doing. The essay went viral and became the book (2018).

In November 2018 interviewed for The Atlantic under the headline 'The Concept Creep of '. said that much of what was now called was simply labor. Writing Christmas cards or keeping a to-do list is work, and it is unequally shared, but it is not the same as managing your feelings as part of a paid job. She did agree that some home tasks involve real , such as handling your own anxiety while caring for others.

later said she would have preferred the phrase 'invisible labor', but that her publisher asked her to keep the term readers already recognized. The disagreement is a useful lesson about concepts in general. A precise term becomes popular because it names something people feel, and the popularity then blurs what it originally meant. When you read 'emotional labor' today, it helps to ask which meaning the writer intends: feeling management that is sold for a wage, in private life, or the broader idea of unnoticed household labor.

  • ('s sense): managing feeling and its display as part of a paid job, under rules set by the employer.
  • : the same kind of feeling management in private life, under social .
  • Invisible or mental labor: unpaid planning, remembering and organizing work at home, which may or may not involve managing feelings.

Elsewhere

Taiwan's cabin crew strikes

Flight attendants, the workers at the center of , have also been at the center of some of Taiwan's most visible labor disputes. In June 2016 members of the struck against . The union's strike vote passed with 2,535 votes in favor and 9 against, the strike began at midnight on 24 June, and an agreement on the union's demands, including restored rest time and a higher overseas allowance, was reached the same evening. Flights returned to normal on 27 June. It was the first strike in Taiwan's aviation sector.

In 2019 the same union led a strike of flight attendants that lasted 17 days, from 20 June to an agreement signed on 6 July, with the strike formally ending on 10 July. It was the longest strike in Taiwan's aviation history. reported that the strikers were all women, because EVA did not hire male flight attendants at the time, and that 2,949 of the airline's roughly 4,600 cabin crew took part. EVA cancelled more than 2,000 flights. The workers won some demands, such as higher per-flight allowances and a seat on employee review committees, but not others.

These strikes were mainly about pay, allowances and rest. They still connect to 's argument in a concrete way. Her book showed that the friendly, unhurried service passengers expect depends on time and staffing, and that when time is squeezed, the emotional part of the job is the first thing workers can no longer supply. Rest between flights is not only a physical issue; it is part of what makes the smile possible. For thinking about cool dairy, the same question applies to any customer-facing work, paid or volunteer: who is doing the emotional work of making customers feel welcome, and is that work counted?

Fun fact

It started with a dish of peanuts

In the preface to , traced her interest in managed feeling to her childhood. When she was twelve her parents joined the , and at diplomatic receptions she passed a dish of peanuts among the guests while looking up at their smiles. Afterwards her parents would decode the gestures: a tight smile or an averted glance carried messages between governments, not just between people. She remembered wondering whether she had passed the peanuts to a person or to an actor, and where the person ended and the act began.
