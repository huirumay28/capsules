Before

Defeat, revolution, and a broken industry

German cinema before 1918 was already an industry. Studios in Berlin, actors, serials, melodramas, and wartime propaganda were facts of commercial production. The Kaiserschlacht broke that continuity. Germany lost World War I in November 1918, the Kaiser abdicated, revolution convulsed the streets, and the Treaty of Versailles in 1919 imposed reparations, territorial losses, and a clause declaring German war guilt. The began amid political chaos: Spartacist uprising and Freikorps violence in January 1919, the Kapp Putsch in 1920, assassinations of politicians, competing visions of what Germany should become. Studios that had made patriotic features pivoted to civilian stories, but the economic ground was unstable. would peak in 1923 when a wheelbarrow of marks bought a loaf of bread.

Material shortages shaped production. Film stock, electricity, and set-building resources were scarce and expensive. That scarcity is part of why 's looked so strange: painting shadows and angles directly onto canvas flats cost less than constructing elaborate three-dimensional architecture and lighting it conventionally. Aesthetics and economics collaborated. The Treaty had also restricted German film exports, so studios initially aimed at a domestic audience nursing defeat and processing trauma. When premiered in February 1920, critics saw crooked fairground streets, a hypnotist, a somnambulist murderer, and an asylum frame story that some read as allegory for authority, madness, and obedience. Historians later borrowed the label "Expressionism" from painting and theater to name what they were watching on screen.

The "before" is therefore double: a commercial studio habit built before the war, and the defeat-and-revolution rupture that left filmmakers working with tight budgets, unstable politics, and a public that had just survived trenches, occupation, hunger, and civil street fighting. Expressionism did not invent German cinema. It was one response — distorted, psychologically heightened, visually radical — to that breakage.

Elsewhere

Same years on other screens

Look sideways without turning the decade into a worksheet. In the Soviet Union, roughly contemporaneous with and , Lev Kuleshov's workshop was demonstrating the Kuleshov effect and Sergei Eisenstein was building Battleship Potemkin (1925) and October (1928) from collision montage — a related capsule on this site. Soviet theorists studied German films as technique and as ideological opponents: they admired Griffith's crosscutting and German camera craft while rejecting what they saw as bourgeois psychology and decorative distortion. The two movements are neighbors and rivals, not parent and child. Both responded to postwar rupture (defeat in Germany, revolution in Russia) with radical form, but leaned toward nightmare and individual psyche while aimed at collective argument and material dialectics.

In France the 1920s saw Impressionist cinema — Germaine Dulac, Jean Epstein, Abel Gance — experimenting with subjective camera work, optical effects, and rhythm. Gance's Napoleon (1927) used rapid cutting and widescreen triptychs; Epstein's The Fall of the House of Usher (1928) borrowed Gothic atmosphere but with different visual language than German shadows. In Hollywood the studio system refined continuity editing and star power; German emigres would arrive after 1933 and seed 's shadows and cynicism (Robert Siodmak's The Killers, Edgar G. Ulmer's Detour, and 's own Hollywood work). In Japan the 1920s were peak silent years with benshi narrators and studio genres developing parallel to European experiments. In Britain documentaries and Ealing features worked social observation; in Mexico the Golden Age was beginning. Same calendar, different industrial roofs and political pressures.

What traveled from Weimar was influence, not a franchise. Universal Horror in 1930s Hollywood — Dracula (1931, shot by ), Frankenstein, The Mummy — borrowed Expressionist lighting and emigre talent. inherited , urban dread, and fatalism from the filmmakers who fled. critics later wrote about and as auteurs and moral witnesses; a related capsule on this site. The style's afterlife in classrooms, festivals, and genre DNA is a migration story as much as an aesthetic one.

After

Emigration, noir shadows, and long genre afterlives

By 1933 the was over. Hitler became Chancellor; Joseph Goebbels took control of German cinema through the Reich Film Chamber. Jewish filmmakers and those politically suspect were expelled or chose exile. , Billy Wilder, Robert Siodmak, Edgar G. Ulmer, Paul Leni (who had already died in 1929 in Hollywood), , , , and many others fled to France, Britain, and Hollywood. Those who stayed worked under propaganda demands or made entertainment that avoided politics. Leni Riefenstahl's Triumph of the Will (1935) showed what monumental cinema looked like under the Nazis — spectacle serving the state, Expressionism's distorted psychology replaced by regimented masses and Führer worship.

The emigres seeded American : shadowy cinematography, urban paranoia, morally ambiguous protagonists, and stories of entrapment. Siodmak's The Killers (1946), Ulmer's Detour (1945), 's The Big Heat (1953), Wilder's Double Indemnity (1944) — Weimar lighting and fatalism meeting American crime stories. Horror continued the line: Universal's Dracula and later creature features, Roger Corman's Poe adaptations, and Tim Burton's Expressionist-influenced design for Edward Scissorhands and The Nightmare Before Christmas. critics wrote about and in Cahiers du Cinéma before making their own films; later cinephile directors from Martin Scorsese to Guillermo del Toro cited the Weimar silents as foundational. Film schools still teach 's , 's vampire iconography, and 's dystopian city as basic visual literacy.

The label remains useful if you keep it fuzzy — a stylistic and thematic cluster responding to 's defeat, instability, and trauma, roughly 1919 to 1931, closed by sound technology and political catastrophe, then scattered by emigration into global influence. Not a single manifesto, not every German film, but a visible enough tendency that critics and historians still argue about its edges and its politics.

A question

A question to keep asking

If a fairground town's streets are painted crooked and its shadows are drawn onto canvas instead of cast by lights, what anxiety or truth about authority, obedience, and reality is the film asking you to see that a realistic set would hide?

Fun fact

Caligari's painted sets were partly a budget solution

's wildly distorted streets and buildings — , crooked windows, impossible perspectives — are often read as pure Expressionist intent. They were also cheap. Post-war German studios faced material shortages and tight budgets; painting shadows and angles directly onto canvas flats cost less than building elaborate three-dimensional sets and lighting them conventionally. Necessity and aesthetics collaborated. The result looked so strange that it became the movement's visual signature, even though later Expressionist films often returned to more realistic sets and relied on lighting for mood.
