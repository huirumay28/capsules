High Modernism (c. 1910–1930): the sentence breaks

Woolf, Joyce, Eliot — and, in the same years, 新青年 and Lu Xun’s broken “I.”

16 August 2026 c. 1910–1930 About 14 minutes

“High modernism” is a later classroom name for a cluster of difficult, self-conscious works written mostly in English, French, and German around the 1910s–1920s. It was never a membership. This capsule uses the name, then looks at the same decades in Chinese vernacular writing — not as an appendix, as the same years.

Before

The stable narrator

Nineteenth-century realist and naturalist novels — in English, French, Russian — often trusted a narrator who could stand outside the scene and tell you what it meant. Even when the news was grim, the grammar was stable. Someone was in charge of the sentence.

That stability was already under pressure before 1914: interior monologue in Dostoevsky, Flaubert’s , late James. High modernism does not invent inwardness. It breaks the sentence that used to carry it, and it does so after a war that made the old voice sound like a lie.

The era

Fragments, magazines, and more than one vernacular

The First World War is not background music. Eliot’s (1922) is a poem that cannot keep a single voice; quotation and ruin do the work a narrator used to do. Woolf’s (1925) puts a shell-shocked man and a party on the same June day. Joyce’s (serialized from 1918, book 1922) gives one Dublin day more interior traffic than a nineteenth-century decade.

The broken “I” is not a London export. It is a contemporaneous solution to a different classical inheritance.

After

Reaction, then other modernisms

By the 1930s and 1940s a reaction is already audible: calls for plainer prose, political clarity, a novel that will do something. Mid-century English and American fiction often treats high modernism as a brilliant dead end. That verdict is a period style of its own.

Later postcolonial writers — in English and out of it — take the broken sentence and the unreliable “I” into other empires. They are not a delayed or a delayed Woolf. They are what happens when the method leaves the original capitals.

A question

Leave this on the table

If the narrator is no longer stable, who is the “we” in “we moderns”?

Fun fact

1918, twice

In May 1918 Lu Xun’s 狂人日記 appeared in 新青年. That same year The Little Review in New York began serializing Joyce’s Ulysses. Two broken first persons, two little magazines, no transatlantic plot connecting them. The coincidence is chronological, which is enough.
