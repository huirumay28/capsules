Before

Ruins, graveyards and the pleasure of fear

In the middle of the eighteenth century, 'Gothic' was mostly an insult. It referred to the Goths who sacked Rome, and by extension to anything medieval, rough and unclassical: pointed arches, dark cathedrals, superstition and feudal lords. Educated taste preferred the balance and reason of Greek and Roman models, and the novels of the day, by writers such as and , were set in recognisable modern England and aimed to be realistic and morally useful.

At the same time, a counter-taste was growing. The 'graveyard poets', such as , whose appeared in 1751, made melancholy, tombs and night into fashionable subjects. Collectors began buying medieval objects, and wealthy men built fake ruins in their gardens. The fake Scottish epic of 'Ossian', published by in the 1760s, made ancient bards and misty landscapes fashionable across Europe.

The most important idea came from the Irish philosopher . In (1757), he separated the beautiful, which is small, smooth and pleasant, from , which is vast, dark, powerful and frightening. argued that whatever excites ideas of pain and danger, so long as we are not actually in danger, produces the strongest emotion the mind is capable of feeling. This gave writers a respectable reason to frighten their readers on purpose.

The spark

Horace Walpole's dream and The Castle of Otranto

was the youngest son of , usually counted as Britain's first prime minister. He was a member of parliament, a famous letter-writer and a collector, and from 1749 he turned a small house at Twickenham, west of London, into , a toy-like castle with battlements, stained glass and a library modelled on old tombs. The house helped launch the in architecture.

In a letter to the antiquarian dated 9 March 1765, explained that in early June 1764 he had dreamed he was in an ancient castle and saw, on the uppermost banister of a great staircase, a gigantic hand in armour. That evening he began writing without knowing what he meant to say, and he finished the book in about two months. came out on Christmas Eve 1764, dated 1765, in an edition of 500 copies.

The first edition pretended to be an English translation, by one 'William Marshal', of an Italian text by 'Onuphrio Muralto', supposedly printed in Naples in 1529. The story is deliberately wild. On his wedding day, Conrad, the son of , Prince of , is crushed to death by a giant helmet that falls from the sky. , desperate to keep his family's stolen title, decides to divorce his wife Hippolita and marry Conrad's bride, Isabella, who flees through the castle's underground passages. Portraits walk out of their frames, statues bleed and giant pieces of armour keep appearing until the castle collapses and the rightful heir is revealed.

The book sold well, and when admitted his authorship in the second edition of 1765 he added the subtitle 'A '. In the new preface he explained his aim: to combine the old romance, where anything could happen, with the modern novel, where characters behave like real people. Critics who had praised a medieval curiosity now attacked a modern fake, but the formula of an ancient building, a tyrant, a persecuted young woman, a hidden crime and a lost inheritance had been set.

The novel moves out of the castle and into the laboratory. , a young Genevan student, builds a man from parts of corpses and brings him to life, then runs away in disgust. Unlike the ghosts of or the devils of , the here is created by human science and human neglect, which is why is also often called the first science fiction novel.

Feminist critics have read it as a book about birth and motherhood written by a teenager who had lost her mother at birth and her own first baby in 1815, and about what happens when a man tries to create life without women. The creature's complaint, that he was made and then abandoned without love or a place in society, has been read as a story about orphans, slaves, the poor and anyone treated as a monster.

The idea

Why this matters

  • began as a game, a fake medieval translation by a rich collector, but it quickly became a serious way of writing about power: who owns the house, who can lock the doors and who inherits.
  • Its central plot, a young woman trapped by a man who wants her property or her body, reflected real law. Before the , a wife's money belonged to her husband, and a family could confine a woman in a private madhouse with little oversight.
  • Women dominated the genre as writers and readers. was the best-paid novelist of the 1790s, and the mockery of 'Minerva novels' was partly mockery of women's reading itself.
  • 's distinction between (suspense and the unknown) and (the shock of seeing) still describes the difference between a slow ghost story and a slasher film.
  • moved fear from the supernatural to human responsibility: the monster is what happens when someone creates a life and then refuses to care for it.
  • The same decades saw a parallel boom in supernatural fiction in China and Japan, where the ghost or fox woman was more often the heroine than the victim.
