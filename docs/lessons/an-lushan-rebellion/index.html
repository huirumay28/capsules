The (755–763)

In December 755 , a frontier general who commanded three of the Tang empire's northern armies, marched south from near today's Beijing. Within seven months he held both Tang capitals, the emperor had fled to Sichuan, and the emperor's favourite consort, , had been put to death by his own guards. The war dragged on for eight years. The survived, but it never again controlled its own provinces the way it had before, and the shock of the war shaped Chinese poetry, calligraphy and politics for centuries.

Before

A golden age with a weak point on the frontier

When came to the throne in 712, the was almost a century old. Its capital, , was one of the largest cities in the world, with perhaps a million people inside and around its walls. Merchants from Central Asia, Persia and Korea lived there, Buddhist monks travelled to and from India and Japan, and the court collected taxes from a registered population of more than fifty million. The first part of 's reign, the Kaiyuan era, is still remembered in Chinese as a model of good government.

The weak point was the army. Early Tang China had relied on farmer-soldiers who served in rotation and went home. As the empire pushed into Central Asia, Manchuria and the southwest, that system could not keep enough men on distant borders. The court replaced it with large professional armies stationed permanently on the frontier, each commanded by a , or . By the 740s there were ten of these commands, and together they held most of the empire's trained troops. The capital region itself had relatively few.

Over time the gained control not only of soldiers but of supplies, local finances and appointments. 's long-serving chief minister preferred to give these commands to non-Han career soldiers rather than to scholar-officials, partly because such men had no family network at court and could not become political rivals. The policy made sense for 's own position. It also placed huge armies in the hands of men whose loyalty rested on the emperor's personal favour.

In the second half of his reign grew less interested in daily government. He devoted himself to music, Daoism and, from the 740s, to , who became his , . Her relatives were promoted and enriched. When died in 752, her cousin took over as chief minister. Meanwhile the empire suffered defeats on the frontier: at Talas in Central Asia in 751, and in costly campaigns against the kingdom of Nanzhao in the southwest.

The idea

Why historians call this a turning point

Chinese historians usually call the war the , after its two main leaders, and . It is often treated as the dividing line between the early Tang and the late Tang, and sometimes as the dividing line between early imperial China and later imperial China.

The reason is not that the Tang fell. It did not. The reason is that the war changed how the empire worked. Before 755, the court in appointed officials, registered households and taxed them directly across most of the country. After 763, large regions were run by who passed their posts to sons or chosen officers, kept their own taxes, and obeyed the court only when it suited them. The court survived by relying on the rich south, on state monopolies such as salt, and on its own palace armies, which were increasingly controlled by eunuchs.

  • Military power moved from the centre to the provinces. The system that caused the war became the normal form of government in much of the north.
  • The economic centre of China began moving south. The Yangzi region was barely touched by the fighting and became the court's main source of revenue.
  • The empire lost Central Asia. Troops were withdrawn to fight the rebels, and Tibet and the took over much of the western frontier.
  • Attitudes toward foreigners hardened. Because and many of his officers were of , Turkic or other non-Han origin, the war fed suspicion of outsiders in a society that had been unusually open.
  • Literature changed. Poets such as wrote about war, refugees and conscription in a direct, personal way that later readers saw as poetry bearing witness to history.

A story

A calligrapher's rough draft for a dead nephew

When rebelled, was prefect of Pingyuan in Hebei and his cousin was prefect of nearby Changshan. Both declared for the Tang. 's son carried messages between them. In early 756 rebel troops besieged Changshan. Help did not arrive, the city fell, and and his son were killed. was taken to and executed after he refused to submit to .

In 758, after Tang forces had retaken the area, sent a relative to search for the bodies. Only 's head was recovered. then wrote a short memorial text addressed to his dead nephew. It survives not as a clean copy but as a rough draft, with crossed-out words, inserted phrases and lines that grow faster and heavier as the text goes on. In one line he writes that traitorous officials did not come to the rescue, the lone city was surrounded, the father was captured and the son died.

Chinese connoisseurs came to value the draft precisely because it is unfinished. It records a man writing in grief, correcting himself as he goes. Later critics ranked it second only to 's among works of running script. It is one of the clearest examples of how a political catastrophe entered Chinese art not through official history but through a private document.

Elsewhere

Eurasia in the 750s: an age of revolts and new dynasties

The happened during a period of upheaval across Eurasia. Historian has pointed out that between about 742 and 755 most of the great states of the continent experienced a major rebellion, revolution or change of dynasty.

  • The steppe: in 742–744 the Second Turkic Khaganate was overthrown and replaced by the . The then became the Tang's indispensable and expensive allies against .
  • The Islamic world: the , launched from Khurasan in 747, overthrew the Umayyad caliphate in 750. In 751 Abbasid forces defeated the Tang at Talas, and in 762 the caliph founded Baghdad as a new capital.
  • Western Europe: in 751 deposed the last Merovingian king and became the first Carolingian king of the Franks.
  • Tibet: the Tibetan emperor was assassinated in 755. His successor exploited Tang weakness after the rebellion, and Tibetan forces occupied for about two weeks in 763.

After

A dynasty that lived on in a new shape

The Tang lasted another 144 years after the rebellion ended, so it would be wrong to say the war destroyed it. But the court never fully recovered control of the north. The surrendered Yan generals in Hebei ran their provinces as hereditary domains. Other followed their example, and in 781–786 several of them rose against the court at once. In 783 a mutiny even forced Emperor Dezong to flee .

The court adapted. The old tax system, based on registering every household and giving it equal land, had collapsed with the census. In 758 the government created a state , which under the official became one of its largest sources of income. In 780 's taxed households according to their property and land, collected in summer and autumn. Historians regard this as one of the most important fiscal reforms in Chinese history, and it set the pattern for later dynasties.

There was a real recovery under (reigned 805–820), who brought several rebellious provinces back under control. The final collapse came later, after the of 874–884 devastated both north and south. In 907 the warlord deposed the last Tang emperor, and China split into the . The Song dynasty, which reunified much of China in 960, deliberately kept generals under civilian control, partly because of the memory of what had done to the Tang.

One question

Who was really to blame?

The traditional histories blame a few people: for his ambition, for provoking him, for distracting the emperor, and for neglecting government. Modern historians put more weight on the system: the court had placed most of its professional soldiers on the frontier under commanders who controlled their own money and men. If had been more careful and the Yang family less greedy, would the empire have avoided a war like this one, or was some kind of military revolt likely once the system existed? And why did the women at the centre of the story, above all, end up carrying so much of the blame?
