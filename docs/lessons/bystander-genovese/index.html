The concept

What the bystander effect claims

The is a claim about groups, not about one Queens street. In its ordinary textbook form it says: a person is less likely to help in an emergency when other people are present. The more potential helpers, the less help. The name that stuck to the mechanism is . Each person feels less personally on the hook. Someone else will call. Someone else is closer. Someone else already knows what this is.

That is a statement about psychology. It can be true, false, or true only sometimes. It does not, by itself, require a famous murder, a number of windows, or a city that had forgotten how to be a neighbor. Those arrived later, as a story to hang the claim on.

A second, related claim in the same literature is : people treat other people’s inaction as information. If no one moves, the smoke is not a fire. If no one calls, the noise is not a crime. Silence becomes evidence. is about who must act. is about whether anything is happening. Textbooks often braid them. They are not the same sentence.

The mechanism

How Darley and Latané tested helping in a group

and designed emergencies you could run twice. A subject hears a seizure over an intercom, or sees smoke under a door, alone or with other people present — sometimes other subjects, sometimes confederates who have been told not to move. In 1968, in the , they reported “”: group size inhibited intervention. The 1970 book is . The Genovese name is in the preface of a field. The data are from rooms where the experimenters could count.

Why did they think it worked that way? Because responsibility, in their account, is not a fixed moral stock. It divides. A person alone owns the next move. A person in a group can wait, look, and assume the waiting is shared. Add the fear of doing the wrong thing in public — of being the one who overreacts — and inaction has two supports: I am not the one, and this may not be the thing. That is the theory. It is a theory about laboratories and, later, field studies. It is not yet a theory about .

After

What the case still teaches — and what it taught wrongly

What it taught wrongly is easy to list. There were not 38 people at 38 windows watching a single continuous crime and choosing, each of them, not to dial. Some neighbors did call. Someone went downstairs. The layout and the two attacks made a single shared audience impossible. and others later argued that a woman’s voice at 3 a.m. was often classified as a private quarrel — a gendered reading the urban-apathy story did not pause for. ’s film records reporters who already doubted Gansberg’s count and did not pick the fight with Rosenthal. The number that organized a research program was already unstable on the morning it was printed: the headline said 37, the story said 38.

What the case still teaches is not a shrug. Some neighbors did too little. The emergency number was a maze. A press story can fit a ready moral — , a city of strangers — and become harder to unwrite than to run. The bystander literature is larger than one street and has been tested without Gansberg’s count. The teaching problem is the one this lesson is for: a discipline can be right about a mechanism and still be wrong about the story it uses to make students care.

Keep the experiments. Label the newspaper story. Do not let the correction become a second simplification — that no one failed anyone that night, or that groups never freeze a person. does not need 38 silent windows. It never did.

A question

A question to keep asking

If a field’s most famous example turns out to be a newspaper story that was not the night, what should it do with the example — drop it, label it, or keep using it because the experiment still works?

Fun fact

Thirty-seven, or thirty-eight

headline of 27 March 1964 counted 37. The story under it counted 38. Reprints have used both. The slip is small and perfect: the number that organized a research program was already unstable on the morning it was printed. What was stable was the moral — a city that would not pick up the phone.
