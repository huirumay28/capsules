Kitty Genovese and the bystander that wasn’t

A 1964 murder in Kew Gardens, a Times story about 38 witnesses, a psychology of helping — and the later proof that the parable was not the night.

16 August 2026 1964 (and the after of the story) About 15 minutes

Catherine “Kitty” Genovese was murdered in Kew Gardens in the early hours of 13 March 1964. This capsule does not describe wounds or sexual violence. It does not use a police portrait of her or of the killer. The Times story of 27 March 1964 is treated as a primary source that later reporting — including the Times itself in 2016 — found flawed. The psychology that followed is real experimental work. The 38 silent witnesses are not.

Before

A quiet neighborhood, a loud city, no 911

in 1964 was a Queens neighborhood of brick apartments, a Long Island Rail Road station, and Austin Street shops — statistically treated, then, as a safe borough compared with the Manhattan the magazines preferred to fear. , 28, managed a bar in Hollis and came home in the small hours. She shared an apartment at 82–70 Austin Street with Mary Ann Zielonko. The city around them was in a year of other headlines: a World’s Fair opening in Flushing Meadows, a few stops away; a Civil Rights Act moving through Congress; a press already fond of the phrase “.”

There was no . A neighbor who wanted the police dialed a precinct or the operator and hoped the line was free. That fact sits under every later sentence about people who “didn’t call.” Some people did try. The system they reached was not a single button.

The era

A night, then a front page, then a lab

In the early morning of 13 March 1964 Winston Moseley followed Genovese from the station parking lot and attacked her on the Austin Street side of the building. A neighbor, Robert Mozer, shouted; Moseley left and came back. The second attack was in a rear hallway, out of the street’s sight. Later reconstructions — Cook, the Times in 2016, the 2007 American Psychologist paper — agree on the shape: two attacks, not three; a layout that made a single continuous audience impossible; knife wounds that made a long, loud scream unlikely after the first blows. She was taken away by ambulance about 4:15 a.m. and died on the way to the hospital. , a neighbor, had gone to her and held her until help came.

The Times of 14 March ran a short notice. The story that lasted was assigned two weeks later, after Police Commissioner Michael J. Murphy told metropolitan editor , over lunch, that the Queens case was “one for the books.” Martin Gansberg’s piece of 27 March 1964 went on page one: “ Murder Didn’t Call the Police.” The text said thirty-eight. It said they had watched. It said none had called. An unidentified neighbor supplied the line the decade needed: “I didn’t want to get involved.”

Social psychology moved faster than the corrections. and designed emergencies in which a subject heard a seizure, or smoke, with or without other people present. In 1968 they reported, in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, that groups inhibit intervention: , pluralistic ignorance, the fear of doing the wrong thing in public. The 1970 book is called The Unresponsive Bystander. The Genovese name is in the preface of a field. The experiments do not need 38 silent windows. Many textbooks gave them those windows anyway.

Look outward from 1964, not only at one street. The World’s Fair opened in Queens that April. The Civil Rights Act passed in July. American newspapers were already running a story about cities as places where no one is anyone’s neighbor. Gansberg’s piece fitted a ready moral. That is one reason it was hard to unwrite.

People

The woman, the editors, the experimenters, the neighbor

Catherine “Kitty” Genovese

1935–1964

A bartender and manager, a daughter of Brooklyn, a woman who lived with another woman on Austin Street. The photograph the papers circulated for years was a 1961 police picture from a bookmaking arrest. This capsule does not reprint it. She is not a mugshot and not a lesson in apathy. She is the person the story used.

A. M. Rosenthal and Martin Gansberg

The Times, March 1964

Editor and reporter of the piece that turned a Queens homicide into a national character test. Rosenthal’s lunch with the commissioner is in his own later telling. Gansberg’s copy supplied the number and the quote. Other reporters, James Solomon’s later film The Witness records, already doubted the count and did not pick the fight.

John Darley and Bibb Latané

Papers 1968; book 1970

They built a research program out of a newspaper story. The program produced real, replicable effects about groups and helping. The debt to Gansberg is historical. It is not a reason to keep teaching his arithmetic as fact.

Sophia Farrar

1928–2020

The neighbor who went downstairs. The 2016 Times correction is careful and almost nameless (“a 70-year-old woman” — a number later writers have questioned). Farrar’s 2020 obituary is clearer: she held Genovese and said help was coming. The parable needed her not to exist.

After

A discipline keeps a story, then some of it lets go

Moseley was convicted in June 1964 and died in prison in 2016. The story outlived him. Introductory textbooks in the United States and Britain used the as a hook for decades. In 2007, in American Psychologist, , Mark Levine, and Alan Collins went back to the archive and wrote that there was “no evidence for the presence of , or that witnesses observed the murder, or that witnesses remained inactive.” They asked the field to keep the experiments and retire the parable as history.

The Times, in 2016, called its second-day story flawed: the number was too high; no one saw the whole attack; many heard a quarrel and did not hear a murder; two people did call; someone went to the dying woman. In October 2016 an editor’s note was added to the online 1964 article. That is as close as a newspaper comes to walking back a founding myth of a social science.

What remains is not a shrug. Some neighbors did too little. Some classified a woman’s voice at 3 a.m. as private. The emergency number was a maze. The bystander literature is larger than one street and has been tested without Gansberg’s count. The teaching problem is the one this capsule is for: a discipline can be right about a mechanism and still be wrong about the story it uses to make students care.

A question

Leave this on the table

If a field’s most famous example turns out to be a parable, what should it do with the example — drop it, label it, or keep using it because the experiment still works?

Fun fact

Thirty-seven, or thirty-eight

The Times headline of 27 March 1964 counted 37. The story under it counted 38. Reprints have used both. The slip is small and perfect: the number that organized a research program was already unstable on the morning it was printed. What was stable was the moral — a city that would not pick up the phone.
