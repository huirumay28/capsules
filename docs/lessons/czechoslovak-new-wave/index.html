Two of them won Oscars. Then the tanks of August 1968 ended the , and many of its best films were locked away for twenty years.

Before

Before the New Wave: a state industry and socialist realism

Czechoslovakia had a strong film culture long before the 1960s. , on the edge of Prague, opened in the early 1930s and was one of the best-equipped studios in Europe. In 1945, right after the war, the government the entire film industry. In 1946 the state opened , the film faculty of Prague's Academy of Performing Arts. Both decisions turned out to matter a great deal. The directors would be state employees, trained at a state school and paid with state money, which gave them studios, crews and budgets that young directors in Paris could only dream of.

After the Communist Party took power in February 1948, film became a tool of the state. Through the early 1950s, the official style was : clear stories about heroic workers, wicked class enemies and a bright socialist future. These films were rarely popular. Audiences stayed away, and even officials came to see that pure propaganda did not work. The most admired Czech films of the period were often the ones furthest from politics, such as the puppet films of and the fantasy films of , which mixed live action with animation drawn in the style of old engravings.

In 1956 denounced Stalin's crimes in a speech to the Soviet party congress, and a slow thaw began across the Eastern bloc. In Czechoslovakia, where the old leadership under stayed in power, the thaw arrived late and unevenly. By the early 1960s, though, censorship had loosened enough, and powerful figures inside the film industry were sympathetic enough, that a new group of graduates could begin making the films they wanted to make.

The style

What made these films different

The was never one single style. Some directors made gentle comedies of everyday life, some made experiments and some made dark political . What they shared was a refusal to make the kind of film the state had demanded in the 1950s. Instead of heroes and clear messages, they showed ordinary people who were confused, lazy, lustful, petty or kind, and they left the viewer to draw conclusions.

  • Everyday life. and filmed factory dormitories, village dances and family dinners, often with non-professional actors and improvised dialogue, which gave their films the feel of a documentary.
  • Humour, often dark. Comedy let the directors show absurdity and corruption without making a speech about it. A committee that cannot organise a dance says more about a system than a lecture would.
  • Experiment. , and broke up time, used colour filters, collage and dream logic, and borrowed from the strong Czech tradition of .
  • Literature. Many films were adaptations of Czech writers such as , , , and .
  • . Because direct criticism was still dangerous, several of the boldest films told stories about picnics, parties or fire brigades that audiences could read as stories about the state.

The is often compared with the , which began a few years earlier. The differences are useful. The French directors were mostly critics who financed small films privately and often rejected tidy plots. The Czechoslovak directors worked inside a industry with full studio resources, usually told stronger stories, and made far more literary adaptations. Their main opponent was not an old-fashioned commercial cinema but a censor.

Deep dive

Daisies: two Maries against the world

(, 1966) is the 's wildest film. Two young women, both called Marie, decide that because the world is spoiled, they will be spoiled too. They trick older men into buying them dinner, disrupt a nightclub, cut up sausages and bananas with scissors, and finally break into an empty banquet hall, eat everything and swing from the chandelier. Neither lead was a professional actress: was a student and worked as a shop assistant.

, and built the film out of constant changes of colour filter, jump cuts, collage and sound effects, such as the creak of an unoiled hinge when the two women move like dolls in the first scene. said she wanted to hold the audience back from simply enjoying the characters, so that they would think about the idea instead. said the two Maries were a pair of silly young girls 'but they could just as well have been two generals.' Critics today read the film as a satire of consumerism, of a society obsessed with order, and of the roles assigned to women. It opens and closes with wartime footage of bombing, and it ends with a dedication to those who get upset only over a trampled bed of lettuce.

After

Think about it

A question to take with you

The had more money and better studios than most independent filmmakers in the West, but it also had a censor. Did working under censorship push these directors toward humour, and experiment that made their films better, or is that a comfortable story we tell afterwards about artists whose careers were damaged or destroyed?

Fun fact

A silent dance floor

For , wanted to record the natural voices of his mostly non-professional cast, many of them real firemen from the town of . So during dialogue scenes the dance band only pretended to play, and the dancing couples wore wool socks or slippers so that their feet made no noise. The music was added later.
