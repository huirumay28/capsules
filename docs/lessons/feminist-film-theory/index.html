Before

Before 1972: women behind the camera, mostly forgotten

Women were present at the start of cinema. The idea

From images of women to the male gaze

The first wave of feminist film criticism, in the early 1970s, mostly looked at . 's (1973) and 's (1974) traced how Hollywood showed women decade by decade. , a critic in New York, argued that the strong career women played by actresses such as Bette Davis and Katharine Hepburn in the 1930s and 1940s had given way to weaker and more sexualised roles, and that American films of the early 1970s were among the worst for women. This approach asked whether a character was a positive or negative image, a stereotype or a real person.

British critics close to the journal thought this did not go far enough. In 'Women's Cinema as ' (1973), argued that the problem was not just bad characters but the language of mainstream cinema itself. In Hollywood films, she wrote, woman works as a sign, a fixed idea that stands for what men want or fear, rather than as a person. She also suggested that some women directors inside the studio system, especially , had found ways to make that language visible from the inside. and developed this argument in a 1975 BFI book on .

went further. Her essay 'Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema', published in in the autumn of 1975, used ideas from Freud and Lacan to ask why films are pleasurable to watch, and for whom. Its main claims can be put simply:

  • Looking is itself a pleasure. used Freud's term , the pleasure of looking at another person as an object, and argued that cinema is built to provide it, since the audience sits in the dark and watches people who cannot look back.
  • That pleasure is split by gender. In her best-known sentence, 'In a world ordered by sexual imbalance, pleasure in looking has been split between active/male and passive/female.' The man looks and acts; the woman is displayed.
  • Women on carry what she called . They are lit, framed and costumed as spectacle, and the story often stops so the camera can dwell on them, as in a showgirl's number.
  • Cinema combines three looks: the camera's, the characters' and the audience's. Classical films hide the first and last, so the audience identifies with the male hero's look at the woman. This structure is what later writers called .
  • Her examples were the films of with and 's , and , in which the hero watches, follows and investigates a woman.

did not want to improve Hollywood. She wanted to break its pleasure on purpose. The essay says so plainly: 'It is said that analysing pleasure, or beauty, destroys it. That is the intention of this article.' She called for a that would free the camera's look and make the audience aware of how it was watching. With her partner she made films on those lines, most famously (1977), a slow, partly abstract film about a mother in London trying to arrange childcare.

Deep dive

Jeanne Dielman: three days of potatoes, beds and dread

was born in Brussels in 1950 to a Jewish family; her mother had survived Auschwitz. She was 25 when she shot in 1975. The film is about 200 minutes long. plays a widow who lives with her teenage son in a small Brussels flat. Over three days we watch her routine in long, fixed shots, often from a single frontal position, with almost no music: she peels potatoes, makes the bed, washes up, breads veal cutlets, buys buttons, and in the afternoons, while the potatoes boil, has sex with a male client for money. The cinematographer was , and according to the Rotterdam festival about 80 per cent of the crew were women.

The film does not use that described. The camera does not move in to admire , and it does not cut away from the dull parts. The sex scenes happen behind a closed door. What the camera does show in full, in real time, is housework, the labour that most films skip in a single cut. later said the film came from watching her mother: 'It came from what I saw as a kid, all those gestures of my mother. That's why the film is so precise.'

On the second day small things begin to go wrong. Jeanne overcooks the potatoes, drops a spoon, forgets to button her housecoat, and sits idle in a chair. Because the first day was so regular, these small slips feel shocking. On the third day she kills her client with a pair of scissors and then sits at the dining table in silence for several minutes as the film ends. Critics have read the ending in many ways, as a protest against a life without freedom, as a breakdown, or as something the film refuses to explain.

When it opened in Paris in 1976, Le Monde called it the first masterpiece of the feminine in the history of the cinema. For decades it was mainly a film for festivals, universities and cinephiles. In 2022 more than 1,600 critics, scholars and programmers voted it first in 's once-a-decade poll of the greatest films of all time, up from 36th place in 2012. It was the first time in the poll's 70 years that a film directed by a woman came first. 's , one of 's main examples of , came second. The result showed how far the questions of the 1970s had moved from the margins into the centre of film culture, and it also started arguments about whether a slow avant-garde film should top a list most people use to find films to watch.

The argument

Who is the woman in the audience?

's essay was hugely influential and quickly criticised, including by herself. The obvious question was where women in the audience fit. If classical films ask every viewer to identify with the male hero, how do women watch, and why do so many enjoy melodramas and romances? In 1981 published 'Afterthoughts on ', inspired by the western (1946), arguing that women viewers may move back and forth between identifying with the active hero and the passive heroine.

Other writers built a theory of the . The strongest criticism came from Black feminists. In 'The : Black Female Spectators', published in her book (1992), pointed out that psychoanalytic talked about women as if they were all white. Black women, she wrote, had rarely seen themselves on at all, and had learned to watch Hollywood critically, with a gaze that looked back and resisted. A year earlier had released (1991), about three generations of women on a South Carolina sea island in 1902, preparing to move north. It was the first feature directed by an African American woman to receive a general theatrical release in the United States, and it showed in practice what a film made from Black women's point of view could look like.

Popular genres got new readings too. In (1992), studied slasher films and named the , the young woman who survives to the end and fights back. She argued that the mostly young male audiences of these films were asked to identify with her, which complicated the simple idea that men in the audience only look at women from outside. By the early 1990s the field had moved from one big theory to many arguments about race, sexuality, genre and real audiences.
