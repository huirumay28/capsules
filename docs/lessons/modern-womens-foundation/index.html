Before

Before the laws: when police refused to take reports

In 1987, when was established in May, Taiwan was still under , which would not be lifted until 15 July of that year. Women had gained the right to vote decades earlier, but the legal system offered almost no protection against violence inside the home or in intimate relationships. A husband beating a wife was considered a family matter. A woman reporting sexual assault faced police who might refuse to take her statement, doctors who might refuse to treat her, and a legal code that classified rape as an offense against public morality rather than against her person.

The 's English-language history describes what victims faced: 'police did not accept their cases, medical professionals refused to treat them, and there were no other governmental services provided to assist them.' If a man beat a woman on the street and a bystander intervened, he could say 'she is my wife' and the bystander would walk away. The assumption was that she must have done something wrong, or had an affair, to deserve it.

This was the gap set out to fill: not only and law reform, which groups like were already doing, but direct services to women whose safety was in immediate danger and who had nowhere else to turn.

Founding

1987 to 1988: from general rights to urgent protection

(), then a Taipei City councilor, founded in May 1987 with a group of people committed to women's rights. The 's original purpose, according to its own account, was to help women navigate the balance between traditional and modern roles. But as soon as they began working, they discovered that many women could not even think about balancing roles because they were not safe.

In 1988 the opened the (), Taiwan's first center dedicated to protecting women and children from sexual assault, domestic violence and sexual harassment. The center brought together lawyers, crime-prevention experts, doctors and social workers to provide what the state would not: legal advice, accompaniment to medical appointments and police interviews, and other emergency services. It was the first organization in Taiwan to do this work, and it had to invent the model as it went.

's own political career gave the unusual leverage. She served as a legislator from 1993 to 2002 and again from 2005 to 2016, always on the ticket. That let her draft bills, lobby colleagues and push for passage from inside the legislature. The combination of direct service and legislative power is rare. Most organizations do one or the other. did both.

The cases that changed the law

Deng Ru-wen and Peng Wan-ru: two tragedies that made reform urgent

Two events in the 1990s turned the 's quiet case-by-case work into a national legislative campaign.

The first was the 1993 case of (). Deng had been raped by , forced to marry him after she became pregnant, and beaten throughout the marriage. Lin also assaulted Deng's family, including attempting to rape her sister and threatening her siblings. On 27 October 1993, after years of abuse and threats to kill her family, Deng killed Lin while he slept. She immediately called the police and turned herself in.

Deng's trial became a referendum on how Taiwan treated domestic violence. Women's groups, including and , rallied to her defense. Lawyer (), retained through the , argued that Deng had been in a state of mental exhaustion from prolonged abuse. After an initial sentence of five years and six months, the High Court reduced it to three years on 2 August 1994, accepting the argument of diminished capacity. Deng was imprisoned on 19 April 1995 and paroled in March 1996, having served less than a year in prison following about nine months of pretrial custody.

The case exposed the absence of any legal framework for domestic violence. Deng had tried to leave, had reported to police, and had been forced back by Lin's escalating violence. The system had no tools to protect her except after she had already killed her abuser. In 1994, women's groups led by began meeting with then-Justice Minister to discuss drafting a domestic violence law. The Interior Ministry provided one million New Taiwan dollars to for research. Progress was slow.

The second event was the murder of () on 30 November 1996. Peng was the director of the 's Women's Affairs Department and a well-known feminist. She disappeared in Kaohsiung the night before a convention after getting into a taxi. Her body was found three days later outside an abandoned warehouse with more than thirty stab wounds. She had been raped. The murder was never solved.

Peng's death produced nationwide outrage. The convention she had been attending passed her proposal to reserve one-quarter of party seats for women. Women's groups organized a massive march in December 1996. , led by , realized that judge () had already completed a draft domestic violence bill based on United States and New Zealand laws. Pan used her position in the legislature to push the bill forward. It was introduced in July 1996, before Peng's death, but her murder gave it unstoppable momentum.

The laws

Four laws in eight years: the legislative record

co-drafted or drafted four major laws between 1997 and 2005. Each one filled a gap the earlier system had left open.

The was promulgated on 22 January 1997. commissioned (), a lecturer at Taiwan Police College, to draft the bill. It established sexual assault prevention centers in every city and county, required coordination among police, medical, social-service and education agencies, mandated prevention education in schools, set up a national offender registry, and created victim services including accompaniment, counseling and legal aid. Before this law, sexual assault victims had nowhere to go for coordinated help. After it, every local government had to provide a center.

The was promulgated on 24 June 1998 and took effect in 1999. Taiwan became Asia's first country to have a system for domestic violence. The act defined domestic violence broadly, covering physical, psychological and economic abuse between family members, spouses and cohabitants. It allowed courts to issue emergency, temporary and long-term protection orders that could bar an abuser from the home, require them to stay a set distance away, and mandate counseling or treatment. It also created family-violence service centers and a mandatory reporting system. Judge led the drafting committee, which had set up in 1996.

The Criminal Code amendment of 1999, pushed through the legislature by , restructured how Taiwan's legal system understood rape. Before 1999, sexual offenses sat in a chapter called 'offenses against morality' (), which treated rape as a public-decency crime rather than a violation of a person's autonomy. The amendment created a new chapter, 'offenses against sexual autonomy' (), and made clear that forced sex was a crime against the victim's will, not against society's sense of propriety. The reform also changed rape from a complaint-only offense to a public offense and explicitly recognized that a husband could rape his wife, which earlier interpretations had left unclear.

The of 2005 was the last of the four. The Gender Equality in Employment Act and the Gender Equity Education Act already covered harassment at work and in schools, but public spaces had no protection. proposed a bill to close that gap. The was promulgated on 5 February 2005 and took effect one year later, on 5 February 2006, covering harassment in any setting not addressed by the other two laws and setting up complaint and investigation procedures.

After 2005, the continued to push for amendments. In 2015 it worked with a team of lawyers to reform the again, adding protections for child victims in court, establishing expert witnesses for child and disability cases, and banning anyone from sharing information that could identify a victim. In 2021, after years of by many women's groups, Taiwan passed the , which had supported. It took effect in June 2022.

Services

What a protection center does: from hotlines to court social workers

The 's English introduction says it assists more than 8,000 victims of domestic violence, sexual assault and sexual harassment each year, and also helps nearly 3,000 high-conflict families in the courts each year. Those are large numbers, close to Garden of Hope's scale in some areas, and they reflect a service model built around crisis response and legal accompaniment rather than long-term residential care.

The core services include twenty-four-hour hotlines, emergency shelter referrals, hospital and police accompaniment, legal advice, court support and counseling. In 2002 established Taiwan's first inside in Taipei. Social workers stationed at the court help victims file protection orders, understand legal procedures, access welfare resources and find safe housing. The model spread to other courts around Taiwan, and by 2013 the was operating service centers in Taipei, Taichung, Hsinchu and Hualien district courts.

The also runs programs for populations that face extra barriers: intimate-partner violence among LGBTQ couples, which it began addressing in 2009 in partnership with the ; and dating violence among teenagers, which it has worked on since 2008. In 2016 it launched an education campaign aimed at young people and set up a website, , to help teenagers learn about healthy relationships.

In 2017 the opened a sexual-assault () to provide longer-term support after the crisis phase. That same year it expanded services in Hualien and Hsinchu to include children who witness domestic violence, recognizing that those children are also victims even if they are not directly harmed.

This is a different model from Garden of Hope's network of shelters and long-term residential programs, and different again from 's law-reform and hotline work. sits in between: it provides direct crisis services, but the services are built to get people through the legal and medical systems rather than to house them for months. It also continues to draft and lobby for laws, but its main identity is as a , not a policy think tank.

Transparency

How Taiwan's laws require foundations to report

Two laws govern how Taiwan's foundations and charities must disclose their finances and activities: the of 2018 () and the ().

The requires every to submit its annual work plan and budget to the supervising authority within one month of the start of the year, and its work report and financial statements within five months of year-end. Foundations above a certain size must have their financial statements audited by a certified public accountant. Those documents must then be publicly disclosed within one month after they are filed with the authority for the record. Foundations must also disclose a list of donors and recipients from the previous year, including names and amounts, unless a donor or recipient objects in writing in advance, or disclosure would hinder or seriously affect operations, and the competent authority agrees. Disclosure can be by newspaper, website or public inspection. Foundations that fail to comply face fines of NT$30,000 to NT$150,000.

The applies to specific fundraising campaigns rather than to general operations. Any organization that conducts a drive must report the campaign results to the supervising authority within thirty days after the campaign period ends, including a list of donors, total funds raised and an income-and-expenditure report. The organization must also publicly disclose that information. Separately, when the funds are spent, the organization must file a completion report within thirty days after the use plan is completed, showing how the money was used, with outcome data and receipts. The deadline for the completion report can be extended by up to thirty days if needed. The government maintains a public online system where anyone can look up a campaign's permit, see whether reports are overdue, and read past performance.

How a shop could partner transparently

If wants to donate proceeds to , there are several standard models used by Taiwan shops and brands.

  • Percentage of sales: the shop commits to donating a fixed percentage of revenue from all sales, or from a specific product line, during a set period. Example: 'Five percent of every purchase in November 2026 goes to .' This model requires the shop to keep clear sales records and issue a single donation at the end of the period.
  • Lump-sum donation with storytelling: the shop donates a fixed amount up front and tells customers that their purchases supported that donation. Example: 'cool dairy donated NT$50,000 to this quarter. Your support made that possible.' This is simpler to execute and report, and it avoids the complexity of tying individual transactions to charity.
  • Product-specific partnership: the shop sells a co-branded item or a limited-edition piece and donates all or part of the profit. Example: a tote bag designed with the 's input, where the proceeds after costs go to the organization. This works well for items that can carry a message or tell a story.
  • Invoice donation code: Taiwan's electronic invoice system lets customers donate their receipt's lottery chance to a registered charity by entering a 'love code' (). The shop can print the 's code on receipts or packaging. The 's website would list its code, or can ask for it. This is a zero-cost way to let customers give directly without requiring the shop to collect and forward money.

Whichever model chooses, transparency means documenting the partnership in writing, keeping receipts, getting a from the , and publishing the total donated and when. Under the , if the shop's campaign counts as —meaning it publicly solicits money tied to a cause— would need to apply for a permit and file reports. However, only public schools, administrative legal persons, public-interest associations, and foundations may obtain fundraising permits under the Act; a for-profit shop cannot. If wants to publicly solicit donations, it must do so under 's own permitted campaign. If the shop is simply donating its own revenue and telling customers about it afterward, that is not and requires no permit. The distinction matters. Before launching, should confirm with the and, if needed, a lawyer which model applies.

, like other established organizations, likely has standard partnership agreements and knows which models require permits. The first step is to ask. The second step is to be clear with customers: tell them exactly how much will be donated, when, to whom, and for what. The third step is to follow through and report the outcome.

Comparison

Modern Women's Foundation, Garden of Hope and Awakening: three models

The three organizations covered so far in this series—, and —represent three different approaches to women's rights work, and understanding the differences helps clarify what a donation supports.

is mainly an and watchdog organization. Its total income in 2025 was about NT$5.7 million. It has six or seven paid staff. Its products are draft bills, policy reports, lobby campaigns, volunteer trainings and public pressure. It does run a family-law advice hotline, but most of its work is aimed at changing systems rather than helping individuals through them.

is mainly a service provider, and a large one. Its total income in 2025 was about NT$640.7 million. It runs shelters, halfway houses, employment programs and counseling centers across Taiwan, many under government contracts. It also advocates for laws and policies, but its identity is as an organization that operates facilities and delivers long-term case management. Its scale is more than a hundred times Awakening's.

is both: it provides crisis services to thousands of victims each year, and it drafts laws. Its scale is closer to Garden of Hope's in service numbers, though its income is likely smaller because its services are shorter-term and often embedded in courts and hospitals rather than in residential centers it owns. It also retains the legislative access that brought when she founded it, even though she is no longer in the legislature. This dual identity—service provider and lawmaker—is unusual.

For a shop like , the choice among them is not about which is better but about what story the shop wants to tell. Supporting Awakening means supporting the people who write the laws. Supporting Garden of Hope means supporting shelters and long-term care. Supporting means supporting the organizations that stand between a victim and the police station, the hospital and the courthouse, at the moment of crisis, and that also built the legal tools those institutions now use.

Fun fact

The foundation's two names and the separated histories

Until around 2015, published combined financial reports with a separate Taipei-based called 台北市. The two shared an office and worked closely together, but they were separate legal entities with separate budgets. The national , , handled nationwide and services; the Taipei focused on local Taipei programs. This structure let the organizations divide labor and access different funding sources. Since 2015, the reports published on the website cover only the national 's work, though the earlier combined reports are still available in the archive. This is the kind of organizational detail that sounds boring but matters a great deal when reading financial statements or signing a partnership: which entity are you actually dealing with?
