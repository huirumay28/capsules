Before

Before the laws: when police refused to take reports

In 1987, when was established in May, Taiwan was still under , which would not be lifted until 15 July of that year. Women had gained the right to vote decades earlier, but the legal system offered almost no protection against violence inside the home or in intimate relationships. A husband beating a wife was considered a family matter. A woman reporting sexual assault faced police who might refuse to take her statement, doctors who might refuse to treat her, and a legal code that classified rape as an offense against public morality rather than against her person.

The 's English-language history describes what victims faced: 'police did not accept their cases, medical professionals refused to treat them, and there were no other governmental services provided to assist them.' The Chinese version of the same history adds that social-welfare offices offered no help either, so victims who had nowhere else to go turned to the . A later Taipei Times retrospective, citing a report by the of Women's Rights Promotion and Development, describes the same period: a man beating a woman on the street could justify it to bystanders by saying 'she is my wife', and people assumed she must have done something wrong to deserve it.

This was the gap set out to fill: not only and law reform, which groups like were already doing, but direct services to women whose safety was in immediate danger and who had nowhere else to turn.

Founding

1987 to 1988: from general rights to urgent protection

(), then a member of the Taipei City Council (she served there from 25 December 1981 to 31 January 1993), founded in 1987 with a group of people committed to women's rights. The 's original purpose, according to its own account, was to help women navigate the balance between traditional and modern roles. But as soon as they began working, they discovered that many women could not even think about balancing roles because they were not safe.

In 1988 the opened the (), Taiwan's first center dedicated to protecting women and children from sexual assault, domestic violence and sexual harassment. The center brought together lawyers, crime-prevention experts, doctors and social workers to provide what the state would not: legal advice, accompaniment to medical appointments and police interviews, and other emergency services. It was the first organization in Taiwan to do this work, and it had to invent the model as it went.

's own political career gave the unusual leverage. She served as a legislator from 1993 to 2002 and again from 2005 to 2016 for the . That let her draft bills, lobby colleagues and push for passage from inside the legislature. The combination of direct service and legislative power is rare. Most organizations do one or the other. did both.

The cases that changed the law

Deng Ru-wen and Peng Wan-ru: two tragedies that made reform urgent

Two events in the 1990s turned the 's quiet case-by-case work into a national legislative campaign.

The first was the 1993 case of (). , a man twenty-two years older with a prior conviction for taking part in a killing, raped Deng when she was a teenager. She became pregnant, gave birth and was pushed into living with him, and he beat her for years. When she ran home, he smashed her parents' belongings, tied up her father and threatened her family. On the night of 27 October 1993, after he came home drunk and threatened to kill her family again, Deng killed Lin after he had gone to sleep. She then turned herself in.

Deng's trial became a referendum on how Taiwan treated domestic violence. Women's groups, including , rallied to her defense. Her defence, which included lawyer (), argued that prolonged abuse had left her in a state of mental exhaustion. The Banqiao District Court sentenced her to five years and six months. In August 1994 the Taiwan High Court, citing her voluntary surrender and her impaired mental state, reduced the sentence to three years. She was paroled in March 1996, after less than a year in prison.

The case exposed the absence of any legal framework for domestic violence. Deng had tried to leave, had reported to police, and had been forced back by Lin's escalating violence. The system had no tools to protect her except after she had already killed her abuser. In February 1994, the day before Deng's first verdict, and other women's groups visited Justice Minister to ask for a law against marital violence. He promised training for judicial staff and administrative rules, but not yet a law. According to the Taipei Times, citing 's study of how the act was made, the Ministry of the Interior then gave NT$1 million for research on a draft, but the effort stalled after the first report for lack of staff and further funding.

The second event was the murder of () on 30 November 1996. Peng was the director of the 's Women's Affairs Department and a well-known feminist. She disappeared in Kaohsiung the night before a convention after getting into a taxi. Her body was later found outside an abandoned warehouse in Kaohsiung County with more than thirty stab wounds. She had been raped. The murder was never solved.

Peng's death produced nationwide outrage. Ironically, the convention she had been attending passed her proposal to reserve one-quarter of party seats for women on the day she is believed to have been killed. Women's groups organized a massive march in December 1996. The domestic violence effort restarted when learned that judge () had already written a draft based on laws in the United States and New Zealand, covering family violence in general rather than only violence between spouses. That bill went to the legislature in July 1996, months before Peng's death. According to the , Peng's murder pushed the Executive Yuan to take women's personal safety seriously and sped up passage of the sexual assault bill the had already commissioned. The 's own account says it set up a committee in 1996 to build a domestic violence law suited to Taiwan, with Kao as drafter and with experts, NGOs, government agencies and survivors involved, and that Pan then worked with legislators from several parties to pass it. On 21 December 1996 women marched at night in Taipei under the slogan 'Women want rights, not violence' (). The passed soon after, and the domestic violence law followed about eighteen months later.

The laws

Four laws in eight years: the legislative record

co-drafted or drafted four major laws between 1997 and 2005. Each one filled a gap the earlier system had left open.

The passed in January 1997. The commissioned () to draft the bill, and it became the first law pushed through jointly by women legislators of the , and New Party, including , Yeh Chu-lan and Chu Hui-liang. The calls it Taiwan's first law to protect the personal safety of women and children. It established sexual assault prevention centers in every city and county, required coordination among police, medical, social-service and education agencies, mandated prevention education in schools, set up a national offender registry, and created victim services including accompaniment, counseling and legal aid. Before this law, sexual assault victims had nowhere to go for coordinated help. After it, every local government had to provide a center.

The passed on 24 June 1998 and took effect in 1999. Taiwan became Asia's first country to have a system for domestic violence. The act defined domestic violence broadly, covering physical, psychological and economic abuse between family members, spouses and cohabitants. It allowed courts to issue emergency, temporary and long-term protection orders that could bar an abuser from the home, require them to stay a set distance away, and mandate counseling or treatment. It also created family-violence service centers and a mandatory reporting system. Judge drafted it for the committee set up in 1996.

The Criminal Code amendment of 1999, pushed through the legislature by , restructured how Taiwan's legal system understood rape. Before 1999, sexual offenses sat in a chapter called 'offenses against morality' (), which treated rape as a public-decency crime rather than a violation of a person's autonomy. The amendment created a new chapter, 'offenses against sexual autonomy' (), and made clear that forced sex was a crime against the victim's will, not against society's sense of propriety. The reform also changed rape from a complaint-only offense to a public offense and explicitly recognized that a husband could rape his wife, which earlier interpretations had left unclear.

The of 2005 was the last of the four. The Gender Equality in Employment Act and the Gender Equity Education Act already covered harassment at work and in schools, but public spaces had no protection. proposed a bill to close that gap. The passed in early 2005 and took effect in February 2006, covering harassment in any setting not addressed by the other two laws and setting up complaint and investigation procedures.

After 2005, the continued to push for amendments. In 2015 it worked with a team of lawyers to reform the again, adding protections for child victims in court, establishing expert witnesses for child and disability cases, and banning anyone from sharing information that could identify a victim. The began pushing for a stalking law in 2013, convened a working group that finished a draft in 2015, and adapted Dr. TK Logan's stalking risk-assessment tool () for Taiwan. After a high-profile case in 2021, the act passed its third reading in November 2021 and took effect on 1 June 2022.

Services

What a protection center does: from hotlines to court social workers

The 's English introduction says it assists more than 8,000 victims of domestic violence, sexual assault and sexual harassment each year, and also helps nearly 3,000 high-conflict families in the courts each year. Those are large numbers, close to Garden of Hope's scale in some areas, and they reflect a service model built around crisis response and legal accompaniment rather than long-term residential care.

The core services include twenty-four-hour hotlines, emergency shelter referrals, hospital and police accompaniment, legal advice, court support and counseling. In 2002 established Taiwan's first inside in Taipei. Social workers stationed at the court help victims file protection orders, understand legal procedures, access welfare resources and find safe housing. The notes that the model is now used nationwide. From 2013 it also began running family-case service centers (家事服務中心) in district courts such as Taichung and Taipei, for parties in divorce and custody cases.

The also runs programs for populations that face extra barriers: intimate-partner violence among LGBTQ couples, which it began addressing in 2009 in partnership with the ; and dating violence among teenagers, which it has worked on since 2008 with more than sixty school talks a year. In 2016 it launched the 'Love Is Respect' programme and set up , which it describes as Taiwan's first website for helping young people practise healthy relationships.

In 2017 the opened a sexual-assault () to provide longer-term support after the crisis phase. That same year it expanded services in Hualien and Hsinchu to include children who witness domestic violence, recognizing that those children are also victims even if they are not directly harmed.

This is a different model from Garden of Hope's network of shelters and long-term residential programs, and different again from 's law-reform and hotline work. sits in between: it provides direct crisis services, but the services are built to get people through the legal and medical systems rather than to house them for months. It also continues to draft and lobby for laws, but its main identity is as a , not a policy think tank.

Transparency

How Taiwan's laws require foundations to report

Two laws govern how Taiwan's foundations and charities must disclose their finances and activities: the of 2018 () and the ().

The requires every to submit its annual work plan and budget to the supervising authority within one month of the start of the year, and its work report and financial statements within five months of year-end. Foundations above a certain size must have their financial statements audited by a certified public accountant. Those documents must then be publicly disclosed within one month after they are filed with the authority for the record. Foundations must also disclose a list of donors and recipients from the previous year, including names and amounts, unless a donor or recipient objects in writing in advance, or disclosure would hinder or seriously affect operations, and the competent authority agrees. Disclosure can be by newspaper, website or public inspection. Under Article 29, directors or supervisors who falsify records or fail to file the required reports can be fined NT$30,000 to NT$150,000.

The applies to specific fundraising campaigns rather than to general operations. Any organization that conducts a drive must report the campaign results to the supervising authority within thirty days after the campaign period ends, including a list of donors, total funds raised and an income-and-expenditure report. The organization must also publicly disclose that information. Separately, when the funds are spent, the organization must file a completion report within thirty days after the use plan is completed, showing how the money was used, with outcome data and receipts. The deadline for the completion report can be extended by up to thirty days if needed. Article 20 also requires these reports to be posted on the supervising authority's website, so anyone can look up a permitted campaign and see how its money was used. Article 18 adds that any spending of NT$10,000 or more from campaign funds must be paid by cheque or bank or postal transfer, not in cash.

How a shop could partner transparently

If wants to donate proceeds to , there are several standard models used by Taiwan shops and brands.

The already has public channels for this kind of cooperation. Its website has a corporate participation page (企業參與), a 'you shop, they donate' page (你購物他捐款) listing brands that give part of their sales, and a charity merchandise page (義賣商品). Reading those pages first will show what formats the already accepts and how it credits partners.

  • Percentage of sales: the shop commits to donating a fixed percentage of revenue from all sales, or from a specific product line, during a set period. Example: 'Five percent of every purchase in November 2026 goes to .' This model requires the shop to keep clear sales records and issue a single donation at the end of the period.
  • Lump-sum donation with storytelling: the shop donates a fixed amount up front and tells customers that their purchases supported that donation. Example: 'cool dairy donated NT$50,000 to this quarter. Your support made that possible.' This is simpler to execute and report, and it avoids the complexity of tying individual transactions to charity.
  • Product-specific partnership: the shop sells a co-branded item or a limited-edition piece and donates all or part of the profit. Example: a tote bag designed with the 's input, where the proceeds after costs go to the organization. This works well for items that can carry a message or tell a story.
  • Invoice donation code: Taiwan's electronic invoice system lets customers donate their receipt's lottery chance to a registered charity by entering a 'love code' (). The shop can print the 's code on receipts or packaging. The 's website would list its code, or can ask for it. This is a zero-cost way to let customers give directly without requiring the shop to collect and forward money.

Whichever model chooses, transparency means documenting the partnership in writing, keeping receipts, getting a from the , and publishing the total donated and when. Under the , if the shop's campaign counts as —meaning it publicly solicits money tied to a cause— would need to apply for a permit and file reports. However, only public schools, administrative legal persons, public-interest associations, and foundations may obtain fundraising permits under the Act; a for-profit shop cannot. If wants to publicly solicit donations, it must do so under 's own permitted campaign. If the shop is simply donating its own revenue and telling customers about it afterward, that is not and requires no permit. The distinction matters. Before launching, should confirm with the and, if needed, a lawyer which model applies.

, like other established organizations, likely has standard partnership agreements and knows which models require permits. The first step is to ask. The second step is to be clear with customers: tell them exactly how much will be donated, when, to whom, and for what. The third step is to follow through and report the outcome.

Comparison

Modern Women's Foundation, Garden of Hope and Awakening: three models

The three organizations covered so far in this series—, and —represent three different approaches to women's rights work, and understanding the differences helps clarify what a donation supports.

There is also a comparison beyond Taiwan. According to the Taipei Times, judge , who drafted the bill for 's committee, went on to help Japan draft its own domestic violence law, which Japan adopted in 2001. Taiwan's 1998 act was therefore not only Asia's first; it also became a reference point for a neighbour.

Its products are draft bills, policy reports, lobby campaigns, volunteer trainings and public pressure. It does run a family-law advice hotline, but most of its work is aimed at changing systems rather than helping individuals through them.

is mainly a service provider, and a large one. Its total income in 2025 was about NT$640.7 million. It runs shelters, halfway houses, employment programs and counseling centers across Taiwan, many under government contracts. It also advocates for laws and policies, but its identity is as an organization that operates facilities and delivers long-term case management. Its scale is more than a hundred times Awakening's.

is both: it provides crisis services to thousands of victims each year, and it drafts laws. Its own figures, more than 8,000 victims and nearly 3,000 court families a year, show a large service operation. I have not compared its income with Garden of Hope's, so check the audited reports before drawing conclusions about size. It also retains the legislative access that brought when she founded it, even though she is no longer in the legislature. This dual identity—service provider and lawmaker—is unusual.

For a shop like , the choice among them is not about which is better but about what story the shop wants to tell. Supporting Awakening means supporting the people who write the laws. Supporting Garden of Hope means supporting shelters and long-term care. Supporting means supporting the organizations that stand between a victim and the police station, the hospital and the courthouse, at the moment of crisis, and that also built the legal tools those institutions now use.

Question

A question to take with you
