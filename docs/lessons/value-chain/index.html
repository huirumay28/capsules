Before

Strategy without a place to look

By the early 1980s, managers already had a language for rivalry. Porter's own earlier book, Competitive Strategy (1980), had given them the : the pressures from rivals, new entrants, substitutes, suppliers and buyers. That framework answers a market question: how hard is it to make a good return in this industry? It does not answer an internal question: which of our own steps create the advantage, and which just add cost?

Companies often answered that second question with vague strengths. They said they were good at 'service', 'quality' or 'innovation'. Those words are too broad to manage. A customer does not pay for a department name. A customer pays for a sequence of concrete steps: the way materials arrive, the way a product is made or assembled, the way it is shipped, sold and supported afterward. Until those steps are named, a strategy debate stays abstract.

Porter's answer in 1985 was to take the firm apart. introduced : a map of the activities a company performs to design, produce, sell, deliver and support what it offers. Advantage, he argued, comes from performing particular activities more cheaply than rivals, or in ways that create more buyer value, or from managing the links between activities better. Harvard Business School's Institute for Strategy and Competitiveness still presents the tool that way: strategy is the set of choices about how activities are configured and linked.

The framework

Nine boxes, and the links between them

Porter splits activities into two layers. sit on the bottom of the classic diagram and touch the product or directly. sit above them and improve the primary ones across the firm.

  • : receiving, storing and handling inputs. For a manufacturer that means parts and raw materials; for a shop it can mean how inventory arrives and is put away.
  • : the steps that turn inputs into the finished offer, from factory lines to a restaurant kitchen to the way a software team builds a release.
  • : getting the finished offer to the buyer, including warehousing, order fulfilment and delivery.
  • : the activities that make buyers aware of the offer and complete the sale, from pricing and advertising to a sales force or a website checkout.
  • : what happens after the sale to keep or raise the product's value, such as installation, repairs, returns, training or customer support.

Strategic fit

Why copying one step is not enough

In 'What Is Strategy?' (1996), Porter drew a sharp line between and strategy. means performing similar activities better than rivals: faster machines, leaner processes, fewer defects. Those gains matter, and they are easy for others to copy. Strategy, he wrote, means choosing a different set of activities to deliver a unique mix of value. The test is not whether you are busy improving. The test is whether your activity choices one another so that a rival who copies only one or two of them still fails.

is his classic low-cost example. Short-haul routes, frequent departures, a standardised fleet of Boeing 737s, fast gate turns, no meals, no assigned seats and no travel-agent commissions were not separate tips. They reinforced one another. Fast turns needed a simple fleet and a simple boarding process. A simple fleet made maintenance and crew training cheaper. Skipping meals and seat assignments made those fast turns possible. A rival that copied 's fares without copying the whole system would not get the same cost structure.

works the same way on the retail side. Magretta lists activity choices that look odd one by one and powerful together: company-designed products, outsourced manufacturing under central control, huge suburban stores with parking, a warehouse attached to the showroom, room displays and large hang-tags instead of floor salespeople, that customers take home and assemble, plus in-store cafeterias and childcare that make long visits workable. cut shipping cost and damage. Suburban car access makes self-delivery realistic. Room displays and hang-tags make low staffing workable. Each choice raises the value of the others. That is what Porter means by .

After

From diagram to habit, and the usual mistakes

After 1985 the value-chain diagram became classroom wallpaper. That popularity created a shallow version of the tool. Teams redraw the nine boxes, drop their department names into them, and stop. Porter's point was never the drawing. It was the search for activities that change price or cost, for that amplify those activities, and for a configuration rivals cannot copy piecemeal.

Later strategy work kept returning to activities even when it used other labels. , covered in an earlier lesson, asks which capabilities underlie many products. Seven Powers asks which structural advantages protect profits. Playing to Win asks where to play and how to win. is the more microscopic map underneath those questions: if a competence or a power is real, it should show up as particular activities done differently, with particular links.

Two mistakes are common. The first is treating the chain as a cost-cutting checklist only. Differentiation also lives in activities: a luxury brand's , packaging and store design are activities that support a higher price. The second is copying a celebrated step in isolation, such as without suburban car access, or a direct website without the inventory and supplier links that make direct selling cheap. is the part that travels badly between firms.
