The (): from rescuing trafficked girls to the 's fight and

The , usually shortened to , was created in 1987 to rescue teenage girls who had been sold into prostitution. Five years later it became the organization that found, recorded and stood beside Taiwan's surviving 'comfort women', the women forced into sexual slavery by the Japanese military during the Second World War. It took nine of them to court in Tokyo, built a museum in their name, and, after the last known survivor died in 2023, turned much of its energy to a newer harm: the non-consensual sharing of sexual images online. This lesson covers its history, its services, how it is funded according to its own audited accounts, and what it would mean for to choose it as a partner.

Before

Before 1987: girls sold into brothels, and a society that looked away

In the mid-1980s Taiwan was still under martial law, which ended in July 1987. The economy was growing fast, but poverty in rural and Indigenous communities remained severe, and some families sold or pledged their daughters to brokers. Many of these girls were in their early teens. They were moved into brothels in places such as Taipei's , an old red-light district in Wanhua, and were held there by debt and violence.

Women's groups of the time, especially the , had already started to talk about women's rights in law and in the family, and church groups such as the Presbyterian Church's were working with Indigenous girls. What was missing was an organization whose main job was to get girls out and to change the laws that let the trade continue. The was created to fill that gap. Founding

1987 to 1988: an association of lawyers and social workers

The association was founded in August 1987. Chinese Wikipedia names , , and among the people who started it, alongside the , the and the . Several of the founders were lawyers. This matters for understanding the organization: from the start, it combined direct rescue with legal advocacy.

In 1987 and again on 9 January 1988, the association, the and the Rainbow women's ministry led marches through , known as the 'march to rescue child prostitutes' (). Chinese Wikipedia describes these marches as a turning point for how Taiwan's media covered the women's movement: newspapers began to report on women's groups seriously and sympathetically. Working with legislators and other groups, the association then pushed for laws to stop the trade, including the youth welfare law and the 1995 (), which was later rewritten as the .

In September 1988 the association applied to become a foundation. Its full legal name today is . In English it calls itself the , and it is often abbreviated as . Its stated mission is to care for women and children who suffer gender-based violence or are overlooked, to push for social reform through advocacy, and to help them become independent through services.

The comfort women

1992 onward: finding the women history had erased

During the Second World War, the Japanese military set up 'comfort stations' across Asia and forced women and girls into sexual slavery there. Most historians estimate the total at somewhere between 20,000 and 200,000 women, mainly from Korea but also from China, the Philippines, Indonesia, the Netherlands, Taiwan and elsewhere. Taiwan was a Japanese colony from 1895 to 1945, and activists estimate that about 2,000 Taiwanese women were among the victims.

For almost fifty years, the subject was silent. That changed on 14 August 1991, when the Korean survivor spoke publicly about what had been done to her. In February 1992 the Japanese Diet member published three wartime telegrams found in a Japanese defence archive that proved Taiwanese women had also been sent to military brothels. The Women's Rescue Foundation opened a complaint hotline almost immediately. According to a 1995 report on the foundation's work, the first survivor called within days. In March 1992 the government formed a cross-ministry task force led by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, and the foundation was made responsible for visiting, verifying and registering survivors.

This arrangement was unusual. The , a Japanese body, later noted that in Taiwan a single private foundation was given three jobs at once: identifying survivors, holding their personal information and passing on the government's living-expense subsidies to them. In other countries these tasks were usually split among different groups. Through the hotline, interviews and document checks, the foundation eventually confirmed 59 Taiwanese survivors. Some were Han Taiwanese and some were Indigenous women; four of the nine plaintiffs in the later lawsuit were Indigenous.

The life of , known as , shows what these women lived through. Born in Taipei in 1922, she was sold into a wine house as a teenager and, in 1942, was taken to a in the Andaman Islands after being told she would work as a nurse's aide. She tried to kill herself three times. When she came home after the war, an uncle threw her suitcase out of the house because of what she had been. She spent her last working years selling coconut water in a market in Pingtung and died in January 2016, aged 94.

The foundation's work with survivors went beyond paperwork. From 1996 it ran body-and-mind workshops for the grandmothers, using drama and art, for about sixteen years. It organized events in which the women wore wedding dresses they had never been able to wear and visited the Presidential Office. It made two documentaries: (), which won Best Documentary at the 1998 , and (, 2015), which followed six survivors through the healing workshops and was released with money raised on the crowdfunding platform . The film's title comes from Isaiah: 'A bruised reed he will not break.'

The lawsuit

1999 to 2005: nine grandmothers in a Tokyo courtroom

The foundation always demanded that the Japanese state, not a charity, apologize and pay compensation. In 1995 Japan created the , which offered survivors money raised partly from private donations. The foundation and other groups in Korea and Taiwan rejected it, arguing that it let the government avoid legal responsibility. The itself recorded that the foundation's stance 'had considerable impact on the victims'.

In 1999 the foundation led nine Taiwanese survivors to file a lawsuit in the Tokyo District Court asking for a formal apology and compensation. Their lawyers included , the foundation's chair in the 1990s, who later served as Taiwan's Minister of Justice. The court dismissed the claims on 15 October 2002, saying among other things that individuals could not bring claims under international law and that the time limit had passed. The Tokyo High Court upheld the dismissal on 9 February 2004, and the Supreme Court rejected the final appeal on 25 February 2005. Two of the nine women had died while the case was in progress. Lawsuits by survivors from Korea and the Philippines also lost in the Japanese courts.

Losing in court did not end the campaign. The foundation continued to call on Japan to apologize, pressed Taiwan's school curriculum and the national history academy to record the history accurately, and turned to public memory: a museum.

The museum

The Ama Museum: memory that costs money to keep

The foundation began planning a museum in 2004. For more than ten years it could not find a building; possible sites fell through over property rights and urban renewal. In 2015 a landlord offered a reduced rent on a two-storey shophouse at 一段256號 in , near the primary school had attended. The museum, named (, 'Grandma's House'), opened on 10 December 2016, Human Rights Day, and opened fully on 8 March 2017. Admission was free, and the foundation relied on visitors' donations. In its first three months, more than ten thousand people visited, many of them groups from Japan.

The foundation's executive director at the time, , said the museum was 'not about remembering hatred, but about knowing how to move forward'. Its texts answered people who claimed the women had volunteered: the exhibits state that the women were military sexual slaves and victims of state violence, and that under a system run by the army, police and merchants, nobody truly volunteered.

The museum lost close to NT$5 million a year. English Wikipedia reports that the foundation sold its own offices in 2019 to keep it open. When COVID-19 cut visitor numbers further, the first museum closed on 10 November 2020. A second, smaller museum opened on 26 November 2021 on the fifth floor of an office building at 承德路三段32號, near Minquan West Road station, which is now also the foundation's headquarters. In 2021 Taipei registered the old shophouse as a historic building. The foundation reported 3,280 visits to the museum in 2024.

On 10 May 2023 the last Taiwanese comfort woman known to the foundation died at the age of 92. She had not wanted public attention, so the foundation announced her death only after her private funeral. It said it would keep pressing for the history to be taught and for Japan to apologize to the survivors' families.

Today

What the foundation does now

The work is now a matter of memory and education, so most of the foundation's daily work is direct service to living victims of gender-based violence. Its own 2024 figures, posted on the 17885 donation platform, list these services:

  • Services for children and teenagers who witness domestic violence: 8,686 instances.
  • Services for adults who witnessed domestic violence as children: 763 instances.
  • Services for victims of : 1,991 instances.
  • Services for victims of sexual harassment and sexual assault: 76 instances.
  • Education, professional training and talks: 248 sessions.
  • visits: 3,280.

Note that these are (), not individual people: one person who receives ten counselling sessions counts ten times. This is normal in Taiwanese social-work reporting, but it means the numbers cannot be compared directly with a count of people helped.

Children who witness violence at home are a specialty. Many organizations serve the adult victim; the foundation has built programmes for the children in the same household, and even for adults still affected by what they saw as children.

From 2011 it collected news reports about leaked intimate images, and in 2015 it opened a helpline and website with the message that a leaked nude photo is not the victim's fault. In 2017 it convened a working group that drafted a bill against the non-consensual sharing of sexual images. After South Korea's , in which men used chat rooms to blackmail and exploit women and girls, the foundation began using the term 'digital sexual violence' (). In January 2023 the Legislative Yuan passed amendments to four laws, including the Criminal Code and the , that create crimes of filming or spreading sexual images without consent and making sexual images, and that require online platforms to restrict or remove such images. The government reports that the main amendments took effect in February 2023. The foundation says it now monitors how these laws are applied, and in 2024 it took part in a further amendment of the .

Money

How the foundation is funded: reading its 2025 accounts

The foundation posts its audited financial statements and its fundraising reports on its annual reports page. The 2025 (民國114年) statements were reviewed by an accountant at 安鈺加會計師事務所, whose certificate is dated 19 March 2026. The statement of revenue and expenses shows the following for 2025:

  • Total income: NT$36,513,266. Of this, contracted government projects () brought NT$16,609,311, or 45.5 percent; donations brought NT$13,597,910, or 37.2 percent; government subsidies brought NT$5,345,543, or 14.6 percent; and the brought NT$393,571, or 1.1 percent.
  • Total spending: NT$33,672,750. Programme spending (業務支出) was NT$29,839,303, or 81.7 percent of income, and administration was NT$3,756,990, or 10.3 percent.
  • Surplus: NT$2,840,516, compared with NT$1,406,357 in 2024.
  • Net assets at the end of 2025: NT$42,340,303, including a of NT$10,000,000.

The size is the first thing to notice. The , covered three weeks ago, reported income of about NT$640.7 million for 2025, roughly eighteen times as much. The Women's Rescue Foundation is a small organization with a large history. A partnership with a small shop would be more visible to it, and a few thousand dollars would matter more, than the same gift to a very large charity.

The second thing to notice is dependence on government money. Contracted projects and subsidies together made up about 60 percent of 2025 income. This is common for Taiwanese social-welfare foundations, which deliver many protection services under contract to city governments or the Ministry of Health and Welfare. It gives stable funding, but it also means that the work that is not contracted, such as the museum, history education and new advocacy, depends heavily on private donations.

The fundraising report shows how this works under the (). The foundation's 2025 campaign, 攜手邁步 婦援守護, was approved by the Ministry of Health and Welfare (permit 衛部救字第1131364824號) to run from 1 February 2025 to 31 January 2026. It aimed to raise NT$6.5 million and raised NT$4,376,948, about 67 percent of its target. The money was spent on case services and advocacy (NT$638,776), professional staff costs (NT$1,999,818), and the 's operation and education programmes (NT$1,287,089). Fundraising costs were NT$451,265. The law allows fundraising costs of up to 15 percent when a campaign raises less than NT$10 million, which here would be NT$656,542, so the foundation stayed under the limit. It also publishes monthly donor lists (捐款芳名錄) on its website, as the law requires.

For , the useful lesson is that this one-page report is exactly the kind of document a partner should be able to show: the permit number, the target, the actual amount, where each part went, and the cost of raising it.

Elsewhere

The same fight in Korea, Japan and the Philippines

Taiwan's movement was part of a regional one. In South Korea, the was founded in 1990, the year before testified, and it organized the weekly outside the Japanese embassy in Seoul. In the Philippines, survivors organized through groups such as , and Filipino survivors sued the Japanese government in Tokyo in 1993; the Supreme Court rejected their case in December 2003. Inside Japan, a network of feminist historians and activists supported the survivors, and many of the 's early visitors were Japanese students and teachers who said they could not learn this history at home.

The movements differed in their politics. Korea's movement became a powerful national cause, closely tied to relations with Japan. In Taiwan the issue never became as central: Taiwan's relationship with Japan is warmer, and its politics are divided over how to remember the colonial period. The foundation has had to keep the subject alive with far fewer survivors and less public attention. It has done so partly by framing the history as one about gender violence in general rather than only about Japan, which is also why it moved naturally into : in both cases, the foundation argues, women are harmed and then blamed and shamed into silence.

Fun fact

Fifty-nine lamps and two thousand reeds

The first on had a 'Song of the Reed corridor'. It held fifty-nine metal lamps, one for each of the 59 confirmed Taiwanese survivors, and two thousand clear tubes standing like reeds for the roughly two thousand women whose names will never be known. When a visitor held a palm under one of the lamps, a survivor's name was projected onto the hand in light.
